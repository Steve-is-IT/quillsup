READ-ALOUDS AND GRAPHIC STORIES
Four illustrated read-alouds for K-2, four for grades 3-5, and three graphic-story scripts for grades 6-8

Trust your hackles. Pause · Check · Tell

Hackles · a free kit · quillsup.org

The Hackles name, logos, characters and artwork are not covered by that license: print and share them unaltered for classroom and family use; any other use needs written permission. No student accounts, no student data collected, ever.

Teacher Quick Start (one page, print and keep)#

What it is: 11 short stories. Four read-alouds for K-2, four for grades 3-5 (with Hackle and the Crew), and three graphic-story scripts for grades 6-8 (no mascots in dialogue). Each takes about 5 minutes to read and 10 to 12 minutes with talk.

You need: the story page, the "Talk about it" questions, a "join in" signal poster (Hackles Up, Pause Palm, Check Eyes, Tell Wave), a Trusted Hand poster, drawing paper. Nothing else.

Teacher Quick Start (one page, print and keep)
Step K-5 read-aloud (10-12 min) 6-8 graphic story (10-12 min)
1 Picture walk (1 min): show the cover; "What do you think will happen?" Set up (1 min): project panels or hand out copies. Say the two promises.
2 Read (4-6 min): use voices; stop at every [Join in!] line. Read (4-6 min): volunteers read captions and speech; you narrate. Stop at each Pause Point.
3 Talk (3 min): pick 3 or 4 questions; accept words, points, thumbs. Talk (3 min): the Pause Point questions.
4 Hackles chant (1 min): Hackles up! Pause. Check. Tell. Skill panel (1 min): keep the last panel on screen; students write their own three-sentence Tell on a sticky.
5 Draw it / Move it (optional). Optional: "Draw the missing panel."

Say the two promises before every story: A false alarm is fine. It is never your fault.

If...

  • A child gets upset or goes quiet: keep reading at a calm pace, offer a Pass, check in privately afterward. Do not push anyone to share a real experience.
  • A child shares a real story: thank them, follow your disclosure box, do not promise secrecy.
  • A character shares a name with a child in your class: swap the name (every name here is pretend).
  • You cannot read the voices: read plainly; the stories still work.

Never: ask a child to act out the stranger or the sneaky message; ask students to write real passwords or accounts.

How to use these stories#

Stories are the gentlest way to practice a hard skill. A child who hears "Maya felt her hackles go up, and she paused, and she told her dad" has already rehearsed the first half of the skill without anyone putting her on the spot.

Each read-aloud has: a title, the skill it teaches, the lesson it matches, the full text with Illustration note page notes (so an illustrator or the teacher can draw or point to a picture), call-and-response moments for children to join in, discussion questions (with a suggested answer or listening-for), and a draw-it extension.

Read-aloud routine (10 to 12 minutes):

  1. Picture walk (1 min). Show the cover and ask, "What do you think will happen?"
  2. Read (4 to 6 min). Use voices. Pause at the [Join in!] lines.
  3. Talk (3 min). Use 3 to 4 questions. Accept short answers, points, and thumbs.
  4. Hackles chant (1 min). Hackles up! Pause. Check. Tell.
  5. Draw it (optional). One picture of "a time I told a grown-up" or "what Hackle's quills look like when it is up."

Promises to repeat in every story: A false alarm is fine. It is never your fault.

Art notes (from the Art Style Guide v2): Hackle is a baby hedgehog with soft pale-yellow, tangerine-tipped quills that stand up with a tiny "!" sparkle when something is off. Latch is a mint-green padlock with a keyhole smile. Peeper is a sky-blue magnifying-glass/owl-ish critter. Pip is a coral hermit-crab/snail. Buddy is a grape bear/dog-ish pal with a heart badge. Ember is a teal flame/sun sprite. Fixit is a tangerine helper robot with a toolbelt. Gifty is a gold gift box with a sparkly bow and a tiny hook. Human kids are drawn with varied tones and abilities (Maya in a bright blue wheelchair, Lily with soft-purple hearing aids, Omar with round glasses, Dev with a sketchbook). No scary imagery. Clean line art for coloring.

Word counts: each story is 400 to 800 words. K-2 stories are written to be read aloud; children do not need to read them.

Part 1: Four read-alouds for K-2 (ages 5-7)#

Read-Aloud 1: Hackle's Quills Pop Up#

Skill: Notice the tingly "uh-oh" (Hackles up), Pause, Check, Tell.
Lesson: K-2 L1 My Tell-Someone Team; L3 Pause and Ask.
Cast: Maya, her dad, Hackle, Peeper.

Illustration note Cover. Hackle (baby hedgehog, quills lying flat, big kind eyes, rosy cheeks) waves from the corner of a family-room couch. Maya sits in her bright blue wheelchair beside the couch with a tablet on her lap.

Hackle is a baby hedgehog, small and round. Hackle's quills are soft and pale yellow, with orange tips, like a little crown of sunshine. When everything is calm, the quills lie down, flat and smooth. That is called Hackles Down.

But when something feels a little bit odd, a little bit too good, a little bit pushy, or a little bit secret, the quills go POP! They stand straight up with a tiny sparkle that looks like an exclamation mark. That is called Hackles Up.

Illustration note Two small pictures side by side. Left: Hackle with quills down, smiling, labeled "Hackles Down." Right: Hackle with quills standing up, tiny "!" sparkle, eyes wide but still cute, labeled "Hackles Up."

One sunny afternoon, Maya was playing a game on her family's tablet. Her dad was in the kitchen, stirring a big pot of soup. Hackle was sitting on the arm of the couch, humming.

Beep-boop! A bright pop-up bounced onto the screen. It had flashing stars and a picture of a fluffy pink pony. It said:

YOU WON A PONY! TAP NOW! ONLY 5 SECONDS LEFT!

Illustration note Maya's tablet screen filled with flashing stars and a pink pony. Maya's eyes are wide. Behind her, Hackle's quills are popping up with a sparkle.

Maya's finger floated over the screen. A pony! A real one! She could almost hear it going clip-clop.

But Hackle's quills went POP!

"Maya," whispered Hackle, "I have a tingly uh-oh feeling."

Maya felt it too. A funny little flutter in her tummy, like a butterfly saying, "Hmmm."

"That's called hackles up," said Hackle. "It means something might be off. A pop-up that says tap NOW and only 5 seconds? That feels pushy. And a pony inside a tablet? That feels too good to be true."

[Join in!] Ask the children: "What did Maya feel?" (Hackles up!) Let them brush up the back of their necks with a hand, fingers spreading like spikes.

"So what do I do?" asked Maya.

"Four steps," said Hackle. "Hackles up. Pause. Check. Tell."

Illustration note Hackle holds up four paws. Four small pictures on the page: quills up (Hackles up), a flat hand like a stop sign (Pause), eyes peeking (Check), a hand on a heart reaching out (Tell).

"First, I Pause." Maya held up a flat hand like a stop sign. Pause Palm. She did not tap. She took one slow breath. In... and out.

"Second, I Check." Maya put two fingers to her eyes and looked around. She saw Peeper, the sky-blue critter with big eyes, peeking over the coffee table with a magnifying glass.

"Hmmm," said Peeper. "A pony that says tap now? Hmmm. Let's look for clues. Is it a secret? Is it in a hurry? Is it too good?"

"It's in a hurry," said Maya. "And it's too good."

"Great noticing!" said Peeper.

"Third, I Tell." Maya put her hand on her heart, then reached out. Tell Wave. She rolled her chair to the kitchen.

"Dad," she said, "I need help. A pop-up said I won a pony, and I got a tingly feeling."

Dad turned off the stove. He knelt down. "Thank you for telling me," he said. "It is not your fault. And you did exactly the right thing."

Illustration note Dad kneeling by Maya's chair, soup pot bubbling behind them. Hackle's quills relaxing slowly, the "!" sparkle fading into a heart.

Dad looked at the tablet. "That is a trick," he said. "It's called a fake prize. It wants you to tap before you think. Pop-ups like that are not real." He closed it and showed Maya how to ask for help any time.

"What if it was real?" asked Maya.

"Then checking was still a good idea," said Dad. "A false alarm is fine! Nobody is ever in trouble for a false alarm."

Hackle's quills lay down, soft and calm. Hackles Down.

"Now, who wants soup?" said Dad.

[Join in!] Everyone did the chant together: "Hackles up! (brush the neck) Pause! (palm) Check! (eyes) Tell! (hand on heart)."

Illustration note Final spread. Maya, Dad, Hackle, and Peeper around the table eating soup. A picture on the wall shows the four moves. Hackle's quills are down, smiling.

The end.

Talk about it#

  1. What did Hackle's quills do? (Popped up! Hackles up.) What did Maya feel? (A tingly uh-oh, a butterfly.)
  2. What made the pop-up feel fishy? (It was in a hurry. It was too good.)
  3. What did Maya do first? (Pause.) Show me!
  4. Who did Maya tell? Who is your trusted grown-up?
  5. What would have happened if it was just a regular game message? (A false alarm is fine.)

Listening for: the words Pause and Tell; that telling is not a problem.

Draw it#

Draw Hackle with the quills up. Draw one grown-up you would tell.

Read-Aloud 2: Latch and the Silly Words#

Skill: A password should be long, silly, and secret. Only tell a trusted grown-up.
Lesson: K-2 L2 Secret Passwords.
Cast: Dev, Latch, Dev's grandmother, Hackle.

Illustration note Cover. Latch, a round mint-green padlock with a keyhole smile, holds a tiny key balloon. Dev sits on the floor with his sketchbook.

Dev loved to draw. He drew rocket ships. He drew dinosaurs. He drew purple bananas.

Today Dev's grandmother had a surprise: Dev could draw on a drawing app on the family tablet! But the app had a little locked door, and a voice that said, "Please make a password."

Dev frowned. "What is a password?"

"A password is a secret key," said a friendly voice. Out of the tablet bag popped Latch, a round, mint-green padlock with a keyhole smile. "I'm Latch! I keep things safe. I love passwords."

Illustration note Latch pops out of a canvas bag with a little hop, sparkles around. Dev's eyes are big. His grandmother is at the stove.

"Dev," said Latch, "most people pick a password like their name and a number. Dev123! But anyone could guess that. A thief could guess it in one try!"

"Oh no!" said Dev. "What do I do?"

"We make a silly password of three silly words or more," said Latch. "Long. Silly. Secret. Those are my three rules."

[Join in!] Hold up three fingers. "Long! Silly! Secret!"

"Think of three silly things," said Latch.

Dev thought. "A purple banana," he said. "A trumpet. A dinosaur."

"Purple... banana... trumpet... dinosaur!" said Latch, clapping. "Wow! That's four silly words! That is a LONG, silly password. Nobody could guess that."

Dev laughed so hard he fell off the cushion.

Illustration note A silly picture of a purple banana playing a trumpet while a green dinosaur dances. The words "purple-banana-trumpet-dinosaur" are written along a ribbon.

Read-Aloud 5: The Treasure That Wasn't#

Skill: Use SPOT (Secret, Pressure, Offer too good to be true, Trick to move you) to spot a fake giveaway; check a different way; tell.
Lesson: 3-5 L3 Scam Spotters.
Cast: Priya, Amara, Peeper, Gifty (the Too-Good Box), Coach Dee.

Illustration note Cover. Priya holding her notebook and magnifying glass; Peeper (sky blue, big eyes) perched on her shoulder; a gold gift box labeled "FREE!!!" with a tiny hook at the edge of the page.

It was Saturday, and Priya was playing Pixel Pals on the family computer with her friend Amara, who was on a video call from her own house. (Pixel Pals is a pretend game.) They were building a castle out of glowing blocks.

PIXEL PALS GIVEAWAY!!! You have been chosen to win 10,000 FREE coins! Only 5 minutes left! Click this link and log in to claim: pixelpals-rewards.example. Don't tell your parents, they might ruin the surprise!

"Whoa!" said Amara. "Ten thousand coins! That's enough for a dragon skin!"

Read-Aloud 6: Mateo's Passphrase Puzzle#

Cast: Mateo, Abuela, Latch, Hackle.

"Yes!" said Mateo. "I'm a codebreaker. I know how."

"Silly!" said Abuela, laughing so hard her glasses slid down.

"Why not use my birthday?" asked Abuela. "I'll never forget it."

"Mateo," said Abuela, "you're a very good codebreaker."

"I'm even better at being a code protector," said Mateo.

The end.

  3. What did Mateo say to Diego? (Nope. My passphrase is only for me and my grown-up.)
  4. Who can help you keep your passphrases safe? (A trusted adult or a password manager your family chooses.)
  5. Make up a silly four-word phrase out loud (not a real one!).

Read-Aloud 8: Sam's Whoops Day#

Lesson: 3-5 L10 Oops Recovery.
Cast: Sam, Grandma Whitfeather, Fixit, Hackle.

Illustration note Cover. Sam (artist, they/them, a pencil behind their ear) at a drawing desk with a tablet; a tangerine helper robot, Fixit, stands next to them holding a toolbox and a bandage.

Sam was an artist. They drew comics about a squad of kids who saved the day with silly gadgets. Sam's grandmother, Grandma Whitfeather, lived next door, and she always read the comics first.

Sam's heart jumped. "My drawings!" they said. "Eight months of comics!"

They clicked the link. A page appeared that looked just like the drawing app. Sam typed their password. Click. The page went blank.

Sam's stomach went cold. "Oh no," they whispered. "I think that was a trick."

Illustration note Sam's tablet screen showing a blank page. Sam's eyes are wide. Hackle's quills are popping up on the desk, with a sparkle.

Sam wanted to slam the tablet shut. They wanted to hide in a closet. They thought, "I should have known."

"I messed up," said Sam, voice small. "I clicked and typed my password."

"Do you want to hear my OOPS card?" said Fixit. It pulled from its toolbelt a bright card with four big letters.

Illustration note Close-up of the OOPS card. O (open hands), O (hands pushing away), P (hand on heart, reaching out), S (turning a pretend key). Each in a different bright color.

"O: Oh, I'm okay," said Fixit. "Take a breath. Open your hands. You are okay."

"P: People," said Fixit. "Tell a trusted adult. That is the most important one."

Sam felt their stomach flip. "What will Grandma say?"

"She will say thank you for telling me," said Fixit. "Trust me. I'm a robot, I have a perfect memory for grandma things."

"And now S," said Fixit. "Secure it. Change the password. Turn on a second lock."

Grandma and Sam went on the family tablet together. Sam changed their password to a long, silly phrase: whale-pudding-umbrella-comet. Grandma showed Sam how to turn on 2-step verification. They checked the account for odd activity. There wasn't any. Sam's comics were safe.

Illustration note Sam and Grandma Whitfeather side by side at the table, Grandma pointing to the screen. Fixit is holding up a tiny wrench. Hackle's quills lie down.

"Good news," said Grandma. "We caught it early. And you told me right away."

"I feel better," said Sam. "Like a balloon went out of my chest."

"That is what telling does," said Fixit. "That's why it's the P in OOPS."

That night Sam drew a new comic. It was about a hero who made a mistake and told someone, and everything turned out fine. The last panel said: "Oops happens. Telling fixes it."

The end.

Talk about it#

  1. What do the four OOPS letters mean? (Oh I'm okay; Off and out; People; Secure it.)
  2. Why did Sam feel scared to tell? (They thought they'd be in trouble.)
  3. How did Grandma Whitfeather react? (Thanked Sam, helped.)
  4. What does "secure it" mean? (Change the password; add a second lock.)
  5. Has a grown-up ever fallen for a trick? What happened? (They fixed it.)

Listening for: that mistakes are normal, telling is safe, and recovery has steps.

Try it#

Use the four OOPS hand motions with a partner as a speed round.

Part 3: Three graphic-story scripts for grades 6-8 (ages 11-13)#

Plain, respectful tone. No mascots in dialogue. Hackle may appear only as a small corner icon (a tiny hedgehog in the bottom-right corner of the page whose quills pop up when a character's hackles go up). Characters come from the 6-8 case files (Jordan, Remy, Anika, Nia, Idris). [VERIFY: align names, ages, and details with the case files CF-1, CF-2 and CF-9 in the Teacher Guide before printing.]

How to use: Read as a class (volunteers read panel captions and dialogue; narrator is the teacher), project panels, or give students blank 6-panel pages to illustrate. Each script has Pause Points (stop and discuss) and an Answer Key-style note for teachers. Reading time: 4 to 6 minutes each.

Page style: Limited palette (black ink, two accent colors), clean line art, expressive but realistic characters (varied tones, glasses, a wheelchair, a hearing aid). No violence, no scary imagery. Screens are drawn as simple rounded rectangles with text.

Graphic Story 1: Jordan's Weird Message#

Skill: Notice early warning signs (flattery, privacy, "don't tell"), use the Help Ladder and the Next-Rung Rule, say the Tell-3 script.
Lesson: 6-8 L1 Help Ladder: Real Practice (CF-1).
Format: 10 panels, one page spread.
Characters: Jordan (12), Jordan's cousin Avery (13), Ms. Alvarez (counselor).

PAGE 1

Panel 1. Wide establishing shot. Jordan's bedroom, evening. Jordan sits on a bed with a controller in hand, headset around the neck. A TV shows a colorful team game (generic, no brand). A tiny Hackle icon sits quietly in the bottom-right corner, quills down.
CAPTION: "Jordan was on a winning streak."

Panel 2. Close-up of the TV screen: a chat panel. A message from "NightFox_77."
NIGHTFOX_77 (chat): "You're really good. Want to chat somewhere more private? Also, don't tell anyone, okay?"
JORDAN (thought bubble): "Huh."

Panel 3. Jordan's face, mid-close-up. A flicker of a smile, then a frown. The corner icon's quills pop up with a tiny "!" sparkle.
CAPTION: "Flattered... and something felt off."

Panel 4. Jordan staring at the screen, thumb hovering. Overlay: a faint thought bubble with a pause symbol.
JORDAN (thought): "Why 'don't tell anyone'? Why somewhere more private?"
CAPTION: "Jordan's hackles were up. Why?"

PAUSE POINT 1: Ask: "What feelings can you see in Jordan's face? What is the message asking for? What clue came first: the compliment, the privacy ask, or the secret?"

PAGE 2

Panel 5. Jordan closes the chat. The screen goes to a game menu. Jordan leans back.
CAPTION: "Jordan closed the chat. Pause."
JORDAN (muttering): "They'll take my tablet away if I tell. Or think I was careless."

Panel 6. Split panel. Left: Jordan imagines a parent looking disappointed (a faint thought bubble). Right: a "Barrier" speech cloud listing: "I'll lose my device." "They'll freak out." "It's probably nothing."
CAPTION: "Barriers are real. They are also fixable."

Panel 7. Jordan texts cousin Avery. A phone screen.
JORDAN (text): "Hey. Weird message. Can you come over?"
AVERY (text): "Sure. 10 min."
CAPTION: "Rung two: a friend who can come with you."

Panel 8. Avery and Jordan at the kitchen table, phone between them. Avery shrugs.
AVERY: "Honestly? I'd ignore it. It's probably just some random."
JORDAN: "But it said not to tell anyone."
CAPTION: "The first person did not help. This is where many people stop."

PAUSE POINT 2: Ask: "Why did Avery say ignore it? Why is that not enough? What would you say to Jordan?"

PAGE 3

Panel 9. A ladder drawn in the margin: four rungs. Rung 1: Yourself (Pause). Rung 2: A friend (checked). Rung 3: A trusted adult. Rung 4: A reporting channel. Jordan's thumb is on a hand drawing of the ladder; an arrow points from rung 2 to rung 3.
CAPTION: "The Next-Rung Rule: if the first person does not help, you are not done."
JORDAN: "Okay. Next rung."

Panel 10. Ms. Alvarez's office, next morning. A calm room with a plant. Jordan holds a printed screenshot and a sticky note with three sentences. Ms. Alvarez listens.
JORDAN (reading from note): "Something is going on and I need help figuring out what to do. A person I don't know asked me to move to a private chat and said not to tell anyone. I'm not in trouble; I just need to tell you something."
MS. ALVAREZ: "Thank you for telling me. You did exactly the right thing. It is not your fault. Let's figure out the next steps together."
CAPTION: "Telling is not tattling. It is the strongest move."

PAUSE POINT 3 / Discussion: (1) Which rung did Jordan climb to first? (2) What barriers did Jordan have? (3) What was the Tell-3 script? (4) What would you say if you were Ms. Alvarez? Teacher note: If a student discloses, follow your Teacher Guide's "If a student discloses" box.

Teacher notes. Jordan's story deliberately shows a low-pressure first contact so students can practice noticing before anything has gone wrong. Do not add details about what a manipulator might say next. Keep it at what Jordan can do. The last panel is the "skill" panel; keep it on the screen while students write their own three-sentence Tell script on a sticky note.

Graphic Story 2: The Chat That Got Loud#

Skill: Save evidence, use the Upstander Four (Say It, Stand With, Keep It [don't pass it on], Tell It), know when to escalate.
Lesson: 6-8 L9 Group Chat Dynamics (CF-9).
Format: 10 panels over 3 pages.
Characters: Anika (12), Nia (12), Idris (13), Mr. Lee (teacher).

PAGE 1

Panel 1. A group chat screen. The group name is "Room 12 Crew." Messages scroll fast. The top message from Idris: "did you see Nia's photo?? lol." Another: "look at her face in it." A third: "someone post it."
CAPTION: "It started as a joke."

Panel 2. Anika at her desk at home, phone in hand. Her shoulders tense. The corner icon (tiny Hackle) pops up.
CAPTION: "Anika's hackles went up."
ANIKA (thought): "This is getting mean."

Panel 3. Close-up on the screen. Messages get faster. "lol" "omg" "send it." An unflattering photo of a person (shown only as a blurred gray shape).
CAPTION: "The chat got louder."

Panel 4. Anika's thumb hovers over the keyboard. A series of thought bubbles: "If I say something, they'll turn on me." "If I don't, it keeps going."
CAPTION: "Speaking up is hard. It is also powerful."

PAUSE POINT 1: Ask: "What does Anika feel? What are her options? What is the risk of doing nothing?"

PAGE 2

Panel 5. Anika taps screenshot. A small camera icon. Caption box shows a checklist: "Save: names, times, words. Don't save: private images. Don't forward."
CAPTION: "Step one: SAVE what matters."

Panel 6. Anika writes a message to the group. The chat window shows her message:
ANIKA (chat): "Hey. That's not cool. Let's stop posting about Nia."
CAPTION: "Say It."
Beneath, two small replies: "ok fine." and "relax."

Panel 7. Anika texts Nia privately.
ANIKA (text): "Hey. I saw the chat. That's not okay. Are you okay? I'm here."
MAYA (text): "Thanks. I didn't know how to say anything."
CAPTION: "Stand With."

Panel 8. Idris, alone in his room, looks at the chat. His face shows guilt. He has not replied yet. A message from another friend reads: "send it, don't be boring."
CAPTION: "Keep It: do not pass it on." Idris's thumb moves away from "forward".

PAUSE POINT 2: Ask: "Which of the Upstander Four did Anika use? Which did Idris use? Which one feels easiest for you? Which is hardest?"

PAGE 3

Panel 9. Mr. Lee's classroom, before the bell. Anika hands Mr. Lee a printed screenshot. Mr. Lee reads it with a serious, kind expression.
ANIKA: "Mr. Lee, can I show you something about a chat? Some people were being cruel to Nia."
MR. LEE: "Thank you for telling me. You are not in trouble. You did the right thing by saving this and telling me."
CAPTION: "Tell It."

Panel 10. A split panel: the chat has gone quiet; a new message from Idris:
DEV (chat): "I'm sorry. I'm taking that down."
BELOW: a small caption: "Everyone can change course."
CAPTION: "Nia's friends sat with her at lunch the next day."

PAUSE POINT 3 / Discussion: (1) What did Anika save? What did she not save? (2) When would this need to go to an adult immediately? (Threats, someone's address, a risk of harm.) (3) What if Mr. Lee were too busy? (Next-Rung Rule: ask the counselor or another adult.) (4) What can Idris do now? Teacher note: If any message suggests someone might hurt themselves or others, follow your school's protocol and involve your counselor; in the U.S. the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available [VERIFY]. Do not leave a student alone.

Teacher notes. The story depicts an unkind chat without reproducing the cruel words. Keep the photo blurred, never show a face, and never ask students to imagine the content. The focus is on what the onlookers can do.

Graphic Story 3: Remy's Locked-Out Game#

Skill: Password reuse and account takeover; the Oops path (Pause, tell, change passwords, turn on 2-step, check); no blame.
Lesson: 6-8 L2 Account Defense (CF-2).
Format: 10 panels over 3 pages.
Characters: Remy (12), Remy's older brother Theo (16), Mom.

PAGE 1

Panel 1. Remy's bedroom, afternoon. Remy sits at a laptop. The screen shows a game's login page with a red banner: "Wrong password." Remy's face shows confusion.
CAPTION: "Remy couldn't log in."

Panel 2. Close-up of the screen. An email notification: "Your password was changed." Remy's expression moves to alarm. The corner icon (tiny Hackle) pops up.
CAPTION: "Someone else had changed it."

Panel 3. Remy's thoughts, shown as a visual flash. Three small memory panels: (a) the same password written on a sticky note; (b) a login to a random site; (c) a "Data breach" headline in a news ticker (generic text, no real company).
CAPTION: "Remy used the same password on lots of accounts."

Panel 4. Remy sits back in the chair. Hands over mouth, then hands down. A thought bubble: "It's my fault. They'll be so mad."
CAPTION: "This is the moment many people hide it. Don't."

PAUSE POINT 1: Ask: "What is the first thing Remy should do? Why does the thought 'it's my fault' make people hide? What is true instead?" (It is never your fault; tell someone.)

PAGE 2

Panel 5. Remy walks into the kitchen where Theo is eating cereal.
SAM: "Hey. I think someone took my game account. I used the same password on a bunch of things."
THEO: "Okay. Breathe. That happens to a lot of people. Let's tell Mom."
CAPTION: "Rung two: someone who comes with you."

Panel 6. Mom, Theo, and Remy at the table with a laptop open. Mom listens calmly.
MOM: "Thank you for telling me. It's not your fault. Let's work on it together."
CAPTION: "Rung three: a trusted adult."

Panel 7. A checklist drawn on a whiteboard in the background: Pause (breathe). Tell. Change passwords. Turn on 2-step. Check for odd messages. Each has a check box.
CAPTION: "The Oops path."

Panel 8. Over-the-shoulder on the laptop screen: the account recovery page. Mom's finger points to "Change password" and "Turn on 2-step verification." The screen shows a simple code prompt, not a real brand.
CAPTION: "Change the password. Add a second lock."

PAUSE POINT 2: Ask: "Why did Remy need to change other accounts too? What does 2-step verification do? What does 'Oops path' mean?"

PAGE 3

Panel 9. Remy and Theo with a password manager (shown as a generic locked vault icon). Theo shows Remy how to make a unique, long passphrase for each account.
THEO: "One passphrase per account. The manager remembers them."
CAPTION: "One lock for each door."

Panel 10. Remy, a few days later, smiling at the login page. A green banner reads "Welcome back." A speech bubble: "Everything is back. I feel better."
CAPTION: "Remy told someone. Remy fixed it. That's the whole story."

PAUSE POINT 3 / Discussion: (1) What are the four or five steps of the Oops path? (2) Which account is the most important to protect? (Email: it resets the others.) (3) What is one change you will make this week? (4) Who is your person to tell? Teacher note: Never ask students to share real passwords. Use the pretend passphrases from the mission or your own examples.

Teacher notes. Offer "Draw the missing panel" as an extension: what happens when Remy tells two friends to change their passwords too? Keep the activity on the page; do not use real accounts or devices.

Accessibility variants (all stories)#

Accessibility variants (all stories)
Need What to do
Color-vision difference The stories rely on shape, expression and words, not color. In the art, every Crew member is also identifiable by shape (padlock, magnifier, shell, bear, flame, robot, hedgehog) and a name tag.
Low vision / blind Read aloud with clear voices; describe each [Illustration] in one sentence; offer a large-print copy (at least 20 pt) and a tactile Trusted Hand poster (fingers cut from felt).
Deaf or hard of hearing Face the group; use big signals for each [Join in!]; show the story page or panels as you read; offer a sign-language reader if available (Lily's sign for "help" is a fist on a palm [VERIFY with a Deaf educator]). Captions for any recording.
Dyslexia / reading difficulty Children never read aloud unless they choose to. Keep the print large with a clear sans-serif font; use the picture notes.
English language learners Do the picture walk first, teach 4 words (pause, check, tell, trusted), repeat the [Join in!] lines, invite home-language retelling in pairs. A bilingual helper can read the call-and-response lines.
Motor / mobility Every motion has a seated or small version (hand-only Hackles Up, tap-the-desk Pause). Maya's wheelchair is part of the story, never a problem in it.
Sensory / noise Use the whisper version of the chant; skip sound effects; offer a quiet corner.
Anxiety / past experiences Preview the story with your counselor if you teach children who have had a recent incident. Read-Aloud 7 (The Mystery Teammate) and Graphic Stories 1 and 2 (Jordan's Weird Message; The Chat That Got Loud) touch on pressure; skip them for families who opted out of the related lessons.
