About Hackles and Competence Collective#

Hackles is a free classroom kit made by Competence Collective. It is free because every child deserves to learn how to handle tricky moments online, and because teachers should not have to buy a program to do it. Competence Collective builds hands-on, practice-first learning for cybersecurity skills.

"Hackles" are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off. The kit teaches children to notice that feeling and act on it: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. "Trust your hackles" never means "you must always be right." A false alarm is fine and welcome. If hackles were ignored, or someone was tricked anyway, it is never the child's fault, and the next step is to tell someone.

Feedback, corrections and ideas are welcome: [CONTACT: to be added].

Where it leads: Hackles is the first step on a learning path that continues with free resources such as picoCTF and CYBER.ORG, and for college and professional learners tools like Competence Collective's Red Herrings (redherrings.app) and Hotwash (hotwa.sh). (This note is for adults only; it does not appear in any student page.)

Part 1: What the Concepts Lab is for#

The Hackles lessons give students behaviors: notice, pause, check, tell. Students ask "why?" all the time: Why do websites store fingerprints, not passwords? Why can't I just change letters to symbols? Why does the lock icon not mean safe? Why do I have to update? Teachers who understand the concept behind the habit answer confidently and teach more accurately.

The Concepts Lab is a library of 14 short, hands-on demos that you do yourself first (roughly 20 to 25 minutes each) and then teach (15 or 45 minutes). By the end of each, you can explain the idea in plain words, to a 6-year-old and a 16-year-old, and you know the common misconceptions.

What the Lab is NOT#

  • Not an attack course. Every demo is defense only. Students never practice on real systems, real people, real accounts or real malware. Where a "thief" or "burglar" appears, it is a scripted paper role.
  • Not a scare. The tone follows the Hackles voice: warm, steady, never shaming, never "gotcha."
  • Not dependent on devices. The demos use paper, dice, cards, tape, envelopes and people.

How every demo file is organized: three parts, in this order#

  1. Teacher guide (keep this; it is for you). Sections: Big idea and "at a glance" table, a 60-second Try This Now, what a teacher should know (with a misconceptions table), materials, Part A (teachers do it first), Part B (teaching it, with 15-minute and 45-minute versions), teacher script, analogies by band (K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12), discussion questions, level-up challenge, safety and ethics notes (defense only), assessment idea, and sources with the [VERIFY] list.
  2. Student handout (a clean, self-contained page or two, with no answers on it). Copy it as it is. It carries everything students need to work: word lists, specimens, cards, tables and blanks. No marketing, no student data collected.
  3. TEACHER COPY ONLY answer page (the last page, after a page break), titled "TEACHER COPY ONLY: Answers for Demo N (do not distribute to students)" with a warning box at the top. It holds the answer keys and teacher notes for the handout and activity cards. Print the handout pages only, or detach the last page before copying. Never project it, post it or send it home.

Quick path for a busy teacher (10 minutes). Read "Big idea" and "at a glance", do the Try This Now, skim the misconceptions table, read the teacher script, and run the 15-minute version with the handout. Come back for Part A and the 45-minute version when you have a staff meeting.

Part 2: How to use it: Do, Debrief, Teach#

Part 2: How to use it: Do, Debrief, Teach
Step What you do Time
1. Do Run Part A of the demo yourself (alone, in pairs, or at a staff meeting). Play every role. 20-25 min
2. Debrief Ask: "What surprised me? What did I get wrong? What will a student ask?" Read the misconceptions table. 5-10 min
3. Check Look at the [VERIFY] items. If you will quote a fact to students, confirm it with the source listed. as needed
4. Teach Pick the 15-minute or 45-minute version. Use the script, then the handout and exit ticket. 15 or 45 min
5. Reflect Use the assessment idea. Note what to change next time. 5 min

Why this order matters. People forget lessons they only read. Doing the demo builds a mental picture, so you can improvise answers when students surprise you.

Staff meeting option. One demo per meeting turns a staff meeting into a 30-minute hands-on session: 20 minutes of Part A plus 10 of discussion.

Grade-band note. Every demo has a K-2 version, but it is usually a concept seed (a story, a rule, a gesture) rather than the full activity. Do not push abstract details on young children. For K-2, always use pretend passwords, pretend scenarios and the "grown-up check."

Part 4: CSTA-style standards map [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: every code below exists in the CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017, revised); mappings are approximate]#

Important. Round 3B check (2026-10-05): every code below was looked up in the CSTA K-12 Computer Science Standards (2017, revised; csteachers.org PDF) and exists; the quoted wording matches. The mapping of each demo to a code is still a best-effort alignment, not an official CSTA endorsement, so say "mapped to" and keep the word "approximate" in any school report or grant document. Some wording below is a paraphrase of the concept, not a quotation. State standards and CSTA's AI-related updates may also apply. ISTE and CASEL mappings follow the same caution.

CSTA concept areas used: NI = Networks and the Internet (includes the cybersecurity sub-concept); IC = Impacts of Computing (privacy, safety, law, ethics, careers); CS = Computing Systems (troubleshooting); DA = Data and Analysis; AP = Algorithms and Programming.

Part 8: Teacher "Why?" Answer Bank#

Short, accurate answers for the questions students really ask. Each has a pointer to the demo to learn more. Items with [VERIFY] should be checked before you quote specifics.

Part 8: Teacher "Why?" Answer Bank
Question a student might ask Short answer Demo
Why is a long password better than a weird short one? Each extra random character or word multiplies the number of possibilities. Length beats tricks like swapping a for @. 1
Why can't I just use my dog's name and a number? Names and numbers are in the first lists guessers try, and details about you can be found online. Random is not guessable. 1, 9
How can a website check my password if it doesn't keep it? It keeps a one-way fingerprint (hash). It makes a fingerprint of what you type and compares. 2
Can hackers un-hash a hash? No, but they can guess passwords, hash each guess and compare. Weak passwords fall fast; long random ones do not. 2
What is "salt"? A random extra bit added before hashing so that two people with the same password get different fingerprints and prepared lists don't work. 2
Why do phishing emails work if they look so fake? Often they don't look fake. They work by pressure and a plausible ask, and they are sent to thousands. 3
Why shouldn't I click the link in a real-looking email? Links can hide their real destination. Go to the site by your own bookmark or app. 3
What is the difference between encoding, encryption and hashing? Encoding changes format (anyone can reverse); encryption scrambles with a key (reversible with the key); hashing makes a one-way fingerprint. 2, 4
Why is a secret code with only 25 keys easy to break? You can try all 25. Real encryption has more keys than anyone could ever try. 4
Can the government or hackers read encrypted messages? Well-designed encryption is very strong; attacks usually target the device, the keys or the person. [Policy debates about lawful access were not researched; say "that is a debate for law and policy" if asked.] 4
What does the padlock in my browser mean? The connection is encrypted and the site proved it controls that domain. It does not mean the site is honest. 5
Can my school see what I do? The network may see site names and amounts of traffic, and school-managed devices may log more, per policy. [VERIFY your school's policy] 5
Does incognito mode hide me? It only stops your device from saving history locally. 5
Why isn't a password enough? Passwords leak or get tricked out of us. A second proof from a different family stops most takeovers. 6
Why can't a fake website steal my passkey? The passkey is tied to the real site's address; your device won't sign for a look-alike. 6
Why do I have to update my phone? Updates fix known security holes; attackers use holes that have fixes but are not installed. 7
What is a zero-day? A hole that nobody on the defense side knew about yet, so there was no fix. Rare compared with known unpatched holes. 7
Why can't I just say no on the phone? You can! The callback rule makes it easy: "I'll call you back at the number I have." 8
Is a family code word silly? It is a simple, cheap lock in a world where voices can be imitated. 8, 12
How can data about me be "anonymous" and still identify me? Combine a few harmless facts (such as ZIP, birthday, gender) and they can point to one person. [VERIFY research figures] 9
What is a data broker? A company that collects, buys and sells information about people, often from sources people didn't think of as sharing. 9
Why should I have a backup if I have cloud storage? Sync mirrors mistakes and damage. A backup keeps a separate, older, protected copy that you've tested. 10
Should we pay a ransomware demand? Authorities advise against it: no guarantee, and it funds crime. It's a decision for professionals with legal advice. [VERIFY] 10
Is a virus the same as malware? A virus is one kind of malware. Others include worms, Trojans, spyware and ransomware. 11
Do Macs and phones get malware? Less often than some systems, but yes. 11
Can I tell a deepfake by looking at the hands? Sometimes, but not reliably, and it gets harder. Check the source and use a second channel. 12
Why is it bad to say "everything is fake"? Real things happen; blanket doubt helps people who want to hide the truth. Verify instead. 12
What is a hotwash? A quick, blame-free review right after an event: what was supposed to happen, what did, why, what next. 13
Is hacking illegal? Accessing systems without permission can be a crime. With permission, and in scope, it's a job. 14
What should I do if I find a hole in a website by accident? Stop, don't share or save anything, and tell a trusted adult or the owner's security contact. 14

Part 9: Safety box: defense only, plus what to do if a student discloses#

Defense only: the Lab's non-negotiables#

  1. No attack how-to. No instructions to break into accounts, devices, networks, websites or people's privacy. No lock or door bypass. No phishing messages written to deceive real people. No malware, no code. No cracking tools. No scanning or probing.
  2. No real people, real organizations or real data. Use fictional characters, brands and passwords ("Brightway Bank", "Quinn", "Maple Street Bakery").
  3. No practice on real people. Role-plays use scripted cards and stay in class. No "test your parents" tasks.
  4. No real passwords. If a student volunteers one, thank them and say "change it tonight."
  5. Sensitive topics are optional for students. Anyone may say "pause" or step out. Offer a trusted adult.
  6. No shaming language. "It was a clever trick," never "you should have known."
  7. Hackles routine on every demo: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell.
  8. Role-play guardrails. Reader cards are read exactly as printed, nobody improvises pressure, no student is made the target in front of the class, and anyone may pass. Sensitive cards are marked optional.
  9. If a student wants more: point to legal practice routes (clubs, capture-the-flag sites made for learning, competitions with rules) described in Demo 14.

If a student discloses (read before your first demo)#

Several demos (3, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14) can prompt a student to say something real: a scam, a threat, a leaked photo, someone pressuring them, being followed or monitored, or something they did that crossed a line. A student telling you is a success, not a problem. This box is a short reminder. Your band's Teacher Guide ("If a student discloses") and the kit's Disclosure and Incident Guide (kit/05_Teacher_Support/Disclosure_and_Incident_Guide.md) are the authority. If anything here differs, follow those and your school's policy.

  • Stay calm and listen. Open prompts only: "Tell me more." "What happened next?" Ask only what you need to get help.
  • Believe and reassure: "Thank you for telling me." "This is not your fault." "You are not in trouble."
  • Be honest: "I can't keep this just between us because I need to get you help. Here is exactly what I'll do next." Do not promise secrecy or outcomes you cannot control.
  • Never view, open, copy, forward or screenshot suspected sexual images of a minor, including AI-generated ones, and do not ask the student to show you. Secure the device per policy and hand it to administration or law enforcement. [VERIFY with district counsel]
  • Do not question the student's choices, contact the other person or their family yourself, or discuss the case in the staff room.
  • Report as your district and state require; most teachers are mandated reporters, and in many states the duty is personal, so telling a principal may not satisfy it; also tell your administrator per your policy, and check your state's statute through the Child Welfare Information Gateway (childwelfare.gov) [VERIFY your state's statute]. For online exploitation involving minors, the school's process may include a report to NCMEC's CyberTipline [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: report.cybertip.org; 1-800-843-5678]. For risk of self-harm or violence, follow your district's protocol, involve the counselor, and use 988 or 911 as the protocol directs [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org].
  • Document factually (student's words, date, time, who you told) and follow up privately.
  • Look after yourself. Use your counselor or support network, without identifying details.

Part 11: Design notes and credits#

Why unplugged. Many security ideas (keys, packets, hashing, public/private keys, redundancy) can be understood with paper, string and people. This approach follows the long tradition of CS Unplugged from the University of Canterbury, whose free activities on cryptographic protocols and information hiding demonstrate the strengths of teaching security concepts offline [CS Unplugged is a project of the University of Canterbury, NZ, with content under CC BY-SA 4.0: VERIFIED 2026-10-05: csunplugged.org; the exact activity names, such as "Sharing Secrets", were not confirmed]. The Concepts Lab's activities are original designs inspired by that tradition; where a demo echoes a classic idea (such as the padlock box and the color-mixing key agreement), the demo says so. Teachers adapting CS Unplugged materials directly should follow its CC BY-SA 4.0 license [VERIFIED 2026-10-05].

Why games and competition. Programs such as Cyber Discovery and CyberStart (UK DCMS with SANS, 2017-2021; CyberStart is now closed [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: cyberstart.com]), Cyber Explorers (UK government, free, ages 11-14 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: gov.uk]) and picoCTF show that mission-based, game-like practice can engage learners at scale; the Hackles Concepts Lab borrows that energy (races, relays, role-plays, points) while keeping every activity defense-only and age-appropriate [participation numbers were not verified; do not cite them].

Why plain language plus honest limits. Each demo states what is known and where the model breaks (for example, "this toy hash is not secure", "paint mixing is an analogy", "hat colors are a shortcut"). Teachers earn trust by saying "that's a model" aloud.

Naming and voice. The Lab follows the Hackles Brand and Rename Rules: warm, steady, never shaming, "never your fault," Pause · Check · Tell, no student accounts or data collected, and no marketing in student-facing pages.

Accessibility notes. Provide movement alternatives (seated sort cards instead of runs), larger print, color-independent labels (write G, Y, R as well as using colors), reading support (read cards aloud), and allow students to pass any role-play. Offer pair-share before whole-class discussion. See the kit's Inclusion and Equity Guide in 05_Teacher_Support.

Revision. Threats and technology change quickly. Review the [VERIFY] items yearly, and update demos with new examples (not new attack details).
