Demo 14: Ethics, Law and Careers (The Permission Test, Hats and Responsible Disclosure)
At a glance

Try This Now

Try This Now (60 seconds, no prep): Hold up green, yellow or red paper: "A friend dares you to guess a classmate's password just to prove it's weak." Which light? Then ask: "What one question would have told you?" (Do I have permission?)

1. The big idea#

Cybersecurity is a dual-use skill: the knowledge that protects systems could also be used to harm them. That is true of locksmithing, medicine and chemistry, too. Society handles this with ethics (what we choose to do), law (what we are allowed to do) and professional norms (what responsible practitioners do).

Three questions form the Permission Test. Always ask them before touching any system that is not clearly yours:

  1. Permission: Do I have clear, written or explicit permission from the owner?
  2. Scope: Am I staying inside what they allowed?
  3. Purpose and harm: Is my aim to help, and am I avoiding harm to people (privacy, safety, disruption)?

If any answer is "no" or "I don't know": stop, and ask a trusted adult. If you find a problem by accident, the answer is responsible disclosure: stop, don't explore further, don't share, and tell the owner or a trusted adult.

This demo also opens the door to the careers that make the internet safer. The message to students: there is a legal, welcome, paid, interesting path, and it starts with ethics.

2. What a teacher should know (plain language)#

2.1 Hat colors: useful, but only a shortcut#

2.1 Hat colors: useful, but only a shortcut
Hat Common meaning Honest note
White hat Security people who have permission and aim to help Also called "ethical hackers" or penetration testers when hired
Black hat People who break into systems without permission for harm or profit Criminal
Gray hat Break rules or boundaries (e.g., access without permission) but without clearly malicious aim Still not allowed by law just because intentions seem good
Red team / blue team / purple team (9-12) Red = authorized "attackers" who test defenses; Blue = defenders; Purple = collaboration Professional, authorized roles

The hats are a metaphor, not a legal category. The law cares about authorization and harm, not about what we feel our intentions are. "I was just curious" and "I was trying to help" do not make unauthorized access legal.

The word "hacker" originally meant a clever tinkerer [VERIFY with a computing-history source such as the MIT Tech Model Railroad Club history]. Say "security researcher" or "ethical hacker" for the good version.

2.2 The law, in general terms (US-oriented; check your own jurisdiction)#

  • Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA, 18 U.S.C. 1030) is the main US federal law. It was first enacted in 1984 (Pub. L. 98-473) and expanded in 1986 (Pub. L. 99-474) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: law.cornell.edu 18 U.S.C. 1030 source note]. It makes it a crime to access a computer without authorization or to exceed authorized access, with various penalties depending on conduct and harm [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1030 (statute text and source notes)].
  • Van Buren v. United States (2021): the US Supreme Court read "exceeds authorized access" narrowly: a person exceeds it when they have authorization to use a computer but obtain information in particular areas (files, folders, databases) that are off-limits to them; the Court did not focus on the person's purpose in using what they could access [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-783_k53l.pdf; implications for counsel].
  • DOJ charging policy (2022) says federal prosecutors should not charge good-faith security research, but it is a policy, not a legal permission, and it does not bind states or private parties [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: Justice Manual 9-48.000, justice.gov/jm/jm-9-48000-computer-fraud].
  • State laws and other countries. Many states have their own computer-crime statutes, and other countries have comparable laws (for example the UK's Computer Misuse Act 1990) [VERIFY].
  • Minors can face criminal, civil and school consequences for unauthorized access, and schools have an Acceptable Use Policy (AUP) with its own consequences [VERIFY your school's AUP]. Fraud, stolen-data, harassment, interception, copyright and child-protection laws also apply.
  • Key lesson: an unlocked door is not an invitation. "It wasn't password-protected" or "it was easy" is not permission. Looking around inside a system without permission can be unauthorized access even if you "didn't change anything".

2.3 Ethics: a short toolkit#

  • Do no harm. Think about who could be hurt (privacy, safety, money, trust).
  • Be honest and transparent about what you did and found.
  • Act responsibly with knowledge. Share what protects; hold back what mainly enables harm.
  • Professional codes such as the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct and various industry codes state similar principles [VERIFY].
  • Gray areas exist. Teach students to reason (Permission Test), not to memorize rules.

2.4 Responsible (coordinated) vulnerability disclosure#

When someone finds a security problem in a system they do not own, the accepted professional approach is:

  1. Stop exploring. Do not access, copy, change or share anyone's data. Do not "prove" it by pushing further.
  2. Document minimally (what, where, when, with only the information needed to explain it). Do not keep or share other people's data.
  3. Report privately to the owner via their security contact (many sites publish a security.txt file or a vulnerability disclosure policy [RFC 9116, "A File Format to Aid in Security Vulnerability Disclosure": VERIFIED 2026-10-05: rfc-editor.org]), or through a bug bounty platform, or through a coordinator (CERT/CC or CISA) [VERIFY]. If there's no obvious contact, a trusted adult or the school's IT can help.
  4. Be patient and constructive. Give the owner reasonable time (professional norms commonly say 45 to 90 days, though nothing requires it) [VERIFY common timelines]. Those timelines are for adult professionals: students always go through a trusted adult.
  5. Do not demand payment or threaten. Asking for money to stay quiet is extortion, not research. Rewards offered by a bug bounty program are different, because they are part of an agreed program.
  6. Don't publish before it is fixed or before the agreed time, except via an agreed process.
  7. Look for safe-harbor language: some organizations promise not to take legal action against researchers who follow their rules [VERIFY examples].

Standards: ISO/IEC 29147 (vulnerability disclosure) and 30111 (handling) [VERIFY].

If a student finds something accidentally (for example, sees another student's information on a school site): Stop. Do not click around or save anything. Tell a trusted adult or the school's IT right away. Thank them, as noticing problems is a good thing.

2.5 Careers#

Job titles vary, but the field is broad and there are roles for different strengths:

2.5 Careers
Role What they do Strengths needed
Security analyst (SOC analyst) Watches alerts and investigates suspicious activity Curiosity, patience, pattern spotting
Incident responder Handles breaches (Demo 13) Calm under pressure, communication
Penetration tester ("ethical hacker") / red teamer Authorized testing of systems to find weaknesses before criminals do Creativity, deep technical skill, ethics
Security engineer / architect Designs and builds secure systems Engineering, systems thinking
Digital forensics examiner Investigates devices and data for evidence Detail, methodical documentation
Cryptographer / researcher Designs and analyzes encryption (Demo 4) Mathematics
GRC (governance, risk, compliance) Policies, laws, risk and audits Writing, judgment, law
Privacy professional Protects personal data, handles rights requests (Demo 9) Law, ethics, communication
IT support, networking, system admin The foundation: many security careers start here Practical troubleshooting

Facts to share, with checking: demand is strong; the US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects information security analyst employment to grow 21 percent from 2025 to 2035, much faster than the average for all occupations [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: bls.gov]; global workforce studies report persistent skills gaps [VERIFY (ISC)2 or CyberSeek data]. The NICE Workforce Framework for Cybersecurity (NIST SP 800-181) is a standard catalogue of work roles and skills [VERIFY].

Legal places to practice: school cyber clubs; capture-the-flag (CTF) competitions and practice sites built for learning (for example picoCTF, now CMU's CyLab Security Academy, free; age rules not stated on its page) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: picoctf.org]; CyberPatriot (a US middle and high school competition run by the Air & Space Forces Association; team fees apply, see the 9-12 Teacher Guide) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: uscyberpatriot.org]; local community college programs; virtual labs with isolated practice targets provided by your school; open-source "bug bounty" programs with rules, for adults and older teens [VERIFY age rules]. Emphasize: practice only on targets that are provided for practice or that you own, and always follow the rules.

2.6 Common misconceptions#

2.6 Common misconceptions
Myth Better understanding
"Hackers are always criminals." The skills are used legally by millions of professionals.
"If I only look, it's not a crime." Unauthorized access can be a crime even without damage.
"If their security was bad, it's their fault, not mine." Poor security does not make unauthorized access legal.
"I had good intentions, so it's okay." Intent matters to judges, but it does not create permission.
"You must learn to attack to defend." You need to understand attacks; you do not need to attack real systems.

3. Materials (zero to low cost)#

  • Light zones: three big signs on the floor or wall: GREEN (go), YELLOW (stop and ask), RED (don't). Tape or paper.
  • Scenario cards (below).
  • Permission Test card (three questions) for each team.
  • Paper hats (white, black, gray, red, blue) for a quick "hat check", optional.
  • Disclosure kit: "Found a Hole" report template (below) and role cards (Finder, Owner, Coordinator).
  • Career cards (the table above, cut into cards) and sticky notes for the "career gallery".
  • Student handout.

4. The activities#

4.1 Green, Yellow, Red Light (movement game)#

Read a scenario aloud. Students move to the light they think fits, then justify. The Permission Test card is the tie-breaker. After each, the teacher reveals the best answer and the reason.

4.1 Green, Yellow, Red Light (movement game)
# Scenario (all fictional) Light Why
1 The school hires a company. A signed agreement lists exactly which systems they may test and when. They test only those. Green Permission and scope.
2 You change the settings on your own tablet at home with a parent's okay to see what each one does. Green Your own device, with adult approval.
3 A friend dares you to guess a classmate's password "just to prove it's weak". Red No permission. Harm to a person's privacy.
4 Using a capture-the-flag practice site built for learners, you solve a challenge. Green The targets are provided for practice.
5 By accident, the lunch-ordering site shows you another student's information. Yellow Stop, don't explore, tell a trusted adult or IT.
6 A company runs a bug bounty program with written rules. You test only what the rules list. Green (for adults, or older teens only where the program's own age rules allow; many require 18) [VERIFY age rules] Permission and scope.
7 The same company has "out of scope" systems listed. You decide to check one "just in case". Red Outside scope.
8 You find a classmate's password on a sticky note and use it "just to look". Red No permission.
9 You notice by accident that a local business's website shows something it should not. You do not poke further. A trusted adult helps you email their security contact privately, and you wait. Green Responsible disclosure: noticed by accident, stopped, told, did not explore.
10 You tell a business, "Pay me or I'll tell everyone about your flaw." Red Extortion.
11 A school cyber club uses isolated virtual machines set up by the teacher for practice. Green Safe, permitted targets.
12 You post a screenshot of a classmate's private info on social media to "prove" your school's site is weak. Red Harm and privacy violation; do not share.
13 A tester hired to test the office also tries the neighboring business's Wi-Fi because it is "easy". Red Out of scope; not authorized.
14 You are not sure whether you are allowed to try something. Yellow When unsure, ask first.

Teaching note: "Yellow" is a skill: stop and ask is the professional's superpower. Praise it.

4.2 The Hat Check (optional, 3 minutes)#

Place each scenario on a hat color (white, gray, black) and notice how much the permission line explains the hats. "What makes a white hat white?" (Permission, scope, purpose, honesty.) "Can a gray hat go to jail?" (Yes; intent does not remove the need for permission.)

4.3 Found a Hole: Responsible Disclosure Role-Play (grades 6-12; simplified for 3-5)#

Setting. The fictional "Greenfield School Fair" website has a ticket page. A student, Robin, sees by accident that the page shows another family's name when the page reloads in a certain way (no technical detail needed). Robin does not investigate further.

Roles. Finder (Robin), Trusted Adult/IT, Site Owner, Coordinator.

Steps.

  1. Stop and note the minimum. Robin writes down only what is needed: the page address, the time and what looked wrong. Robin does not save or share anyone's information.
  2. Tell a trusted adult. The adult helps find the right contact.
  3. Write the report using the template.
  4. The owner responds with thanks and a timeline.
  5. The fix is made; Robin is thanked. The group does a mini hotwash (Demo 13).

"Found a Hole" report template:

  • Where I noticed it (page or system): __
  • When: __
  • What I saw, in one or two sentences (no personal data): __
  • Why I think it matters: __
  • What I did not do: I did not copy, share or change anything. __
  • How to reach me: __
  • I'm happy to help if useful. __

Variation: wrong ways. Show three quick "bad responses" (demand money, post online, keep poking) and have the class say why each is wrong and what the better action is.

4.4 Career Gallery Walk (5-10 minutes)#

Career cards around the room. Students walk, put a sticky dot on two that interest them, and write one question. Debrief: "Which roles don't involve breaking into anything?" (Most.) "Which skills do you already have?"

4.5 Ethics Debate: The Rule or the Rescue? (grades 9-12)#

Structured academic controversy. Scenario: a student finds a flaw in the school's grade portal and, without permission, tests how bad it is and then reports it. Pairs argue (a) "She meant well and reported, so she acted ethically" and (b) "She had no permission, so she acted wrongly", then swap sides, then find a consensus about what should have happened. Teacher guardrail: the consensus must land on "stop at the accidental find and ask or report; testing without permission is not authorized, however good the motive", plus what the school should do (a clear reporting policy, thanking reporters, fair consequences). Use the fictional portal only, never a real school system, and let any student pass.

5. Part A: Teachers do it first (about 25 minutes)#

  1. Traffic lights (10 min). Sort the 14 scenarios as individuals, compare, and check the reasons. Which did you hesitate on?
  2. Disclosure role-play (8 min). Do the Found a Hole roles in trios and write a quick report with the template.
  3. Know your school's rules (7 min). Find your school's AUP, its process for reporting a security concern and its contact. Draft a calm first sentence for a student who says "I found a hole" or confesses something: "Thank you for telling me. I want to help you do the right thing."

7. Teacher script#

Hook (2 minutes). "A locksmith can open a locked door in about thirty seconds. Is that a crime? When is it a crime? When is it a job? ... The skill is the same. What changes? Permission. That's the whole game today: the difference between a burglar and a locksmith is permission, because both can own the same lock picks. Let's learn the Permission Test."

During the lights. "Notice the Yellow zone. Stopping to ask is what professionals do before they touch anything."

On disclosure. "Imagine you're walking by a store and notice the back door is unlocked. Do you go in and look around? No. You find the owner and say, 'Your back door's open.' That's responsible disclosure."

On careers. "Look at the cards. Which job would you want? Notice how many of them are about helping people, writing, teaching, noticing patterns, or fixing things. This field needs lots of kinds of brains. And all of it starts with ethics."

Close. "Curiosity is a gift. Use it where you have permission: your own devices, clubs, practice sites. If you ever find a problem by accident, stop, don't share, and tell a trusted adult. That's the heroic move. And if you've already crossed a line, talk to a trusted adult. It's never too late to do the right thing, and the help is real."

9. Discussion questions#

  1. What is the difference between a locksmith and a burglar? What is the same?
  2. Why isn't "I was only curious" enough, and why is "stop and ask" a professional skill?
  3. What should a school do when a student reports a security problem? What shouldn't it do?

10. Level-up challenge#

  • Write a Vulnerability Reporting Policy for a fictional school: how to report, what is in and out of scope, how fast the school responds, and a promise not to punish good-faith reporters who follow the rules [VERIFY with counsel before any real adoption].
  • Join legal practice. Help start or join a school cyber club or CTF team under teacher supervision, using only learning platforms and isolated labs. A career interview (a visitor or a recorded talk) works well too.
  • Ethics case study (9-12). Read a well-documented public case of security research that prompted legal threats and discuss what each side could have done differently [VERIFY a suitable public case].

11. Safety and ethics notes (defense only)#

  • No attack instruction. Do not teach or demonstrate how to access, scan, probe or exploit any system. Do not run tools against school or home networks, websites or accounts. Do not recommend any tool for use against a target not provided for practice.
  • No "try it and see". Do not give any "homework" that involves testing real systems, including students' own accounts on third-party services whose terms forbid it.
  • Be careful with confessions. A student may confide that they did something unauthorized. Respond calmly: "Thank you for telling me. I'm glad you told me. I need to share this with the right people so we can sort it out and make sure no one is hurt." Do not promise confidentiality; follow the school's policy; do not punish the telling. Involve the counselor and administrator. [VERIFY with administration the policy for student-reported incidents.]
  • Use only legal practice environments and keep to the program's age rules and terms.
  • Equity. Cyber clubs and competitions may cost money or require devices; avoid implying that only some students belong. Present multiple paths.
  • Disclosures. If a student reveals that they are being threatened, coerced into hacking or extorted, or that an adult is directing them to do it, treat it as a safety matter and follow your reporting process (see the Index box).

12. Assessment idea#

  • Exit ticket. "Write the three questions of the Permission Test. Then say what you'd do if you found a hole by accident." Rubric: 3 = permission, scope, purpose/harm and stop-don't-share-tell; 2 = permission and one action; 1 = "I'd look further".
  • Scenario sort. Four new scenarios; students assign lights with a reason.

Student Handout: The Permission Test#

Name: ____

The big idea#

The difference between a defender and a lawbreaker is permission, scope and purpose, not skill.

The Permission Test (ask before touching any system that isn't yours)#

  1. Permission: do I have clear permission from the owner?
  2. Scope: am I staying inside what they allowed?
  3. Purpose and harm: is my aim to help, and am I avoiding harm?

If any answer is "no" or "not sure": stop and ask a trusted adult.

Green, Yellow, Red#

Write G, Y or R:

  1. A school hires a company with a signed agreement to test only its own systems. ____
  2. A friend dares you to guess a classmate's password. ____
  3. You solve a challenge on a practice website made for learners. ____
  4. You accidentally see another student's information on a school site. ____
  5. You tell a business: "Pay me or I'll publish your flaw." ____

If I find a hole by accident#

  1. Stop. Don't poke around.
  2. Don't share or save anything.
  3. Tell a trusted adult or the school's IT.

Careers are a lot of different jobs#

Circle two that interest you: analyst / incident responder / ethical hacker / forensic examiner / cryptographer / privacy professional / teacher / IT support / policy and law

One skill I already have: ____
One skill I want to build: ____

Where can I practice legally?#

Ask your teacher about school clubs, practice websites made for learning, and competitions with rules. Never test real systems that aren't yours.

Remember#

  • Curiosity is great. Use it where you have permission.
  • "It was easy" and "I only looked" are not permission.
  • If you ever crossed a line, talk to a trusted adult. It's never too late to do the right thing.

End of student handout.
