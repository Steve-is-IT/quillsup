1. How to use this pack#

The two-way loop. Most school programs send a letter home and stop there. This kit sends a take-home card out with each lesson and gets a Family Reply slip back, so families can tell you what they tried, what questions they have, and what help they'd like. The reply slip is optional and never graded.

Principles for talking with families

  • Partners, not problems. Assume every family cares deeply about their child's safety.
  • No blame, no fear. Leave out the alarming statistics and the scary stories. Describe the skills and what children will practice.
  • Plain language. Short sentences, no jargon. If you need a technical word, put it in the glossary.
  • Multiple formats. Paper, email, text or a phone call, whichever the family prefers.
  • Language access. Send materials in each family's home language where you can (see Section 10).
  • Every family is different. Say "grown-up you trust" or "caregiver," and don't assume anything about family structure, devices or income.

2. Take-home letter A: General introduction#

[School letterhead]

[Date]

Dear families and caregivers,

This year, [Class/Grade] will take part in Hackles, a short weekly program that helps children stay safe, kind, and confident online. Each lesson takes about 15 minutes, and children spend that time practicing skills, with no scary stories anywhere in sight.

What your child will learn

  • How to keep accounts and devices private and protected.
  • How to spot tricks, scams, and fake pictures or voices.
  • How to think about what to share online.
  • How to recognize when someone is not being safe or kind.
  • How to tell a trusted adult. This is the most important skill, and we practice it often.

Our habit: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell.

  • Hackles up? Notice the "uh-oh" feeling: something seems too good, pushy, secret, or scary.
  • Pause. Stop and take a breath before clicking, replying, or sending.
  • Check. Ask: Is this real? Who is this?
  • Tell. Tell a trusted adult. Telling is never getting in trouble.

What we ask of you

  • Talk with your child about what they're learning. Each lesson comes with a take-home card and a question to try at home.
  • Fill in the optional Family Reply slip and send it back. It tells us what worked and what questions you have.
  • Let your child know they can always come to you, and that they won't be punished for telling.

What this program does not do

  • It does not collect student accounts or personal data.
  • It does not teach children how to attack or break into systems.
  • It does not replace your family's values, rules, or conversations.

A few lessons cover more sensitive topics. Before those, we'll send you a note, along with a way to review the materials or ask questions.

If you have questions, please contact [Teacher name] at [email/phone] or our school counselor, [Name], at [email/phone].

Thank you for being our partner in this.

[Teacher name], [Title]

3. Take-home letter B: Sensitive-topic pre-notice#

[School letterhead]

[Date]

Dear families and caregivers,

On [date], our class will have a Hackles lesson called "[Lesson title]." We're writing ahead of time because this lesson covers a more sensitive topic.

What the lesson is about
[Choose one and edit.]

  • K-2, My Body Is Mine: Children learn that their bodies belong to them, that no one needs pictures of their body, and that they should tell a grown-up if anyone asks. The language is simple and not graphic.
  • 3-5, Game Chat Red Flags: Children learn warning signs when someone in a game or chat asks for secrets, gifts, or to move to another app, and practice saying "no" and telling a trusted adult.
  • 6-8, Grooming Patterns / Pressure Online / Fakes That Hurt: Students learn to recognize when someone online is building trust in unsafe ways, pressuring for pictures, or making fake images, and practice a response plan: STOP, SAVE, TELL, REPORT. The lesson is not graphic.
  • 9-12, Sextortion and Image Abuse / Coercion in Relationships Online: Students learn how threats and pressure can happen online, that it is never the victim's fault, and how to get help and report. The lesson is not graphic.
  • Plus lessons on trafficking and exploitation (6-8 P1, "Work, Pressure and Promises Online"; 9-12 P1, "Recruitment Online"): Students learn the warning signs of a risky online offer or relationship (secrecy, deals that are too good, pressure to go private, debt, someone holding money, phone or papers) and who to tell. Everything is made up. Nobody acts out a victim or a recruiter. The lesson contains no explicit content and no description of what happens to victims.
  • Plus lessons on mental health and compulsive use (6-8 P2, "Scroll Science"; 9-12 P2, "Mind, Mood, and the Scroll"): Students learn what researchers know and do not yet know about social media, sleep, mood and friendships, how to notice signals, and how to try a small reset and tell someone. Students are never asked to share their own screen time or feelings.

What the lesson does not include: graphic images or descriptions, or instructions for harming anyone.

Why we teach it: Children are safer when they have clear words to use, know that telling is safe, and know who to tell. Research on prevention suggests that practicing skills works better than warnings alone [VERIFY].

What support is available

  • Our school counselor, [Name], will be available on the day of the lesson.
  • Children may take a break, draw instead of discuss, or ask to speak with the counselor.
  • Nobody is asked to share personal experiences in front of the class.

Your options

  • Review the materials. You are welcome to preview the lesson. Contact [Teacher] by [date], or visit [time/place].
  • Opt out. If you prefer that your child not take part, complete the attached form. Your child will do a respectful alternative activity, and their grades will not be affected. We will still make sure your child knows who to tell if something worries them.
  • Talk at home. The take-home card will arrive after the lesson with questions you can use.

If you'd like to talk about this, please contact [Teacher name] at [email/phone] or [Counselor name] at [email/phone]. We're happy to answer questions, and we can arrange an interpreter.

Thank you,
[Teacher name], [Title]

4. Conversation starters (10 per grade band)#

A few tips before you start:

  • Pick one question at a time, in a relaxed moment: a walk, a car ride, cooking, bedtime.
  • Listen more than you talk. "Tell me more" does a lot of work.
  • If your child shares a mistake, begin with "Thank you for telling me."
  • You don't need to know the answer, and "Let's figure it out together" is a perfectly good thing to say, even when the question is about an app you've never heard of.
  • No device needed.

K-2 (ages 5-7)#

  1. Who are three grown-ups you could tell if something felt yucky or scary?
  2. What is a secret you should always keep, and a secret you should tell?
  3. Why do we keep our passwords secret, even from friends?
  4. What would you do if a pop-up said you won something? Where in your body would you feel your hackles go up (tummy, neck, hands)?
  5. What is the Oops Button, and what do you do when you press it?
  6. What is something kind you could say online or in a game?
  7. Can you show me how you say "I need help"?
  8. How can you tell if a picture is real or pretend?
  9. What makes your body yours? Who can you tell if someone asks you for a picture?
  10. What is your favorite thing to do when you take a screen break?

3-5 (ages 8-10)#

  1. What are the three rungs of the Help Ladder?
  2. What makes a good, strong passphrase?
  3. Have you seen an offer that seemed too good to be true? What gave it away?
  4. What are some red flags in a game chat? What does it feel like when your hackles go up?
  5. What would you do if someone in a game asked you to move to another app?
  6. What are some ways to be an upstander when someone is being treated unkindly?
  7. How can you tell if a picture, voice, or chatbot is real or made by AI?
  8. What would be a good family code word? When would we use it?
  9. If you clicked something by mistake, what would be your first step?
  10. What is one thing you would want me to know about your online world?

6-8 (ages 11-13)#

  1. What does "Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell." look like for you in a group chat?
  2. If a friend told you a secret that worried you, what would you do? Did your hackles go up?
  3. What are some signs that someone online is not who they say they are?
  4. Which of your accounts would be hardest to lose? How do you protect it?
  5. Have you seen a fake picture or video? How did you find out it was fake?
  6. How do AI chatbots help you, and where are they not good enough?
  7. If someone online pressured you for a picture, what would you do first? Who would you tell?
  8. What would make it easier to tell me about something hard online?
  9. What is one app setting you could check together with me?
  10. How do you know when it is time to take a break from your phone or game?
  11. (Plus lessons) If an online offer asked you to keep it secret, or to hand over something like your ID, who would you tell?
  12. (Plus lessons) How can you tell whether a post is solid or just going around? What do you ask first?

9-12 (ages 14-18)#

  1. What would your digital defense plan look like if you had to protect your most important accounts?
  2. Which accounts have two-step verification or a passkey? Which do not?
  3. What is a pattern you would notice if someone was manipulating you or a friend online? What does your gut say first, and what do you do with it?
  4. What is your plan if someone threatens to share a private picture, real or fake?
  5. How do you decide if something you see online is true?
  6. How do you handle it when a friend is struggling and asks you not to tell?
  7. What do you think about how much data companies have about you?
  8. What does respect and consent look like in online relationships?
  9. What is one thing you would like adults to understand better about your online life?
  10. If I made a mistake in how I reacted to something online, how would you want me to respond?
  11. (Plus lessons) What makes a job offer or a new relationship online feel real and safe, and what would make you stop and ask someone?
  12. (Plus lessons) Which feature of an app pulls on you most, and what setting could we change together?
  13. (Plus lessons) What is the difference between mean and harmful online, and where would you report?

5. Family Tech Agreement template#

This is a conversation, and it is no kind of punishment. A family tech agreement is a set of shared promises that everyone makes together, adults included. It works best when children help write it, when the rules fit your child's age, and when you come back to it often. Write your own, use this one, or skip any part that doesn't suit your family.

Three rules before you begin

  1. The most important promise: "If something goes wrong online, I can tell you, and I will not be punished for telling." Adults and children both sign this one.
  2. Everyone participates. Adults agree to model the habits they ask for.
  3. It can change. Review it every few months, and as your child grows.

6. Family Code Word guide#

What it is. A family code word is a secret word or short phrase that only your family knows. Say someone calls, texts or sends a voice message claiming to be a family member in trouble. You ask for the code word. If they can't say it, you don't act on the request.

Why it helps. Here is the problem: scammers use fake voices (including AI-cloned voices copied from a short audio clip), fake texts and a sense of urgency to push families into sending money or information. The FTC warns about these family emergency scams [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: consumer.ftc.gov]. A code word gives you a calm way to check.

How to set one up (10 minutes at the kitchen table)

  • Step 1: Choose it together. Pick a word or phrase that's easy to remember and hard to guess, which rules out pet names, street names, birthdays and anything that appears on social media. Two random words work well. "Purple kettle."
  • Step 2: Keep it private. Don't post it, text it, or write it on a shared device. Tell only family members and a few trusted adults.
  • Step 3: Agree on when to use it. Examples: a call or message saying someone is in trouble or needs money; an unfamiliar person who says they were sent to pick up a child; any message that says "don't tell anyone."
  • Step 4: Add a callback rule. Even with the code word, hang up and call back on a number you already know. Never use the number the caller gives you.
  • Step 5: Practice. Do one pretend call this week and let your child ask for the code word. Make it a game.
  • Step 6: Change it if it gets out. If the word is shared, picked up by a stranger, or used by mistake, choose a new one.

What to say if you are being pressured. "I'll call you right back." "What is our code word?" "I'm going to check with [family member]." Anyone who is really your family will understand. Teach the whole family the phrase: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. The code word is your "Check." Tell children, too, that a false alarm is welcome. If their hackles go up and it turns out to be nothing, they did exactly the right thing.

Red flags that mean hackles up, so pause

  • Extreme urgency ("Right now!")
  • Secrecy ("Don't tell anyone.")
  • Unusual payment ("Gift cards," "wire money," "crypto," "payment apps")
  • A voice that sounds like family but is on an unknown number

Not just for scams. A code word can also be a safety signal for children. If a child texts the code word, it means "Come get me now, no questions asked." Some families add a second word that means "I need to talk, but I'm not in danger." Decide this together, and promise that when the word is used, the adult responds calmly and without punishment.

Special situations

  • Shared custody or several households: make sure all caregivers know the word.
  • Grandparents and older relatives: emergency scams target them often. Include them in the practice.
  • Young children: keep it simple, and focus on "Never go anywhere with someone who doesn't know our word."

Trust your hackles. If a child's hackles go up at a message or call that claims to be family, the code word is how they check. If a child uses the code word or says "my hackles are up," thank them every time.

9. One-pager: What to do if your child tells you something scary#

If your child tells you something scary online: Your first minutes matter.

Children often wait to tell because they fear being blamed, punished, or losing their device. What you do in the first minutes can decide whether they tell you the rest.

1. Stay calm. Breathe. Keep your voice and face gentle. Your child needs you steady more than they need answers.

2. Say these words.

  • "Thank you for telling me."
  • "I'm glad you told me."
  • "This is not your fault."
  • "You are not in trouble."
  • "We will get through this together."

3. Listen. Let them talk. Ask "Tell me more." Do not interrogate or ask "Why did you...?"

4. Do not blame or punish. Not now. Not for telling. Not for being tricked.

5. If it involves a picture or video of your child

  • Do not look through it, share it, copy it, or forward it. Do not ask your child to show you again.
  • Do not delete the account or messages yet. Keep the account name, links, and dates.
  • Do not pay. Do not send more. Stop replying.
  • Do screenshot text threats, usernames, and profile links if you can do so without capturing images. If unsure, wait for guidance.

6. Report and get help

  • NCMEC CyberTipline: report.cybertip.org or 1-800-843-5678 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: report.cybertip.org]
  • Take It Down (free image removal for people under 18 at the time): takeitdown.ncmec.org [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: takeitdown.ncmec.org]
  • Local law enforcement, or FBI: tips.fbi.gov [VERIFY]
  • The platform: use its report or block tools after evidence is saved.
  • Your child's school counselor or teacher: they can help and support your child.
  • Child abuse hotline (Childhelp): 1-800-422-4453 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: childhelphotline.org]

7. Watch for signs your child is struggling. If your child seems hopeless, says they want to hurt themselves, or cannot stop crying, stay with them and call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), or call 911 in an emergency [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: official sites of the named services]. You can also text HOME to 741741 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: crisistextline.org].

8. Take care of yourself too. You may feel angry, scared, or ashamed. That's normal. Talk with someone you trust.

9. Keep the door open. Check in gently over the next days, and plan the next steps together. Review privacy settings, and think about a family tech agreement later, when things have calmed down.

10. Remember: The person who threatens, tricks, or harms is responsible. Your child is not.

11. Why we say "trust your hackles"#

A parent and caregiver explainer. Copy it into a newsletter, send it home, or read it at a family night.

What are hackles? Hackles are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off. Our program uses the word as a friendly name for that "uh-oh" feeling. Our mascot, Hackle, has orange spikes that stand up when something isn't right.

Why it matters. Children often feel that something is wrong long before they can say why. It might be a message that's too good to be true, a stranger who is oddly pushy, a request to keep a secret, or a picture or link that feels scary. Many of them ignore the feeling because they don't want to cause trouble, or because the person seemed nice. We teach children that the feeling is useful information, and that they can do something about it.

The habit: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell.

  1. Hackles up? Notice the feeling. Something seems odd, too good, pushy, secret, or scary.
  2. Pause. Stop. Do nothing yet. No clicking, replying, paying or sending.
  3. Check. Is this real? Who is it from? What do they want?
  4. Tell. Tell a trusted adult, every time, with no shame.

What "trust your hackles" does not mean.

  • It doesn't mean your child must always be right. A false alarm is fine and welcome. A child who says "my hackles went up, and it was nothing" did the right thing.
  • It doesn't make your child responsible for stopping every trick. If hackles were ignored, or your child was tricked anyway, it is never their fault. Please help them tell someone. Scammers and people who harm children are skilled at making things feel normal, which is exactly why a child can miss the signs and still have done nothing wrong.
  • It is no reason to be afraid. We teach skills. Fear isn't part of it.

How you can help at home

  • Ask: "Have your hackles ever gone up online? What did it feel like?" Then listen before you advise.
  • Use the words yourself: "My hackles went up at that text. Let's pause and check."
  • Stay calm when your child tells you something. The first reaction teaches them whether it's safe to tell you next time, so start with "Thank you for telling me."
  • Try the hand signal together: hand raised and brushed up the back of the neck for Hackles up, then a flat Pause palm, Check eyes, and a Tell wave.

If something serious happens, see the one-pager in Section 9 and contact your child's school.
