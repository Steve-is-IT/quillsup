Consult counsel and policy. Surveys of students can be subject to parental-notice or consent requirements under federal and state law, including the Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment (PPRA) for surveys on certain topics, as well as FERPA and state student-privacy law. Keep these surveys to knowledge, skills and confidence. Do not add questions about a student's own experiences, sex behavior or attitudes, illegal or demeaning behavior, mental or psychological problems, family relationships, religion, politics or income. In its August 26, 2026 letter to states and districts, the US Department of Education said a survey on those eight protected areas given by a teacher, counselor or other school official is treated as required, so the school must let parents review it and get written parental consent first; an opt-out is not enough. Parents also have PPRA rights to inspect surveys and instructional materials, and mental-health screeners fall in the protected area. Rights pass to the student at 18. [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: studentprivacy.ed.gov, SPPO letter of Aug. 26, 2026; law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/20/1232h] Confirm with your administrator and district counsel. 1. Purpose and how this pack works#

What this measures. This pack helps your class and school see whether students are gaining the skills the curriculum teaches: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. and the Help Ladder (and, from grade 6, STOP, SAVE, TELL, REPORT). It focuses on help-seeking, recognition, and confidence, not trivia.

What you get

  • A short pre/post survey for each of the four grade bands (K-2 is oral and picture-based).
  • An answer key and scoring guide.
  • A skill rubric and a role-play observation checklist for performance-based assessment.
  • A class tally sheet.
  • A teacher feedback form and a one-page administrator summary.
  • An honest note on what these results can and cannot show.

Design choices

  • Three core items per band (marked with a star) are the minimum if you are short of time. They measure the central outcome: knowing what to do and being willing to tell.
  • Pre and post surveys are identical so that results can be compared. The post survey adds two feedback questions that are not compared.
  • No student names are needed. Students use a simple code to allow matching (see Section 2).
  • No grades. These are learning check-ins. Tell students plainly that they are not tests and no one is in trouble for any answer.
  • No fear. Scenarios are general and not graphic.

2. Administration guide (all bands)#

Before you start

  • Confirm that your administrator has approved the surveys and any required family notice. [VERIFY]
  • Tell your counselor when you will administer the survey. Some students may react to topics, and some may write something concerning in an open answer. A counselor should be available.
  • Print enough copies. Bring pencils or crayons.
  • Decide how students will create matching codes (below).

Matching codes (so you can compare pre and post without names)
Use one method for your whole class:

  • Method A, student code: First letter of your favorite color + the day of the month you were born (two digits) + the last letter of your street name or pet's name. Example: B-07-S. Keep it private.
  • Method B, teacher-held number: Give each student a number from a roster that only you keep, on a locked or private list. Write the number on a sticker. This is simpler for younger students.
  • If students cannot or do not want to give a code, their survey is still useful for the class tally. They simply do not count in matched analysis.

Read this aloud before the survey
"We are going to answer some questions about staying safe and kind online. This is not a test. There are no grades. Nobody will get in trouble for any answer. Your answers help us make the lessons better. You can skip any question. If anything in the survey makes you feel worried or upset, tell me, or you can talk to [counselor]. Please do not put your name on this paper."

Setting the room

  • Seat students so they cannot see each other's papers, but do not make it feel like a test.
  • Read aloud for students who need it. Reading support, extra time, and translation are always allowed. [VERIFY IEP and 504 accommodations]
  • Do not help students choose answers. You may read items again.

If a student writes something concerning

  • Open-ended answers may contain disclosures. Read them the same day.
  • If a response suggests a student is being harmed, is at risk, or has harmed someone, follow the Disclosure and Incident Guide and your school's policy. Do not wait for the class results.
  • Even anonymous surveys can reveal a student's identity from handwriting or details. Treat concerning content as a disclosure.

After the survey

  • Collect papers and store them securely. Do not leave them in a shared folder or on a personal device.
  • Enter group totals on the Class Tally Sheet and then follow your district's data retention policy. [VERIFY]
  • Share only class-level summaries. Do not share individual surveys.
  • Thank students for their honesty.

3. Pre/post survey: K-2 (picture-based, read aloud)#

How to give it. Hand each child a half-sheet printed with the picture choices below (one row per question). Read each question aloud twice. Children circle a picture. Walk around and quietly help children who need it. Total time about 10 minutes. Items marked with a star are the core three.

Child's code: ______ Circle one: BEFORE (pre) AFTER (post)

Question 1 (star). "Something online felt yucky or scary. Circle the grown-ups you could tell."

  • 👩 A family grown-up
  • 🧑‍🏫 My teacher
  • 🧑‍⚕️ The school counselor
  • 👤 A person I only know from a game
  • 🤐 Nobody

Question 2. "A pop-up says, 'You WON! Click here!' What do you do?"

  • ✋ Stop and ask a grown-up
  • 👆 Click it fast

Question 3. "Who can know your secret password?"

  • 🔒 Just me and my grown-up
  • 🧒 All my friends
  • 🌍 Everyone

Question 4 (star). "Someone in a game asks you to send a picture of yourself. What do you do?"

  • 🗣️ Tell a grown-up
  • 📷 Send it
  • 🤐 Keep it a secret

Question 5 (star). "If you tell a grown-up about something yucky online, will you get in trouble?"

  • 👍 No, I will not be in trouble
  • 👎 Yes, I will be in trouble

Question 6. "Can a computer make a picture look real when it is pretend?"

  • ✅ Yes
  • ❌ No

Question 7 (feelings). "How do you feel about telling a grown-up when something is yucky?"

  • 😀 Okay, I can do it
  • 😐 A little worried
  • 😟 Very worried

Question 8 (hackles, picture-based). "Sometimes you get an 'uh-oh' feeling, like your hackles are going up, when something online is odd. Which face looks like that feeling? What would you do?"

  • 😟 Uh-oh feeling, and I stop and tell a grown-up
  • 😀 No feeling, I just keep going
  • 🤷 I am not sure

Post survey only: feedback (not scored)

  • "Did you like the Hackles lessons?" 😀 😐 😟
  • "What was your favorite part?" (Child draws or tells the teacher, who writes down the words.)

Teacher administration notes

  • Read answer choices in a different order each time so that children do not favor the first or last choice.
  • Children may point to their choice and let you mark it, if circling is hard.
  • Do not show approval or disapproval for any choice.
  • Note: Question 5 is about belief. A child who circles "yes, I will be in trouble" gives you important information. Tell the child gently, "Telling is never trouble," and plan more practice. Alert the counselor if a child seems very worried.

4. Pre/post survey: Grades 3-5#

Code: ______ Circle one: BEFORE AFTER Date: ______

This is not a test. Nobody gets a grade. Please do not write your name. You can skip any question.

1 (star). Someone in a game says, "Let's chat on a different app, and don't tell your parents." What is the BEST first move? (Circle one.)

  • A. Go to the other app, since they seem nice.
  • B. Pause, don't answer, and tell a trusted adult.
  • C. Ask them to prove they are a kid.
  • D. Block them but tell nobody.

2. Which is the strongest password? (Circle one.)

  • A. Fluffy2014
  • B. MySchoolName123
  • C. purple-kettle-jump-river
  • D. password!

3. A message says, "Free 10,000 game coins! Type your password to get them." What should you do? (Circle one.)

  • A. Type the password. It's free!
  • B. Do not answer, and show a trusted adult.
  • C. Send it to your friends so they get coins too.
  • D. Type a fake password.

4 (star). True or false: "If I tell a trusted adult about a mistake I made online, I will probably get in trouble." (Circle one.)

  • True
  • False
  • I'm not sure

5. Write the names or roles of two trusted adults you could tell.

  1. ____ 2. ______

6. A classmate is being picked on in a group chat. Which is an upstander move? (Circle one.)

13. Honest note on evidence limits#

We believe that measuring the program is part of using it responsibly. Please read this section before you present any numbers.

What the research supports (and does not)

  • Reviews of youth internet-safety education (for example, Finkelhor and colleagues, 2021 [VERIFY]) find that skills-based, multi-session programs with practice are more promising than one-time assemblies or fear-based warnings. This curriculum is built on those principles.
  • That evidence base is partly from related fields (such as school-based abuse-prevention education), and it is not a guarantee about any one program.
  • We are not aware of a rigorous trial of a curriculum like this one that shows it reduces real-world sextortion, image abuse, or other harm. [VERIFY: confirm no such trial exists at the time of publication] Anyone who tells you this program will "prevent" these harms is overstating.
  • What you can responsibly say: the program teaches recognition and response skills, and it measures whether students' knowledge, confidence, and willingness to tell changed.

What these surveys can show

  • Whether students' answers moved toward the best responses.
  • Whether students became more confident and more willing to tell.
  • Which lessons and items need more teaching.

What these surveys cannot show

  • Cause. Without a comparison group, a change may be due to the program, to maturing, to other lessons, to news, to being asked the same questions twice, or to chance.
  • Behavior. Surveys capture what students know and say they would do, not what they will do under real pressure.
  • Honest answers. Students may choose the "right" answer because they think it is wanted (social desirability), especially after lessons that stress what to say. Post scores may be inflated for this reason.
  • Long-term effects. A post survey right after the lessons shows short-term learning. Skills fade without review.
  • Small-class precision. With fewer than about 20 matched students, percentage changes can swing a lot from a few students.
  • Ceiling effects. If most students answer well before the program, there is little room to improve.
  • Fewer disclosures does not mean less harm, and more disclosures does not mean more harm. After lessons, disclosures often go up, because students feel safer to tell. This is a sign the program is working. Do not treat an increase in disclosures as a failure.

Good practice

  • Report results as "changes in students' survey answers," not "the program prevented harm."
  • Share the limits paragraph with every summary.
  • Avoid comparing teachers or classes with each other. Use the data to improve teaching.
  • Protect students' privacy.
  • If you want stronger evidence, partner with a university or district research office, and consider a comparison group and a delayed post survey (see Section 14).

14. How to run a simple pre/post analysis#

You do not need statistics software. A pencil, a calculator, and the Class Tally Sheet are enough.

Step 1: Gather#

Collect all pre and post surveys. Check that each is the same version. Score each with the answer key. Exclude students who did not answer an item from that item's tally.

Step 2: Find class percentages for each item#

Percent correct = (number of students with the best answer divided by the number of students who answered) times 100.

Step 3: Calculate the change#

Change in percentage points = post percent minus pre percent.

Worked example (grades 6-8, 24 matched students; invented numbers for illustration only)

Step 3: Calculate the change
Item Pre correct Pre percent Post correct Post percent Change
2 (response steps) 9 of 24 38 percent 19 of 24 79 percent +41 points
5 (confident to tell, 3 or 4) 12 of 24 50 percent 18 of 24 75 percent +25 points
7 (first sentence, score 2) 5 of 24 21 percent 14 of 24 58 percent +37 points

Tell-Readiness (core items): add up all correct core answers across students and divide by the possible total (24 students times 3 items, or 72). If pre was 26 of 72 (36 percent) and post was 51 of 72 (71 percent), the change is +35 percentage points.

Step 4: Check matched pairs#

Using Part D of the tally sheet, count students who went up, stayed the same, or went down.

  • Example: 15 up, 7 same, 2 down. "Most students (15 of 24) improved. A few (2) went down."
  • Look at students who went down or did not move. Offer additional practice.

Step 5: Look at the answer patterns#

  • Which wrong answers were popular on the post survey? These show lingering misunderstandings.
  • On the 9-12 survey, compare the "what would make you hesitate to tell" tallies before and after. If "worry about punishment" stays high, review how your school responds to disclosure.

Step 6: Add a simple reasonableness check#

  • Small classes (under about 20): treat results as informative but not conclusive. Combine classes in your school for the administrator summary.
  • Large changes (more than about 30 points): a big jump may reflect that students learned the "right" answer, which is a good outcome, but check it against your observations and the rubric.
  • No change: this is useful information. It might mean the students already knew the content, the lessons were rushed, or the item was unclear.
  • A drop: can occur by chance. Look at it only if it appears across several classes.

Step 7: Optional improvements to your design#

Step 7: Optional improvements to your design
Improvement How What it adds
Delayed post survey Give the same survey 4 to 8 weeks later Shows what lasted
Comparison group Survey a class that has not yet had the lessons (for example, a class that will start next term) at the same two times Helps rule out changes that would have happened anyway
Combine classes Add up classes in the same band Reduces random swings
Observe skills Use the rubric in a role-play Measures what students can do, not just what they say
Gather multiple years Repeat each year Shows trends and program effects over time
Third-party review Have a university or district evaluator review your approach Improves credibility

Step 8: Report honestly#

Use language like this:

  • Good: "Between September and December, the percentage of 6th graders who could name the correct first steps if threatened rose from 38 percent to 79 percent among 24 matched students."
  • Not good: "The program reduced sextortion at our school."
  • Always include: the number of students, that there was no comparison group, and the limits paragraph.

Step 9: Protect data#

  • Store paper surveys securely and destroy them according to your district policy. [VERIFY retention rules]
  • Report only class-level or school-level results. Do not report results for groups so small that a student could be identified (for example, fewer than 10 students in a subgroup) [VERIFY your district's suppression rule].
  • Do not use survey results to evaluate individual students or teachers unless your administrator and policy approve it.
