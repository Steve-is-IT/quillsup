About Hackles and Competence Collective#

Hackles is a free kit from Competence Collective, a small publisher of practical security-skills resources, and it's free because the habits it teaches (noticing when something feels off, checking, telling a trusted adult) shouldn't sit behind a paywall or an account. Print it. Share it. Adapt it for non-commercial use, with credit.

Your hackles are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off. This kit teaches students to notice that feeling and act on it: Hackles up? · Pause · Check · Tell. Trusting your hackles doesn't mean being right every time. A false alarm is fine and welcome. If the hackles were ignored, or someone was tricked anyway, it is never their fault.

Feedback, corrections and classroom stories are welcome: [CONTACT: to be added].

Hackles is the first step on a path that continues with free resources such as picoCTF and CYBER.ORG. College and professional learners can go on to Competence Collective's Red Herrings (redherrings.app) and Hotwash (hotwa.sh).

No student accounts, no student data collected, ever.

Band 4 of 4. Ten lessons of about 15 minutes each, built around one outcome: every student leaves with a personal, written Digital Defense Plan they actually understand, and with the practiced habit of Hackles up? · Pause · Check · Tell.

Part A: Start Here#

A1. What this band is, and is not#

Teens are not small children. They are not naive, either. Most of them can recite the slogans already ("don't talk to strangers," "don't share your password"), and they also know that adults often have no idea how their online lives really work, which is a fair complaint more often than adults like to admit. This band does three things differently:

  • It treats students as capable security people. They build their own threat model, make their own risk decisions, and teach each other.
  • It teaches recognition and response, and leaves attack methods out. Students see how manipulation, scams and image abuse work in general patterns, so they can spot them and respond. They never learn to break into anything, build a scam, or make a fake.
  • It makes help-seeking the central skill. Research on youth online safety education consistently finds that rehearsed skills (refusing, exiting, saving evidence, reporting, telling a trusted adult) work better than lectures, one-off assemblies and fear [Finkelhor et al. 2021, VERIFY]. Shame is the main reason teens don't tell. So every lesson says, in plain words: it is never your fault, and telling is not getting in trouble.

What isn't here: accounts, logins, student data collection. Every lesson works with paper, a pen and your voice. Optional tech versions are marked. Nothing needs a login.

A2. How each lesson is built (locked template)#

Every lesson has the same 12 parts, so you can teach it cold:

  • Header (title, strand, big idea, time breakdown)
  • Standards (ISTE, CSTA, CASEL; approximate)
  • Materials (low-tech default, optional tech)
  • Hook, 2 minutes, word-for-word script
  • Activity, 8 minutes, step by step for 25-30 students
  • Discuss, 3 minutes, 3-5 questions
  • Takeaway, 2 minutes, one-line link to Pause · Check · Tell. The exit ticket is collected as students leave.
  • Exit ticket, one paper question
  • If a student discloses (boxed)
  • Differentiate (support, extension, low or no internet at home, language access)
  • Family card and reply slip
  • Teacher notes (facts, [VERIFY] flags, questions teens ask)

Time. The core lesson is 15 minutes: Hook 2 + Activity 8 + Discuss 3 + Takeaway/exit ticket 2. Every lesson also lists an Extended-block option (a full 40-50 minute period) for teachers lucky enough to have one. Lesson 10 is a three-part sequence (10A, 10B, optional Hackles Day) and has its own section.

Make It Stick (optional). Each lesson also carries a boxed set of add-ons after the disclosure box: a 2-minute hook variation, a debate or game version, a peer-teaching or choice option, a Level-Up Challenge, a say-it-back line, and a timing note. Pick one or two, because none of them replaces the core lesson or any safeguard. In the sensitive lessons (5 and 7, and the role-play cards in 8) the boxes are deliberately calm: no scoring, no timers, no competition.

Workbook link. Each lesson ends with the student adding one line to their Defense Plan page in the workbook. By Lesson 10 the plan is nearly built. The capstone is assembly and teaching, with nobody starting from a blank page.

Plus lessons. Part B2 adds four optional Plus lessons (P1 to P4) in the same template, for states such as Florida that ask for topics the core ten touch lightly. P1 (trafficking and exploitation) and P2 (mental health and compulsive use) are sensitive and need the review, counselor heads-up and family notice described there. A8 says where they fit, and covers the Florida pathways.

A3. Tone guide: say this, not that#

A3. Tone guide: say this, not that
Instead of Say
"Victim", "stupid mistake", "you should have known" "Target", "this was designed to work on anyone", "it is never your fault"
"Revenge porn", "child porn", "sexting scandal" "Image-based abuse", "child sexual abuse material (CSAM)", "someone shared an image without permission"
"Don't ever share anything online" "Decide what you share, with whom, and what could happen if it travels"
"If you do this, bad things will happen" "Here is what the pattern looks like, and here is exactly what to do"
"Predators are everywhere" "Most pressure starts with someone who seems like a peer. Here are the signals."
"Just tell an adult" "Pick one adult you would actually tell. Here is how to start the sentence."
"Hackers" (for everyone who knows security) "Attackers" for people who cause harm; "security professionals" or "defenders" for the people who protect

Other tone commitments:

  • No shock statistics, no graphic images, no horror stories about real children. Where this guide cites numbers, they are there to say "this is a known, scripted crime and you are not alone," never to scare. The figures in this guide were checked against their primary sources on 2026-10-05 where marked VERIFIED; check any figure again before you quote it, because sources update yearly.
  • Never ask a student to describe their own experience in front of the class. Use fictional cases, and offer private routes (the Question Box, a note, a counselor visit).
  • Be honest about uncertainty. If a student asks something you don't know, say "I do not know; let us find out" and use the sources listed in Part C.
  • Your room holds LGBTQ+ students, students in controlling homes, students who are already targets, and students who have already made a choice they regret. Write and speak as if each of them is listening. Someone is.

A4. Room setup and group agreements#

Before Lesson 1, spend 2 minutes on these five agreements and post them:

  • We talk about patterns and fictional cases, not about named people (in this room or outside it).
  • Nobody has to share anything personal. "Pass" is always allowed.
  • Questions are welcome. There are no embarrassing questions in a security class.
  • What is said here about other people stays here, with one exception: if someone might be in danger or being hurt, I will get help for them, and I will tell you how I will do it. (This is the honest limit on confidentiality. Say it out loud every time.)
  • We do not guess who a case is "really" about.

The Question Box. Keep a closed box or folder, or an anonymous paper-slip routine, ready for every lesson ("Anonymous questions go here"). Answer the box at the start of the next lesson, out loud, with no names guessed at and no eyebrows raised. If a slip suggests a student is in danger, treat it as a disclosure (see A6).

Seating. Pairs and groups of 3-4. Mix the groups, and don't let students choose only close friends for Lessons 5, 7, and 8. For 25-30 students, plan on 8 groups of 3-4 or 14 pairs.

A5. Pause · Check · Tell, adapted for teens#

The recurring routine in every band. For teens it's a decision habit, and no longer a rhyme. It starts with noticing.

Hackles up? This is the noticing step. For teens, frame it as trusting your instincts and threat-modeling the feeling: when something feels off (too good, pushy, secret, or scary), that gut signal is data. Ask what it's picking up on, then run Pause, Check, Tell. A false alarm is fine and costs little, and if a student ignored the feeling, or was tricked anyway, it is never their fault and the answer is still to tell someone. Keep the tone plain and respectful, the way you'd talk to a colleague you like. This band has no mascot dialogue; Hackle, the kit's mascot, appears at most as a small corner icon. The optional hand signal is a hand brushed up the back of the neck.

A5. Pause · Check · Tell, adapted for teens
Step What it means for a teen The sentence to practice
Hackles up? Notice the feeling that something is off: hurry, flattery, fear, secrecy, a "special" offer, or just a gut sense. Name it, and ask what it might be picking up on. "My hackles are up. Why?"
Pause Notice the pull: hurry, flattery, fear, secrecy, a "special" offer. Take one beat before anything you cannot undo (paying, sending, clicking, logging in, deleting, replying). "Nothing here has to happen in the next five minutes."
Check Verify using a second channel you chose yourself: call the known number, open the official app directly, ask the person face to face, trace the original source. Ask: who gains if I act right now? "I will confirm this a different way."
Tell Bring in a person. The Help Ladder: a friend you trust, then an adult you trust (family, counselor, coach, teacher), then a formal report (platform, school, law enforcement, NCMEC, a hotline). You can start at any rung, and you can skip up a rung. "Something is going on and I need help figuring out what to do. Can I show you?"

Response Drill (images and threats): STOP · SAVE · TELL · REPORT. Introduced in Lesson 5, then reused in Lessons 6, 7, and 8.

  • STOP replying and paying. Do not send more. Do not delete the account or conversation.
  • SAVE details (usernames, links, threats, dates, payment demands). Do not copy, forward, or re-send any nude image of a minor, not even as "proof."
  • TELL a trusted person.
  • REPORT on the platform, to NCMEC (Take It Down and the CyberTipline), and with an adult to law enforcement.

Two-pass practice. Teens decide alone and fast, so each lesson asks them to say the step out loud to a partner at least once, because a skill that has been said aloud is a skill that's easier to find at 1 a.m.

A6. If a student discloses: read this before Lesson 1#

Disclosures are expected in a class like this, most of all in Lessons 5, 7, and 8. A student telling you is a success. It is not a problem.

In the moment

  • Stay calm. Move to a private place if you can do so safely, in view of others if school policy requires it.
  • Listen. Do not interrogate. Open-ended prompts only: "Tell me more." "What happened next?" Ask only what you need to know in order to get help.
  • Believe and reassure: "Thank you for telling me." "This is not your fault." "You are not in trouble."
  • Be honest: "I can't keep this just between us, because I need to get you help. Here is exactly what I will do next."
  • Do not promise secrecy. Do not promise outcomes you cannot control.

Never

  • View, open, copy, forward, or screenshot suspected child sexual abuse imagery. Doing so can itself be a crime and re-harms the student. Do not ask the student to show you the images. If a device holds them, secure it per school policy and hand it to administration or law enforcement. [VERIFY with district counsel for your state.]
  • Question the student's choices ("Why did you send it?") or react with shock or disgust.
  • Contact the other person, the offender, or the offender's family yourself.
  • Tell other students or discuss the case in the staff room.

Report

  • You are a mandated reporter in essentially every US state [Confirm your state's statute and who is covered with district counsel]. In many states the duty is personal (telling the principal may not discharge it; check your state at childwelfare.gov) and the timeframe varies by state [VERIFY]. Report to child protective services or law enforcement as required, and follow district policy.
  • If the matter is online exploitation (sextortion, enticement, CSAM, AI-generated sexual images of a minor), also report to NCMEC's CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org, 1-800-843-5678) per policy and with administration [CyberTipline VERIFIED 2026-10-05: report.cybertip.org; routing through your school follows your policy]. Your school may handle this through its counselor, administrator, or school resource officer. Know the route before the lesson.
  • Florida routes (use if your school is in Florida). Florida Abuse Hotline 1-800-962-2873 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: myflfamilies.com]; Florida Human Trafficking Hotline 1-855-FLA-SAFE (1-855-352-7233) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: fdle.state.fl.us]; National Human Trafficking Hotline 1-888-373-7888 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: humantraffickinghotline.org/en/contact]. Any person who knows or has reasonable cause to suspect child abuse must report immediately to the central abuse hotline, and school personnel who report must give their name [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: s. 39.201(1), leg.state.fl.us]. A school resource officer or school safety officer, if your school has one, is one of the trusted adults a student may choose; any adult who hears a disclosure still follows the reporting duty and district policy.
  • Evidence in three cases. (1) Words and records (comments, threats, usernames, links, dates, scam messages): screenshots and saved copies are fine; keep them private and never post or forward them. (2) Intimate or sexual pictures of anyone under 18, including fakes: do not screenshot, save, forward or show anyone, not even as proof; save only the surrounding words; tell a trusted adult.   • Follow up privately in the next day or two. Protect the student from gossip and retaliation.
  • Look after yourself. Hearing a disclosure is heavy. Use your counselor, EAP, or a trusted colleague, without sharing identifying details.

A10. Glossary for teachers (use sparingly with students)#

  • Threat model: a plain answer to "what am I protecting, from whom or what, how bad would it be, and what will I do about it?"
  • Authentication factor: something you know (password), have (phone, key), or are (fingerprint, face).
  • Passkey: a login credential stored on your device that replaces the password; it uses a private key that never leaves your device and is tied to the real site, so it is much harder to phish [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: fidoalliance.org: passkeys are phishing-resistant; details of sync and recovery vary by platform].
  • Social engineering: manipulating a person (rather than breaking a system) to give up access, money, or information.
  • Data broker: a company that collects and sells personal data it did not collect directly from you.
  • Synthetic media / deepfake: image, audio, or video created or altered with AI.
  • Provenance: the origin and edit history of a piece of media.
  • Sextortion: blackmail using real or fabricated intimate images (or threats of them). Most reported youth cases are financially motivated and run from fake accounts [NCMEC, FBI; VERIFY current figures].
  • NCMEC: the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children. Runs the CyberTipline and the Take It Down service.
  • CFAA: the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, a US federal law about unauthorized computer access; states have their own laws too [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1030].
  • Coordinated (responsible) disclosure: reporting a security flaw to the owner privately so it can be fixed before details go public.

Hook (2 minutes)#

Say, word for word:

"Raise a hand if you have ever locked your phone, locked a bike, or chosen who gets to see a story. Okay, that is security. You already do it. Nobody locks everything the same way. You lock your bike more carefully than your water bottle, and you tell different people different things. Today we make that instinct deliberate. Security professionals call it a threat model, and it is just four questions: What do I have that matters? What could go wrong? How bad and how likely? What is the smallest thing I can do about it? Not 'be paranoid.' Be specific."

Write the four questions on the board: Matters? Wrong? How bad / how likely? Smallest fix?

Activity (8 minutes): Assets, Threats, Protections sort#

Groups of 3-4 (about 8 groups for 28 students). Each group has one set of R1 cards.

  • Step 1 (1 min): Pick five assets. Each student privately circles five things that matter to them on W1 (the Asset list is also on R1: main email, social account, photos and videos, location and routine, money and game items, school work and records, reputation, friendships and group chats, phone and laptop, health and personal information, creative work, AI chat history). Say: "Pick what matters to you, not what you think should matter."
  • Step 2 (3 min): Group threat sort. The group spreads out the Threat Cards (account takeover, scam message, lost or stolen device, oversharing friend, data broker or leaked data, someone you know who has your password, AI fake or impersonation, pressure or blackmail, platform data breach, location leak). Together they place each card on the 2x2 grid on W1 by likelihood and harm. Disagreement is expected. Say: "There is no answer key. Defend your placement in one sentence."
  • Step 3 (3 min): Protections. Each student picks the top two threats for their own life and writes the smallest protection for each in the "Smallest fix" column on W1 (examples: a unique password and a passkey for email; turn off precise location for two apps; agree on a callback rule with family; ask a friend before posting a photo of them). Remind them that a good protection fits your actual life: a teen who shares a family phone has different priorities from a teen with a public creator account.
  • Step 4 (1 min): One-line share. Each group states aloud one threat they placed in the "likely and big harm" corner and one protection. Teacher points out patterns: many groups will land on the same two or three, and different students will disagree about others. That is the point.

Tip. Circulate and listen. If a group is all on "account takeover" fine. If a student's card choice sounds like a real situation ("someone has my password"), note it quietly for a private check-in and see the disclosure box.

Discuss (3 minutes)#

  • Q1. Which threat did your group argue about most? What changed someone's mind?
  • Q2. Why might two people in this room have totally different top threats?
  • Q3. What is a protection that costs nothing but time? What is one that costs money, and is it worth it?
  • Q4. When is "do nothing" a reasonable answer to a threat?
  • Q5. Who in your life could help you with a protection you cannot do alone?

Takeaway (2 minutes)#

Say: "Security is not about fearing everything. It is about knowing what to Pause over, what to Check, and who to Tell. Your threat model tells you where to aim those three. Write one line on your Defense Plan page: 'The thing I most want to protect is _, and my first move is _.'"

Hackles up? Your threat model is a map of where your hackles should go up. Name one situation where yours already would.

Exit ticket#

On a slip: "Name one asset you decided to protect first, and one thing you will do about it this week." (Collect as students leave. No names needed; use for a quick tally of top assets and protections.)

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (Lesson 1)

  • Why it might come up: A student may reveal that someone they know has their password, is tracking their phone, or is threatening them, usually during the "someone you know" or "pressure" threat cards.
  • Say: "Thank you for telling me. That is not your fault, and you are not in trouble. I am glad you said it. Let us find someone who can help with this today."
  • Don't say: "Why did you give them your password?" "That was risky." "Just block them." Do not promise to keep it secret. Do not ask to see messages or images.
  • Escalate when: a student says someone is threatening, blackmailing, stalking, hurting, or pressuring them; they mention self-harm; an adult or someone with power over them is involved; images are involved. Follow the district reporting route that day.

Report suspected abuse or exploitation personally, to CPS or law enforcement as required, and per school policy; telling the principal is usually not enough. "Quick poll, hands up, and nobody has to say anything out loud: who reuses one password, or a close variant of it, across more than one account? Keep it up if you did that on an account that matters, like email. Okay, that is almost everyone, and it is not a character flaw. It is the system being hard. Here is why it matters. When one site gets breached, the stolen list of emails and passwords is tried on other sites automatically. The attacker does not need to guess you. They just try the list. So the real risk is not 'a smart hacker targets me.' It is 'my one password was already sitting on a list.' The fix is boring and strong: unique and long, and where possible, no password at all."

Activity (8 minutes): Passphrase Builder and Recovery Map#

Pairs (14 pairs for 28 students).

  • Step 1 (3 min): Build a passphrase the random way. On R2, the word table has 36 words; a die roll picks the row and a second roll picks the column. Each pair rolls the die twice per word, four words total (for example roll 3 then 5 = the word in row 3, column 5). They write the four words. Say: "That phrase was chosen by dice, not by your brain, so it is unpredictable. Real systems use bigger lists of 7,776 words [VERIFY], but the idea is the same." Students then compare to a typical "clever" password ("Fluffy2009!") and discuss which one an automated guesser would try first. Do not use the phrase you made as a real password. Say so explicitly. It is for practice and has been seen by others.
  • Step 2 (3 min): Recovery Map. On the Recovery Map card, each student draws their own (fictional or real, but with no account names or passwords written) "master key" chain: Which account can reset the most others? (Usually email, then phone number.) Draw an arrow from each account to its recovery method (email, phone, backup code, another account). Ask them to circle the single point of failure and star the account that needs the strongest protection. Say: "If I own your email, I can reset almost everything. So email gets the longest passphrase and the strongest 2-step. This is the highest-value fix in this lesson."
  • Step 3 (2 min): Rank the doors. Show or write these five on the board and have pairs rank them from weakest to strongest: (a) password only; (b) password plus a code sent by text; (c) password plus a code from an authenticator app; (d) a passkey or a physical security key; (e) a password you also use on three other sites plus a code by text. Reveal: roughly, (e) weakest, then (a), (b), (c), (d) strongest, with the caveat that texted codes are better than nothing but can be intercepted or phished more easily [VERIFY current guidance, e.g., CISA and NIST]. Add: "Never read a code to anyone who contacts you. A real service never asks for it."

Passkeys in one minute (teacher explanation, say it aloud): "A passkey is like a lock-and-key pair. The site holds the lock. Your device holds the key, and the key never leaves your device and works only for the real site. So a fake page cannot trick you into handing over something useful. You approve with your fingerprint, face, or device PIN. It is harder to phish than a password. The tradeoffs: you need a recovery plan if you lose the device, and not every site supports them yet [adoption varies by service; no figure is claimed]."

Discuss (3 minutes)#

  • Q1. Why do people reuse passwords even though they know it is risky? What would make not reusing easier?
  • Q2. If you could only fix one account today, which one would it be and why?
  • Q3. Where does a password manager help, and where might it be a bad fit (a shared family device, an account you cannot recover)?
  • Q4. Someone gets your phone. What would they be able to reach? What would you change?
  • Q5. Who might legitimately need to access your accounts (a parent for a young sibling, a school, a shared account), and how is that different from someone pressuring you for a password?

Takeaway (2 minutes)#

Say: "Pause before you type a password or a code anywhere you reached by a link. Check by going to the site or app yourself. Tell a trusted adult if someone asks for your password, even someone you like. Add to your Defense Plan: 'My strongest-protected account will be _, and I will use _ (passkey, authenticator, or passphrase) on it.'"

Hackles up? A message that pushes you to log in or share a code is a classic trigger. Trust the feeling, then go to the site yourself.

The HOOKS (post and hand out):

  • Hurry: a deadline or countdown designed to stop you thinking
  • Official: borrowed authority or trust ("support", "your bank", "your friend", "school IT")
  • Offer or outrage: a too-good reward, or a scary threat
  • Keep it quiet: secrecy, "don't tell anyone", isolation
  • Strange method: gift cards, crypto, reading out a code, moving to another app, remote access, paying a stranger you cannot verify

Hook (2 minutes)#

Say:

"A magician does not need to hack your eyes. They steer your attention. Social engineering is the same trick: instead of breaking a lock, the attacker persuades someone to open it. Here is the thing nobody tells you. It works on smart people, because it is designed to work on smart people who are busy, tired, or caring. A good script triggers emotions: fear, hope, loyalty, urgency. So we are not going to ask 'who is dumb enough to fall for this.' Nobody is. We are going to ask 'what is the pattern, and what is the one move that breaks it?' Today you are the analysts. Every case is made up, and none of them teaches how to do this. They teach how to see it."

Pass out the HOOKS card.

Activity (8 minutes): Case Lab#

Groups of 3-4. Each group gets two Case Cards from R3 (C1-C8) and a Case Lab Sheet (W3).

  • Step 1 (1 min): Orient. Review HOOKS on the board in 30 seconds. Say: "For each case, answer four things on the sheet: (1) Which HOOKS are present? (2) What is the ask, the actual thing they want? (3) What is the Check, the second-channel way to confirm? (4) If someone already did it, what are the first two moves?"
  • Step 2 (4 min): Analyze. Groups work. For the Check column, steer toward actions the student controls: call the number on the back of the card; open the app yourself, do not use the link; ask the person in a way they would have to know (a callback on a number you already have, a family code word); search the official site for the policy; ask a trusted adult. For the "already did it" column, use the Fix It moves: Lock (change the password from a safe device, sign out other sessions, turn on 2-step); Tell (a trusted adult, so you do not carry it alone); Report (the platform, your bank or card issuer, and reportfraud.ftc.gov; identitytheft.gov if identity details were shared) [VERIFY URLs]. Say: "Telling is the fastest fix. Speed matters, and shame slows people down."
  • Step 3 (3 min): Share-out. Two or three groups each share one case in 30 seconds: "Our case was . The HOOKS were . Our Check was ___." Ask the class: "Which hook showed up the most?" Tally on the board. (Expect Hurry and Strange method to dominate.) Ask: "What do all the Checks have in common?" (They leave the attacker's channel and go to a channel you chose.)
  • Teacher note on cases: Case C3 (the "easy money" job) is the money-mule pattern: a stranger asks you to receive and move money. Teach recognition and the legal point only (it can be a crime to move stolen money even if you were told it was a job, and teens are recruited this way [VERIFY with FTC and FBI public guidance]). Do not describe how laundering works.

Discuss (3 minutes)#

  • Q1. Which hook worked best on you, personally, in real life? You do not need to say a case. Just name the hook.
  • Q2. Why does "Keep it quiet" make every other hook stronger?
  • Q3. What is the difference between being careful and being paranoid? How would you tell a real friend asking for help from a script?
  • Q4. If you did fall for one, what would stop you from telling someone? How can we make that easier?
  • Q5. Which Check would be hardest for you to do in the moment? What would help?

Takeaway (2 minutes)#

Say: "Every case had a Pause moment: the second you felt hurried, flattered, or scared. Your best move is to Check in a channel you chose, and to Tell someone, especially when told not to. Add to your Defense Plan: 'My go-to Check is ____.'"

Hackles up? In each case, the first sign was a feeling: hurried, flattered, or scared. Learn to catch that feeling before you decide.

Activity (8 minutes): The Response Drill#

Pairs for steps 1-2; triads or pairs for step 3.

  • Step 1 (2 min): Read the pattern. Hand out the Scenario Card (R5). Read it aloud, or have a student read it (ask for a volunteer, never assign). The card is fictional and has no explicit content. It follows "Rowan, 16" who is contacted by "Riley," who appears to be the same age. Ask students to underline the pattern markers: fast closeness, flattery, a push to move to a different private app, "you first" or "I'll send one if you do", sudden change of tone, a deadline, a payment demand in gift cards or similar, and a threat to share with followers or school, plus "do not tell anyone." I am scared, and I do not want to be blamed." Student B (as the trusted person) responds with: "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. We will figure it out together." Swap. Keep discussion fictional and general. If a student starts to speak about their own experience, gently redirect: "Thank you. Let us talk after class. I want to give this the time it deserves."

Takeaway (2 minutes)#

Say: "Pause the moment the speed, flattery, or secrecy begins. Check whether this person is who they say they are, and notice that you cannot. Tell, tonight. The drill is Stop, Save, Tell, Report. If you remember one thing: telling is the way out, not the way in trouble. Add to your Defense Plan: 'The one adult I would tell first is _, and my first sentence is _.'"

Hackles up? Speed, flattery, and secrecy are exactly what hackles are for. Trusting them is enough reason to stop and tell, tonight; being unsure it is "serious enough" is not a reason to wait.

Lesson 6: Synthetic Media and Truth#

Big idea: Images, voices, and videos can now be made or altered by AI. "Looks real" is not proof, and "can't prove it's fake" is not proof it is real. The habit that works is verifying the source, not hunting for glitches.

Hook (2 minutes)#

Say:

"Last night, someone sent you a voice message that sounds exactly like your cousin. They are in trouble and need money. Or a video of a famous person endorsing a giveaway. Or an image of a classmate that you know, in your gut, cannot be real. Two years ago, 'look for the weird hands' was decent advice. Now it is bad advice. The glitches are going away, and detectors are unreliable. So the new skill is not 'spot the fake.' It is 'verify the source.' Today you will practice four moves that work whether the fake is good or terrible. And I want you to hold on to a second idea. When we cannot prove something is fake, that does not mean it is real. We will practice sitting in 'I do not know yet.'"

Activity (8 minutes): Verify Lab#

Groups of 3-4; each gets a set of nine Claim Cards (R6), or divide the nine among groups (two or three cards per group).

  • Step 1 (1 min): Explain the four moves. Say: "Pause: before you share or react, give it a beat; strong emotion is a signal. Source: who posted it first, and can I find them? Second source: does an independent, reliable outlet or person confirm it? Trace: can I find the original, and is it the same?" (This follows lateral reading and the SIFT approach of Mike Caulfield [VERIFY attribution].) Add one more move for claims that are harmful or false and spreading: Report it in the app, and tell an adult (the state guide says students should be able to report misinformation on a platform, p.7 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: FLDOE Student's Guide to Internet Safety, May 2025 PDF]). For a chart or infographic, add the data check: who or what is it about, when and by whom was it collected, one source or several, and where does the scale start [guide p.8, same source]. [edit P-10]
  • Step 2 (4 min): Sort the claims. For each Claim Card (for example: "an audio clip of a school official announcing a cancellation", "a video of a public figure promoting a crypto giveaway", "a photo of a protest with a surprising banner", "a voicemail from a relative asking for money", "a screenshot of a classmate's 'private message'", "an image of a classmate that looks like a nude", "a chart with an unlabeled axis and a source of 'a survey'"), groups decide: (a) Which of the four moves would you use first? (b) What would count as good evidence? (c) What is your confidence: Probably real, Probably fake, or Do not know yet? (d) What action do you take now: share, do not share, report, or tell?
  • Step 3 (2 min): Special case. Point at the two special cards (V7, a voice-cloned family emergency call; V8, a sexual fake of a classmate). Say: "If the media shows someone being sexualized or humiliated, the question is not 'is it real?' It is 'this is harmful whether or not it is real.' Do not forward it. Do not 'investigate' by passing it to friends. Use the Response Drill from Lesson 5: Stop, Save details (not the image), Tell, Report. If it is a voice or video emergency about a person you know, call them back on a number you already have, or use your family code word."
  • Step 4 (1 min): Say what you know. One group says: "We said 'do not know yet' about , and that was the right call because ."

Discuss (3 minutes)#

  • Q1. Which is more dangerous: a fake that fools people, or the real thing that people claim is fake? Why?
  • Q2. If AI detectors are unreliable, what else can you rely on?
  • Q3. Where do AI chatbots fit? When an AI tells you a "fact", what is your Check?
  • Q4. Why does a fake that makes you angry spread faster? What could slow you down?
  • Q5. What do we owe a person who is the subject of a harmful fake? What do we owe ourselves?

Takeaway (2 minutes)#

Say: "Pause on emotion. Check the source and a second source. Tell a trusted adult, and report, when the fake is meant to hurt a person. Add to your Defense Plan: 'My verification habit is _, and my family code word or callback rule is _.'"

Hackles up? A strong emotional reaction to a clip, voice, or message is a trigger to verify before you react or share.

Exit ticket#

"A friend sends you a shocking clip. Write the first two things you do before reacting or sharing."

If a student discloses (Lesson 6)

Hook (2 minutes)#

Say:

"Think about the people who matter most to you: a partner, a close friend, a family member. Now think about what trust looks like: you can say no, you can have privacy, you can say 'I am busy' without a fight. Sometimes control sneaks in disguised as care: 'I just worry about you,' 'If you trusted me you would show me,' 'Everyone shares their location.' Online tools make control easy: checking your phone, tracking your location, demanding replies, going through who you talk to. Today we are going to practice telling the difference between caring and controlling, for any kind of relationship, and what to do when it is the second one. We are going to use made-up statements. No one needs to say anything about their own life. And I want to say one thing clearly: if you are ever in a situation like this, it is not your fault, and there are people who can help."

Write on the board: Consent is freely given, specific, ongoing, and can be withdrawn. Pressure, threats, and guilt are not consent.

Activity (8 minutes): The Line-Up#

Whole class. Each student has a green, yellow, and red card (or a half-sheet for G, Y, R). Votes are private until "three": students hold up one card on the count of three. (If a class is uncomfortable, have students write G, Y, or R on paper, collect, and tally.)

  • Step 1 (1 min): Set the code. Say: "Green means this is healthy. Yellow means watch closely or it depends. Red means this is not okay: it is control or coercion. We are talking about statements, not people."
  • Step 2 (4 min): Six statements, 40 seconds each. Read a statement. Count to three. Students hold up a card. Ask one volunteer for a reason ("Why red?"), then reveal the teacher view. Use statements from R7. Recommended six:
    • S1. "We share locations because we both chose to, and either of us can turn it off any time without a fight." (Green. Choice and an easy exit are key.)
    • S2. "They check my phone every night and say it is because they love me." (Red. Monitoring is control, regardless of the reason.)
    • S3. "We swapped passwords to show trust." (Yellow. Trust is not proven by access. Can you say no? Can you change it without a fight?)
    • S4. "They said if I do not send a photo they will leave me and tell people I am boring." (Red. Pressure, guilt, and threats are not consent.)
    • S5. "I asked to delete a photo of me and they did right away." (Green. Respecting the request.)
    • S6. "They posted a private chat in the group to embarrass me." (Red. Sharing private messages or images to humiliate is abuse.)
    • Optional extras if time: S7. "My friend gets angry if I do not reply within minutes and says my other friends are 'a problem.'" (Red or yellow: isolation.) S8. "A relative checks my location, and we talked about it and agreed on when." (Yellow: depends on transparency and agreement.)
  • Step 3 (2 min): The signs of digital control. Say: "Here are things to notice. Constant checking and demands for replies. Going through your phone or accounts. Demanding passwords or locations. Controlling who you can talk to or follow. Pressure for images. Threatening to share a private message or photo. Impersonating you or making fake accounts to watch you. Guilt: 'If you loved me.' If you see a couple of these, that is a signal to pause and tell someone." Display the Help Map (R7): Help Ladder; loveisrespect (call 1-866-331-9474, text LOVEIS to 22522, chat at loveisrespect.org) [VERIFY]; 988; Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: crisistextline.org]; the school counselor and Title IX coordinator [VERIFY]; the Take It Down and NCMEC route if images are involved (Lesson 5); 911 for immediate danger.
  • Step 4 (1 min): The boundary sentence and device safety. Say: "Practice one boundary sentence out loud with a neighbor: 'I'm not comfortable with that. I'm saying no.' or 'I need privacy on my phone. That is not about you.' If you ever suspect someone is monitoring your device, do not confront them first. Use a device they cannot see, such as a school or library device or a friend's, to look for help. Clearing your history can sometimes alert a monitoring person, so talk to a counselor about a safe way." [VERIFY with loveisrespect or NNEDV safety-planning guidance.]

  • Q1. Why can controlling behavior feel like love at first? What is the tell?
  • Q2. If a friend is going through this, what is one thing a good friend says and one thing they avoid?
  • Q3. Why might it be hard for someone to say no, even when they want to? What makes it easier?
  • Q4. Does this apply outside of romantic relationships (friends, family, teams)? How does it look the same, and how is it different?
  • Q5. Where do you draw the line on "sharing location" or "sharing passwords" and why?

Takeaway (2 minutes)#

Say: "Pause when love or loyalty is used as a reason to give up your privacy or choice. Check whether you can say no and it stays safe. Tell a friend, a trusted adult, or a hotline. You do not need to be sure it is 'bad enough'; you can ask for help before it is. Add to your Defense Plan: 'My boundary sentence is _, and the person or place I would Tell is _.'"

Hackles up? If a request about your privacy or your choices leaves you uneasy, that unease counts. You do not need proof to ask for help.

If a student discloses (Lesson 7)

Hook (2 minutes)#

Say:

Activity (8 minutes): Helper Role-Play#

Triads (about 9 triads for 27 students). Roles: Friend (reads a Friend-Message Card, then improvises a few lines in character), Helper (uses Hear · Help · Hand off), Observer (uses the Observer Card checklist).

  • Step 1 (2 min): Teach the model. Present the Helper Card:

  • Permission: Do I have clear, written permission from the owner? (My own device and accounts count; someone else's do not.)

Hook (2 minutes)#

Say:

"A locksmith who opens your door because you locked yourself out, and a person who opens your door because they felt like it, can use the same skills. What separates them is not skill. It is permission. In security, that is the entire ethical line. Security professionals get paid to look for weaknesses, but only with written permission and inside a defined scope. Without permission, even a curious, harmless, 'I was just checking' attempt can be illegal, and teens have faced real consequences. Today is about three things. What the law says in plain terms. What to do if you accidentally find a flaw. And what a career or a next step looks like, because security needs people, and there are free ways to start that are legal and fun. We will not learn how to break into anything today. We will learn how to be the person who gets hired, not the person who gets in trouble."

Lesson 10: Capstone, My Defense Plan#

Grade band: 9-12 | Strand: All (A-F)
Big idea: You now have what a security professional uses: a threat model, habits, and a plan. Turn what you learned into a short personal plan you will actually use, and teach one part to someone else. Teaching is the best way to make it stick.
Practiced skill: Assemble a personal Digital Defense Plan; teach back one defense; plan the next 90 days.

How this lesson works. The capstone is a short sequence, not a single 15-minute session:

  • Part 10A, Build (15 minutes): assemble the plan.
  • Part 10B, Teach-Back (15 minutes): teach one defense to a peer; self and peer reflection; teacher assessment.
  • Optional: Hackles Day showcase (60-90 minutes, once a year): a student-led event for the school and families.

Each part uses the standard 15-minute template (Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway and exit ticket 2).

Part 10A: Build My Plan (15 minutes)#

Hook (2 min). Say:

"For nine lessons, you have been collecting pieces: your threat model, your master-key account, your go-to Check, your location rule, your adult to Tell, your verification habit, your boundary sentence, your helper plan, and your permission rule. Today those pieces become one plan on two pages. A good plan has three features: it is short enough to remember, specific enough to do this week, and personal enough that it fits your life. Nobody will grade your private choices. You will teach one defense to someone else next session, and that is what gets looked at."

Activity (8 min): Assemble the plan.

  • Step 1 (1 min): Hand out W10 and ask students to lay out their nine Defense Plan lines.
    • Hackles up? Add one line on W10: "My hackles triggers", the situations (a rush, flattery, a secret, a threat) where your instincts say Pause, Check, Tell. Pull from the moments you noticed across the lessons.
  • Step 2 (5 min): Complete the W10 sections silently:
    • My top three assets (from DP-1). My top three threats.
    • My Lock It moves (DP-2 and DP-4): master-key account protection; one location or settings change.
    • My Spot It moves (DP-3 and DP-6): my go-to Check; my verification habit; my family code word or callback rule.
    • My Safe People moves (DP-5 and DP-7): my Response Drill (STOP · SAVE · TELL · REPORT, written in my own words); the person or place I would Tell first; my boundary sentence.
    • My Helper plan (DP-8): my Hand off adult and sentence.
    • My well-being setting (optional line, edit P-7; from Plus Lesson P2 if you taught it): one app habit or setting I will adjust for sleep, mood or focus.
    • My Permission rule and next step (DP-9).
    • My Help Ladder: write roles (not names if private): a friend, a trusted adult at home, a trusted adult at school, an outside resource (hotline, NCMEC), and the platform.
    • Do this week / Do this month / Review on: pick three concrete actions and set a 90-day review date.
  • Step 3 (2 min): Choose a Teach-Back topic from Lessons 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, or 9, or the general Response Drill. Fill out the Teach Card in R10: Pattern (what it looks like), Pause (what to notice), Check (what to do), Tell (who to bring in), and one example. Students use only general or fictional examples, never real people or incidents.

Discuss (3 min).

  • Q1. Which piece of your plan was easiest to write? Which was hardest, and why?
  • Q2. What is the one change in your plan that you believe will matter most?
  • Q3. What would make you not follow your plan? What could help?
  • Q4. Whose plan is different from yours, and why?

Takeaway (2 min). Say: "A plan is not a promise to be perfect. It is a way to make good choices easier. Pause to notice the pull, Check with a second channel, Tell a person. Put the plan somewhere you will see it (a phone note without passwords, a card in a wallet) and tell one person that you made it."

Exit ticket (10A): "Write one action you will take this week and the date you will review your plan."

Part 10B: Teach-Back (15 minutes)#

Hook (2 min). Say:

"The best test of whether you understand a defense is whether you can explain it to someone else in 90 seconds, without scaring them or blaming them. You are about to be both the teacher and the learner. As a teacher: be clear, be kind, give one thing they can do. As a learner: ask one question and give one piece of feedback using the form. We teach patterns and defenses, never attack methods, and never real people."

Activity (8 min). Two rounds of teach-back.

  • Step 1 (1 min): Pair students with someone they have not partnered with before. Give each the Peer Feedback Form (R10). Remind: 90 seconds to teach, 1 minute for feedback and a question.
  • Step 2 (3 min): Round 1. Student A teaches their Teach Card to Student B using the structure Pattern, Pause, Check, Tell, and one doable step. Student B completes the form (Was it clear? Respectful and shame-free? Doable? One thing to add). Then they talk for 30 seconds.
  • Step 3 (3 min): Round 2. Swap roles.
  • Step 4 (1 min): Students hand in the Teach Card and the Peer Feedback Form. The teacher uses the Capstone Rubric (R10) on Teach Cards and a quick listen-in. If time is short, observe 5-8 pairs per session and rotate.

Discuss (3 min).

  • Q1. What made a teach-back clear? What made it hard to follow?
  • Q2. What did you learn from teaching that you did not know when you only listened?
  • Q3. Which defense will you tell your family or a younger sibling about?
  • Q4. What would you tell a younger student starting 9th grade?

Takeaway and exit ticket (2 min). Say: "You are now someone others can ask for help in this. Pause, Check, Tell: and if someone needs help, Hear, Help, Hand off. Your plan lives on, so review it in 90 days." Exit ticket (10B, a short written reflection): "What is one thing you now do differently, and what is one thing you would tell a friend?"

If a student discloses

Lesson P4 (Plus): From Mean to Harmful: Recognize, Respond, Report#

Educator review before use. Counsel should confirm the evidence wording in the teacher notes (Crosswalk Section 10, D1) before the lesson goes to families. [COUNSEL TO CONFIRM]

Grade band: 9-12 (ages 14-18) | Strand: E, Kind & Well (with D, Safe People)
Statute component: C5b: identify cyberbullying on the Internet (with C6 reporting)
Big idea: Cyberbullying is bullying through technology. Four questions help tell mean from harmful: Repeated? Hurtful? Public or hidden? Power? Respond in ways that protect you and the target, and report in the app and at school. Evidence is for adults who can help, not for spreading.

Materials#

RP4 Situation Cards (eight, fictional; one set per pair), the Four-Question Strip (also on the board), the Response Menu, the Reporting Quick Card, workbook page W-P4, Question Box, Pass cards. Optional tech version: none.

Hook (2 minutes)#

Say: "Hackles up? When something online starts as teasing and keeps going, or makes someone go quiet, notice it. You do not need to be sure to pause. Florida law defines bullying and cyberbullying and says cyberbullying includes bullying through technology or electronic communication [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: s. 1006.147, leg.state.fl.us, read in part]. The state guide describes it as using technology to send, post or share negative, false or mean content, and describes online harassment as unwanted behavior directed at a person through technology (p.13) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: FLDOE Student's Guide to Internet Safety, May 2025 PDF]. Today you will ask four questions that help tell mean from harmful, then practice what to do and where to report."

Activity (8 minutes): Four Questions and the Response Menu#

  • Step 1 (1 min): The Four-Question Strip. Write on the board: REPEATED (has it happened more than once?), HURTFUL (does it humiliate, threaten, exclude or spread private information?), PUBLIC OR HIDDEN (are many people seeing it, or is it anonymous or in a group chat?), POWER (is the target outnumbered, less popular, or picked on for who they are?). Say: "If two or more are yes, treat it as harmful, not as a joke."
  • Step 2 (3 min): Sort (pairs). Pairs sort eight fictional Situation Cards: ANNOYING, HURTING, TELL NOW, and mark which of the Four Questions apply. The threat card and the intimate-picture card must land at TELL NOW. One card (a single rude comment with no pattern) is meant to land at ANNOYING, so students practice not over-labeling.
  • Step 3 (3 min): Response Menu (groups of 4). Groups assign each card a first move from the menu: Pause and do not retaliate. SAVE words, usernames and times. Block or mute. Report in the app. Support the target privately. Tell a trusted adult. Report at school. Take a break. Read the three-case evidence rule aloud, and post it:
    1. Words and records (comments, threats, usernames, links, dates, scam messages): screenshots and saved copies are fine. Keep them private, give them to a trusted adult, never post or forward them to classmates.
    2. Intimate or sexual pictures of anyone under 18, including the student, a classmate, or an AI or edited fake: do not screenshot, save, forward or show anyone, not even a teacher or a friend, and not as proof. Save only the surrounding words (username, message, link, date). Tell a trusted adult, who uses the platform's report tool and, where right, NCMEC or law enforcement. Use the Response Drill from Lesson 5.
    3. Not sure which kind it is? Do not capture it. Tell a trusted adult first. Telling matters more than saving.
      Also post the order for threats and for requests for pictures or money: tell first, block with an adult's help, delete nothing. For ordinary harassment, block and report after saving the words. [COUNSEL TO CONFIRM]
  • Step 4 (1 min): Where to report. Say: "Your school has a process for reporting bullying and harassment under Florida law; know who takes the report [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: s. 1006.147 requires a district policy; VERIFY how your school receives reports]. A trusted adult can be a parent, a teacher, the school counselor, or the school resource officer if your school has one; you choose. For threats or crimes, local law enforcement. For abuse, the Florida Abuse Hotline, 1-800-962-2873 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: myflfamilies.com]. In danger: 911."

Discuss (3 minutes)#

  • Q1. Which question helps most in telling mean from harmful? Why?
  • Q2. Why do bystanders matter? What can one private message do?
  • Q3. Why is "do not retaliate" hard, and why is it a good first rule?
  • Q4. How can a screenshot be used for help and for harm? (This is where the three-case rule lands.)
  • Q5. What would make you more likely to report? What gets in the way? What good can come from a report? (the state guide's positive-outcomes framing, p.15)
  • Q6. What can a team, a class or a school do to make reporting easier? (the community level, p.11)

Takeaway (2 minutes)#

Say: "Pause before you reply or add to the pile. Check the four questions. Tell a trusted adult, and report in the app and at school. It is never the target's fault, and saving evidence is for adults who can help, not for spreading."

Exit ticket#

"Write the first move you would make for a card that was TELL NOW, and one person you would tell." Optional: "[ ] I would like to talk to the counselor privately."

If a student discloses#

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (Lesson P4)

  • Why it might come up: a student may be, or may have been, a target, a bystander or a participant.
  • Say: "Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. We will work it out together." For a student who took part: "Thank you for being honest. We can fix this. You can stop and make it right."
  • Don't say: "Just ignore it." "Why didn't you tell sooner?" Do not ask for images.
  • Escalate: threats, sexual images of a minor, self-harm, hate or stalking: same-day counselor and administrator; law enforcement per policy; Florida Abuse Hotline for abuse; the district bullying procedure under s. 1006.147 [VERIFY]; self-harm: district protocol and 988 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org, as tagged in C1]. Document the student's own words, the date and time, and who you told. Do not view, copy, forward or screenshot an intimate image of a minor.

If a student discloses

Make It Stick (Lesson P4): optional add-ons. They never replace the core lesson, the disclosure box, or any safeguard.

  • 2-minute hook variation: Joke or Hurt? (60 seconds): the teacher reads one invented line ("Nice haircut, at least it's not your usual one") in two contexts (between close friends, and repeated in a group of twelve). Pairs say what changed.
  • Debate or game version: Calm: Menu Match. Groups swap a completed card and the receiving group adds one move the first group did not use. Debate option: "Is staying out of it ever the right choice?" (Draw out: not for TELL NOW situations.)
  • Peer-teaching or choice option: Choice: write the one-sentence private message you would send a target, or the sentence you would say to a friend who is piling on.
  • Level-Up Challenge: A one-page "upstander guide" for younger students (counselor-approved, no real cases).
  • Say-it-back line (post it): "Repeated, hurtful, public, powerless? Treat it as harm. Tell first."
  • Timing and extended block: If time is short, use five cards. Extended block (30 to 40 min): add a third pass on the guide's nine steps beside the Response Menu (teacher notes) and a Question Box read-out.

  • Support: Use four cards and a labeled Response Menu.
  • Extension: Students design a one-page upstander guide for younger students.
  • Low or no internet at home: The strip and the menu work for texts, calls and in-person bullying too. A student can tell a school adult without any device.
  • Language access: Translate the strip and the menu [VERIFY]. Let students discuss in their home language.

C3. Hard questions teens ask (short answers)#

  • "What if I already sent something?" It is still not your fault. The plan is the same: Stop, Save details, Tell, Report. Adults can help.
  • "What if I get in trouble?" The person who threatens or exploits is the one doing wrong. Laws about teen images vary by state, and adults can help you navigate that [VERIFY]. We do not promise an outcome, but we do promise you will be heard.
  • "What if my parents will freak out?" That is a common fear. Pick another adult (a counselor, a relative, a coach). Parents often react with fear because they care; many calm down after the first minute.
  • "Can the school see my phone?" A teacher will not look at your device unless school policy and the law allow it; in cases involving images, adults are trained not to view the images themselves [VERIFY district policy].
  • "Is it true no one can be fully safe online?" No one is 100 percent safe anywhere. The aim is to lower the risk and know what to do.
  • "Why are you telling us this like we are the problem?" We are not; the problem is the people who exploit and the systems that make it easy. You are the people who can do something about it.
  • "Isn't this just fear?" It is meant to be the opposite: patterns, skills, and a plan, so you feel prepared rather than scared.

C4. Inclusion and trauma-informed delivery (quick notes)#

  • Offer choice at every step: pass, step out, write instead of speak, sit near the door.
  • Use fictional characters and keep scenarios free of graphic detail.
  • Keep the room predictable: tell students what is coming, and give the same structure each time.
  • Avoid assigning roles that make a student act out being a target or an offender.
  • Plan for students with disabilities: read-aloud text, large-print cards, alternatives to card-up responses, extra time.
  • Remember that some students are survivors, some are currently affected, and some have already caused harm; your language should leave room for all of them to ask for help.
  • Offer a follow-up: "If anything from today stays on your mind, here are the people you can talk to."
