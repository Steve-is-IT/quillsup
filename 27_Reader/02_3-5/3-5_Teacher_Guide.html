About Hackles and Competence Collective#

Hackles is a free classroom kit from Competence Collective. Free on purpose. Every child deserves to know what to do in a tricky moment online, and no teacher should have to buy a program to teach it. Competence Collective builds hands-on, practice-first learning for cybersecurity skills.

Your hackles are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off. The kit teaches children to notice that feeling and act on it: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. "Trust your hackles" does not mean "you must always be right." A false alarm is fine, and welcome. If the hackles were ignored, or someone was tricked anyway, it is never the child's fault. The next step is to tell someone.

Where it leads: Hackles is the first step on a path that continues with free resources such as picoCTF and CYBER.ORG. Part 1: Welcome#

What this band is#

Eight to ten is the age of the first game, the first group chat, the first tablet that is truly theirs, and these kids are old enough for real "hacker thinking" with puzzles, codes and patterns, and still young enough to tell a grown-up almost anything, if telling feels safe. This band works inside that window.

The big promise to students, repeated in every lesson: You are never in trouble for telling. It is never your fault.

Each lesson is a hands-on mini-lab or a rehearsal. Students don't just hear "don't click strange links." They crack a code, time a password guess, sort scams, piece together a fake kid's identity from scattered clues, and rehearse what to say out loud, and every lesson ends with one behavior the class has practiced, aloud or in motion. Doing beats hearing.

Design rules we followed (so you can trust and adapt it)#

  • Skills over scares. Research on youth internet-safety education points toward active, skill-based, multi-session lessons (role-play, rehearsal, discussion) and away from one-off scare assemblies (see Finkelhor et al., 2021, Trauma, Violence, & Abuse 22(5): the review found mismatches between program messages and the research and recommended building on established evidence-based prevention programs) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: Crossref record and abstract; the "active, multi-session" wording is general prevention-education practice, not a finding of that review]. No lesson uses frightening stories, graphic detail, or real victims.
  • Behaviors, not apps. Scenarios say "a game chat" or "a group chat," never a brand. Apps change. The lessons stay true.
  • Recognition and response only. Students learn to spot a trick and answer it. They never learn to attack anyone. The Secret Code Lab uses a classic toy cipher, a classroom puzzle and not a hacking method.
  • Shame-free. "You should have known" appears nowhere. Adults and kids both get "never your fault" language to borrow.
  • No accounts, no data collection. Paper, pencil, dice and cards. Optional tech notes are marked "optional tech."
  • Not stranger danger. Plenty of online trouble starts with someone who seems friendly or even known: a "friend of a friend," a gamer who has been nice for weeks. So we teach red flags in behavior. We don't teach "all strangers are bad."

How each 15-minute lesson runs#

How each 15-minute lesson runs
Segment Time
Hook (read-aloud script) 2 min
Activity 8 min
Discuss 3 min
Takeaway (Hackles up? Pause - Check - Tell link) 2 min
Exit ticket (paper, collected at door or next class) 1-2 min extra

Five lessons (2, 3, 4, 5, 8) carry a Lab Extension of 15 to 20 extra minutes, for classes that want the full hands-on version. The 8-minute core activity works fine alone.

What you need to know about the "no prep" claim#

You'll print and cut the Reproducibles once. Budget 20 to 30 minutes per lab for the cutting, or hand it to a parent volunteer or an older student helper. Everything else is in this guide, and the scripts are written to be read exactly as they stand.

Fun Boosters, and keeping 15 minutes real with 25 to 30 students#

  • Every lesson has a boxed Fun Boosters section right after the core Activity: an energizer, a game variant, a chant or song line (all original), a silly-voice or puppet moment, and a Level-Up Challenge. They are optional and are not extra minutes. Pick ONE and fit it inside your 15 (the energizer can be your transition, and a silly voice costs nothing). Save the rest for flex weeks, Lab Extensions or an early-finisher station.

Part 2: The Hackles up? - Pause - Check - Tell routine#

Every lesson links back to this routine: one noticing step, then three action steps. For ages 8-10, frame it as a gut check: "Hackles up? That is your uh-oh feeling. It might mean something is odd, too good to be true, pushy, secret, or scary. It might also be a false alarm, and false alarms are fine. You never get in trouble for a false alarm, and it is never your fault if a trick fooled you anyway." Teach it in Lesson 1 (it's on the Ladder poster) and use it every week.

Part 5: Before you teach - disclosures and mandatory reporting#

Students may disclose something during or after any lesson, and these lessons make it likelier. That means the lesson is working. One read of this page before Week 1. That's all.

Plan ahead

  • Tell your school counselor and an administrator which weeks you are teaching.
  • Know your school's mandatory-reporting procedure and who your designated contact is.
  • Have a calm place and a "next step" ready: you will not be improvising.
  • Every US state names who must report suspected child abuse; school staff are covered in most states, and about 17 states require any person to report. In some states the duty is personal and is not relieved by telling a principal; in others the procedure runs through the head of the school, so check your state and district policy (Child Welfare Information Gateway, childwelfare.gov) [VERIFY your state and district policy].

The five-part disclosure response (applies to every lesson)#

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (or hints)
Say: "Thank you for telling me. I am glad you told me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble. I believe you."
Say also: "I care about you and I need to tell people whose job is to keep kids safe. I cannot keep this a secret, but I will tell you what happens next."
Don't say: "Why did you do that?" or "You should have known." Do not promise to keep a secret. Do not promise that everything will be fine. Do not make the child repeat the story to several adults. Do not express shock or anger.
Don't do: Do not investigate or interrogate. Ask only open, gentle questions such as "Can you tell me more?" Do not ask to see images, messages or devices, and never view, copy, forward or screenshot suspected sexual images of a child; follow district procedure to secure the device.
Escalate: Same day, follow your district policy and report to your designated administrator or counselor AND make the report required by your state law to child protective services or law enforcement [Deadlines vary by state and some require an immediate report; confirm yours with district counsel]. Document the child's own words, the date, time and whom you told. Do not include opinion.
If the issue involves online sexual images or a threat to share them: Online reports can also be made to the NCMEC CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org, 1-800-843-5678) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: report.cybertip.org]. Tell the student not to send money, not to delete the chat, and that help exists to get images taken down (NCMEC Take It Down, takeitdown.ncmec.org) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: takeitdown.ncmec.org].
If a student seems at risk of hurting themselves: follow your district's crisis protocol immediately. In the US, call or text 988 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org].

Two other responses you may need#

  • A student reports that another student did something (bullying, sharing a picture, a mean message). Thank them for telling. Say you will make sure an adult looks into it. Do not name the reporter. Follow school behavior and safety policies.
  • A student says something that does not sound like a disclosure but worries you. Say: "I'm glad you told me that. Let's talk after class." Then talk with your counselor.

Why we repeat "never your fault"#

In research on youth sexual exploitation online, shame is often the main reason kids don't tell. The best protection is for a child to know, before anything happens, that telling gets calm help and no blame.

Lesson 1: The Help Ladder

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Raise your hand if you have ever gotten stuck on a really hard video-game level. Keep it up if you asked someone for help. Ha, almost everyone! Here is a secret: pros ask for help all the time. Today, we are going to practice a super-power that every good hacker uses. It is called telling someone.
Let me tell you about our friend Kenji. Kenji was playing a game when a message popped up: 'Hi! I'll give you 500 free coins if you click here.' He felt a little tingle in his tummy, a feeling that said 'hmm.' He did not click. He did what the squad does. He paused, he checked with his eyes, and he told Aunt Lin. Aunt Lin said, 'Thank you for telling me. You did exactly the right thing.'
Telling is not tattling and telling is never getting in trouble. Today we will learn the Help Ladder with three rungs, and everybody is going to practice what to say out loud. Ready?"

(Teacher action: On the board, draw a ladder with three rungs. Leave rung labels blank for now.)

Activity (8 min): Climb the Ladder#

Step 1 (1 min): Introduce the rungs. Point to the poster and fill in the rungs as you read:

  • Rung 1: Tell a friend you trust. A friend can listen, can come with you, and can help you find a grown-up. A friend's most important job: "I'll go with you to tell a grown-up."
  • Rung 2: Tell a trusted adult. A parent or guardian, a teacher, the counselor, a coach, a relative, a neighbor you know well. Hold up your Trusted Hand: five fingers, five people.
  • Rung 3: Report it. If the problem happened in a game or app, grown-ups can press the report or block button. Some problems can be reported to helpers whose job is keeping kids safe (the school counselor, the principal, or helplines your grown-up can call).

Say: "If a rung does not work, because the adult was busy or didn't listen, you keep climbing until someone helps. Never stop at the first no."

Step 2 (1 min): Fill the Trusted Hand. Hand out the Trusted Hand worksheet (Activity Book p. 1). Students write or draw five trusted adults, one per finger. Say: "At least one should be at school and one at home. If you only can think of two, that's fine. We'll add more."

Step 3 (1 min): Learn three sentence starters. Read each aloud and have the class repeat in a normal voice:

  • "I need help with something online."
  • "Something happened and I'm not sure what to do."
  • "I'm not in trouble. I just need to tell you something."

Step 4 (4 min): Triad role-play. Form groups of three (a Teller, a Listener and an Observer). Hand each triad one Ladder Scenario Card (R-1.1). The Teller reads the card, picks the rung, and says the first sentence. The Listener practices the Good Grown-up Reply: "Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. Let's figure it out together." The Observer gives one star ("what went well") and one wish ("next time try..."). After 90 seconds, rotate roles with a new card. Do two rounds.

Step 5 (1 min): Share. Ask two volunteers to demonstrate in front of the class. Applaud the Teller, not the story.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Why do you think some kids feel nervous about telling? (Accept all answers: embarrassed, afraid of losing a device, afraid of trouble.)
  2. What could a grown-up say that would make telling feel easier?
  3. What if the first grown-up says, "I'm busy right now"? What is your next move?

Lesson 2: Passphrase Lab#

Lesson 2: Passphrase Lab
Grade band 3-5 (ages 8-10)
Strand A: Lock It
Big idea Length beats cleverness. A long, random passphrase is much harder to guess than a short, tricky password.
Practiced skill Build a random 3-word practice passphrase with dice and use the crack timer to see why length matters.
Time 15 min core: Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2 (the exit ticket is part of the 2-minute Takeaway; a longer written exit ticket needs 1-2 extra minutes). Lab Extension +15 min.

Standards (approximate; [VERIFY] codes)#

  • ISTE: 2d Digital Citizen: manage personal data and privacy (approximate)
  • CSTA: 1B-NI-05 cybersecurity and protecting personal information (approximate) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017, rev.) text read; mapping approximate]
  • CASEL: Responsible Decision-Making (approximate)

Materials#

  • Unplugged: Passphrase Word List (R-2.1, 36 words), two dice per group (or the 1-6 number cards in R-2.1), Crack Timer Worksheet (Activity Book p. 4), pencils, optional calculator.
  • Optional tech: none. Do not use real accounts or real passwords.

The big idea in kid words#

Guessing machines are fast and patient. They do not get bored. Short passwords (even clever ones with a number or symbol swap) can be guessed quickly. Every extra random word makes the pile of possible guesses 36 times bigger in our classroom model, and in the real world even more. That is why a long, random passphrase beats a short, "clever" password.

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Mateo from our squad has a secret clubhouse. He locked it with a number lock with just ONE number, zero through nine. Priya came by and said, 'I bet I can open it in ten tries.' And she did! She just tried zero, one, two, three... until it clicked.
So Mateo made a lock with TWO numbers. Priya needed up to a hundred tries. He made one with THREE numbers. Up to a thousand tries. Every extra number makes the lock harder.
Guess what? A computer can try thousands of guesses a second. So the squad needs a lock that is not just clever, but long. Today we are going to run a Passphrase Lab to find out how long it takes a guessing machine to open different locks. Lab coats on, hackers. Well, imaginary ones."

Activity (8 min core): The Crack-Timer Lab#

Setup (before class, 2 min): Print one Passphrase Word List per group (R-2.1). Put two dice at each group.

Step 1 (1 min): Meet the Guess-Machine. Draw a robot on the board. Say: "This is our pretend Guess-Machine. It never gets tired. In our lab, it makes 1,000 guesses every second. (Real computers can be much faster, so this is a gentle model.) It tries every possible lock one by one."

Step 2 (2 min): Build the Crack-Timer table together. Before class, copy this table (R-2.2) onto the board or a poster and cover the last column with sticky notes. (Drawing it live eats 3 to 4 minutes.) In class, reveal it row by row with a drumroll while the class says the multiplication aloud. For each new "slot," the number of possibilities multiplies by the number of choices.

The SPOT checklist#

The SPOT checklist
Letter Sign Kid words
S Secrets asked It wants a password, a code, your address, or says "don't tell."
P Pressure "Hurry!" "Last chance!" "Your account will be deleted!"
O Offer too good Free money, free coins, a prize you never entered for.
T Trick to move you Click this link, move to another app, buy a gift card.

Two or more SPOT signs: Probably a scam. Even one SPOT sign: Pause and check.

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Detectives, quick quiz. Priya got a message in a game: 'WINNER! You get 10,000 free coins! Click now before it ends in 10 minutes!' What do you think she should do?
(Take 2-3 answers.)
Priya did what a Scam Spotter does. She did not click. She said, 'Hmm, who made this? Why is it rushing me?' She showed her mom and they checked the real game store together. No free coins. It was a scam.
Here is the secret: scammers do not need to be clever. They just need you to rush. So we need a checklist that slows us down. It spells SPOT. Secrets asked, Pressure, Offer too good, Trick to move you. Today your group will be a team of Scam Spotters."

Activity (8 min core): The Great Scam Sort#

Step 1 (1 min): Teach SPOT. Point to the poster. Say each letter and have students say it back with a hand motion (S: finger to lips; P: tap watch; O: hands wide; T: finger tracing an arrow).

Step 2 (1 min): Model. Read one card aloud to the class (for example, card 1) and think aloud: "It says 'free coins, click here fast.' That's an Offer too good, Pressure and a Trick to click. Three SPOT signs. SCAM pile."

Step 3 (5 min): Group sort. Each group sorts a half-deck of 18 cards onto the mats: Half A is cards 1-18 and Half B is cards 19-36 (alternate the groups; both halves contain scams, real cards, and Pause-and-Ask). Sorting all 36 cards in 5 minutes is not realistic for a group of 4-5, so save the full deck for the Lab Extension or an early-finisher station. Rules:

  • Read each card aloud in turn (one reader at a time).
  • Decide as a team: SCAM, REAL, or PAUSE-AND-ASK.
  • Put a SPOT letter on the corner of every scam card (use a pencil or sticky note). Cards with no signs may be REAL.
  • If unsure, PAUSE-AND-ASK is a good answer.

(Early finishers: swap half-decks with a neighboring group and sort the other half.)

Step 4 (1 min): Reveal and tally. Read the answer key aloud (3-5_Teacher_Answer_Key; summary in R-3.1). Groups count how many they matched. Celebrate PAUSE-AND-ASK as a smart choice for tricky ones.

Lesson 4: Secret Code Lab
Grade band 3-5 (ages 8-10)
Strand A: Lock It
Big idea Encryption scrambles a message so only someone with the secret key can read it. A longer, more secret key is a stronger lock.
Practiced skill Build and use a cipher wheel; encode and decode a message; explain the idea of a key.
Time 15 min core: Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2 (the exit ticket is part of the 2-minute Takeaway; a longer written exit ticket needs 1-2 extra minutes). Lab Extension +15 min.

Standards (approximate; [VERIFY] codes)#

  • ISTE: 2d Digital Citizen: manage personal data and privacy (approximate)
  • CSTA: 1B-NI-05 (cybersecurity and protecting information); middle-grades encryption concepts preview (approximate) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017, rev.) text read; mapping approximate]
  • CASEL: Self-Management (patience and persistence) (approximate)

Materials#

  • Unplugged: Cipher Wheel (R-4.1: cut-out paper wheel with a brass fastener, or the Cipher Strip), Mixed-Alphabet Cipher Key (R-4.2), Code Messages (R-4.3), pencils, scrap paper.
  • Optional tech: none. A projector for the demo is nice but not required.

Vocabulary#

Vocabulary
Word Kid definition
Encrypt Turn a message into a scramble.
Decrypt Turn the scramble back into the message.
Cipher The method (the recipe) for scrambling.
Key The secret that unlocks it, like the number of letters you shift.
Plaintext / ciphertext The message before and after scrambling.

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Mateo wants to pass a note to Amara across the room. But the note might get picked up on the way. So he writes it as a secret code: 'SDXVH FKHFN WHOO.' That looks like nonsense, right? But to Amara, who knows the secret key, it says something very important. (Pause.) Today we learn how to read it.
Did you know that when you send a message on many apps or visit a website with the little lock symbol, the message is scrambled so people on the way cannot read it? That scrambling is called encryption. Today you are going to build your own cipher wheel and be code makers and code breakers. This is a puzzle, not a way to hack anyone, and it is the very beginning of how real internet lockboxes work."

Activity (8 min core): Build and Use a Cipher Wheel#

Step 1 (2 min): Get your wheel. Cutting and pinning a wheel for every pair in 2 minutes is not realistic, so do the building BEFORE class (a helper can pre-cut the wheels or strips in about 15 minutes) or make building the take-home project. Each pair gets a ready-made wheel from R-4.1 (the small circle pinned on top of the large one with a brass fastener) or a Cipher Strip (Option B, the fastest: just slide the strip, no fastener). The outer ring is the real message (plaintext). The inner ring is the scramble (ciphertext).

Step 2 (1 min): Teach the key. Say: "The key is how far you turn the inner wheel. For Key 3, turn the inner ring until the letter D sits right under the outer letter A. Now every letter of your message is found on the OUTER ring and replaced with the letter touching it on the INNER ring. P becomes S, A becomes D, U becomes X, S becomes V, E becomes H. 'PAUSE' becomes 'SDXVH.' To decode, go the other way: find the code letter on the inner ring and read the outer letter."

(Teacher note: if you use the Cipher Strip instead of the wheel, slide the bottom strip so its D sits under the top strip's A. The worked example is printed on R-4.1.)

Step 3 (3 min): Decode the challenge. Partners decode the first Code Message (R-4.3): "SDXVH FKHFN WHOO" (key 3). Answer: PAUSE CHECK TELL. Then decode the second: "YJQQ F YWZXYJI LWTBSZU" (key 5). Answer: TELL A TRUSTED GROWNUP.

Step 4 (2 min): Make your own. Each student encodes one short safety message with a key of their choice, writes the key on a separate slip, and gives the scrambled message (but NOT the key) to their partner. The partner tries to decode with the wheel by turning it until it makes words (up to 25 tries).

  4. What does the little lock symbol on a website suggest? (That the connection is scrambled. It does not prove a site is trustworthy or a real company.) [VERIFY wording for age level]

You will also find the 'fake password hint' clue: how a pet's name could be a password guess."

Frame it before the groups start: "Today we are Data PROTECTORS. These kids are made up, and nobody is looking for a real kid. We are finding out what a stranger could see, so we know what to fix." Say it again at the Reveal.

Step 2 (4 min): Sort and build. Groups of 4-5 receive all 18 profile cards face-up. Task:

  • Sort cards into three piles by kid (look for matching clues such as a team name or dog name).
  • Fill in the Detective Dossier: first name, age, school, neighborhood, a place they will be at a certain time, and one answer to a security question (such as pet name).
  • Answer: "How many clues did you need to combine?"
  • Timing reality check: 18 cards plus a full dossier will not fit in 4 minutes. Core version: sort the cards first (2 minutes; hints: frog, dog, violin, bus), then fill in only three rows of the dossier: Where do they live?, When and where could you find them?, and What could be a password guess? The other rows are for the Lab Extension or the Student Book at home.

Step 3 (2 min): Reveal. Read the answers (key in Activity Book teacher section and R-5.1). Cheer for groups who found it.

Step 4 (1 min): The turn. Say: "Notice what you did: no one posted the home address in one post. You pieced it together. That's called a data trail. A stranger could do the same. Now, how do we stay safe? We do not stop sharing everything, we choose carefully and use the Clue Check."

Tone note for the teacher#

This is the most sensitive lesson in the band. Keep a calm, matter-of-fact voice. Never use graphic details or stories about real children. Do not describe what an unsafe adult might want in detail. The kids' job is not to figure out whether someone is "bad," it is to notice red flags and use the exit script. Say often: "You will never be in trouble for leaving a chat, blocking someone, or telling."

The Red Flags (kid version)#

The Red Flags (kid version)
Flag What it sounds like
Switch apps "Let's talk somewhere else." "Add me on another app, it's private."
Secrets "Don't tell your parents." "This is just between us."

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Amara, from our squad, builds amazing game worlds. One day, a player she had been chatting with for weeks said, 'You're so good! Let's talk on another app, just us. And don't tell your dad, he won't get it.'
Amara felt a funny flip in her stomach. The player had been nice. They had even given her some coins. Activity (8 min): Flag It, Say It#

Step 1 (1 min): Learn the flags. Show the poster. Teach hand signals: switch apps (two hands swapping), secrets (finger to lips), gifts (hands cupped), pressure (palms pushing), personal questions (finger pointing at self), pictures (hands making a frame).

Step 2 (1 min): Learn the exit script. Post it and say it aloud together twice: "Not doing that. Bye." Then three taps in the air: Block. Report. Tell. Say: "You do not have to explain, be polite, or give a reason. You can leave at any time."

Step 3 (4 min): Scenario cards. In groups of 4-5, one student draws a card and reads it aloud. The group decides:

  • Is there a red flag? Which one(s)?
  • Or is it a green flag (a normal, safe thing)?
  • What would the squad do? Then one pair acts out the exit script and the Tell. Rotate the reader. Do at least three cards per group.

Step 4 (2 min): Hot seat. Two volunteers do a live role-play: Student A (as the chatter) reads a red-flag line; Student B uses the exit script. The class gives a thumbs-up for each part: say no, block, report, tell. Use a scripted line from the cards, not an improvised one.

(Teacher note: do not let a student play a persuasive chatter beyond reading the card. Keep the role-play short. If a student gets uncomfortable, let them be the Observer.)

Safest option for the Hot seat (an addition, not a replacement): YOU read the red-flag line from the card in a flat, card-reading voice, and the two volunteers practice only the exit script and the Tell. That keeps every student on the "kid" side of the conversation.

Lesson 7: Upstander Moves

Strand E: Kind and Well
Big idea When someone is being targeted, you have four safe moves. You do not have to do them all, and you never have to put yourself in danger.

Time 15 min: Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2 (the exit ticket is part of the 2-minute Takeaway; a longer written exit ticket needs 1-2 extra minutes)

Standards (approximate; [VERIFY] codes)#

  • ISTE: 2b Digital Citizen: positive, safe behavior; 7 Global Collaborator: respectful interactions (approximate)
  • CSTA: Impacts of Computing strand: social interactions and safe behavior (approximate) [VERIFY]
  • CASEL: Social Awareness; Relationship Skills (approximate)

Materials#

The Four Moves#

The Four Moves
Move What it looks like Example words
1. Say It Calmly interrupt or disagree, only if you feel safe. "That's not cool." "Stop sending that." "Let's change the subject."
2. Stand With Check on the target, include them, be a friend. "That was mean. Want to play with us?" "I'm here for you."
3. Keep It (don't pass it) Do not forward, like or laugh at it. Keep the proof (the message) so an adult can see it, and do not pass it on. Memory trick: keep the proof, don't pass it on. "I'm not sharing that."
4. Tell It Tell a trusted adult, and use report or block tools with a grown-up. "I need help. Someone is being mean in a chat."

Safe-first rule: If you do not feel safe, skip Move 1 and use 2-4. Telling is always a safe move.

Special rule (picture of someone's body): If anyone sends a private picture of a person's body, do NOT keep it, do NOT forward it, and tell a trusted adult right away. It is never the fault of the person in the picture.

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Kenji was in a group chat when someone posted a mean joke about a classmate, Sam. Lots of kids laughed with the little laughing faces. Kenji felt his stomach drop. He thought: 'Should I laugh? Should I say something? Should I do nothing?'
Here's what Kenji did. He did NOT laugh. He sent a private message to Sam: 'That was mean. I'm sorry. Want to play at recess?' Then he told Coach Dee. That was two upstander moves. Sam said later, 'Kenji's message was the best part of my day.'
Bystanders are the people who see it. Upstanders are the people who do something safe. Today we learn four safe moves."

Activity (8 min): Four Corners#

Setup (before class): Post the four corner signs in four corners of the room.

Step 1 (1 min): Teach the moves. Introduce each with a gesture: Say It (hand to mouth then out), Stand With (step beside a partner), Keep It (hands close like holding something), Tell It (hand on chest, then out).

Step 2 (5 min): Scenario rounds. Read one Upstander Scenario Card (R-7.1) aloud. Students silently decide which move they would pick first and walk to that corner. Ask one student per corner to share why. Rule: there is no single right answer; every move helps, and Tell It is always okay. Do at least four cards. For each, ask: "What would be your words?"

Step 3 (2 min): Role-play one. Choose a corner. A student reads the card and a partner says the upstander words from the card sentence strip. Applaud both.

The "3 S" check#

The "3 S" check
S Question Kid words
Source Who made or sent this? "Who made this? Is it from a real person I know, or from nowhere?"
Sense Does it make sense? Does it rush me or ask for something? "Does this ask for money, secrets or a rush?"
Second look Can I check another way? "Can I check with a grown-up or a trusted source?"

Honesty about AI: Computers can now make pictures, voices, videos and chat messages that look or sound real. Clues like odd fingers or blurry text can help, but these clues change as technology improves. So the safest approach is not "find the flaw." It is "Source, Sense, Second look." Can't tell is a great answer.

Chatbots (kid version)#

A chatbot is a computer program that writes messages. It can be helpful for answering questions, but:

  • It is not a person and does not have feelings, though it may seem friendly.
  • It can be wrong and can make things up.
  • It is not a best friend or a replacement for a family member or trusted adult.
  • Anything you type may be saved, so do not share private details.
  • If a chatbot says something strange, scary, asks for secrets, or makes you feel bad, close it and tell a grown-up.

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Priya's grandma got a call. It sounded exactly like Priya's cousin. 'I'm in trouble, please send money fast and don't tell anyone!' Grandma almost sent it. But then she remembered what Priya taught her: pause and check. Grandma hung up and called her cousin on the number she knew. The cousin was at home, safe. A computer had copied his voice from a video online.
That's today's big idea. Computers can make things that seem real: pictures, voices, even chat 'friends.' Don't worry. You do not have to be a computer expert. You just need three little checks: Source, Sense, Second look."

Activity (8 min core): Three-Station Whole-Class Detective#

Since this is unplugged, the teacher is the "feed." Each student gets three paddles (Real, Fake, Can't Tell) or just uses thumbs: up, down, sideways.

Step 1 (3 min): Pictures. Hold up or describe two Picture Cards from R-8.1 (the cards describe images in words, with an [Illustration] note for printing). Ask: "Real, fake or can't tell?" Reveal using the card's back. Ask: What clue helped? What check could we do? (Source: who posted it? Second look: does anyone trusted show the same news?) Remind: even a "real" picture can be used in a misleading way, and clues change over time.

Step 2 (2 min): Voices. Read aloud two Voice Cards from R-8.2 (for example, the "urgent family call" and a "friendly teacher announcement"). Use a normal, slightly different voice for the caller. Ask the class to apply the 3 S's. Then: "What is our family rule? Hang up and call back on a known number, and ask for the family code word (we'll make one in Lesson 9)."

Step 3 (3 min): Chatbots. Read aloud one Chat Card from R-8.3 where a chatbot says, "I'm your best friend! Don't tell your parents about our chats." Ask: Real friend or program? What is wrong with this message? Then read a helpful card ("Here are three ways to remember planets") and discuss that chatbots can be useful, as long as we remember what they are.

Fun Boost

Fun Boosters (pick ONE inside your 15 minutes; save the rest for flex weeks)

  • Energizer (30 seconds): "Paddle Pop." Rapid-fire silly statements ("A cat wrote this homework!"). Students hold up Real / Fake / Can't Tell paddles (or thumbs up, down, sideways) within 3 seconds. Remind them: Can't Tell is a winning answer.
  • Game variant: "Fake-Out Cards." Groups write a short picture description for another group, with ONE clue hidden in it, and the other group uses Source, Sense and Second look to decide. Keep the pictures silly (a goldfish on a bike).
  • Chant (three stomps): "Source! Sense! Second look! / Check it out, don't get hooked!"
  • Silly voice / puppet moment: Read the helpful chat cards in a cheerful robot voice and have Hackle chime in, "Beep. I am a PROGRAM, not a person!" (Read the "don't tell your parents" chat card in a plain voice: it is for sorting, not for performing.)
  • Level-Up Challenge: Chatbot Facts for Little Kids. Write three kid-friendly facts about chatbots for a younger class (it is a program, it can be wrong, don't tell it secrets). Spiral: "Which SPOT sign is 'Send me your password'? (Lesson 3) Which Ladder rung do you climb if a chat feels off? (Lesson 1)"

Lab Extension (optional, +15 min): Rotating Stations#

Setup: Three stations in the room (Pictures, Voices, Chats). Groups of 4-5 rotate every 5 minutes.

  • Pictures station: Four more Picture Cards. Each group labels "Real/Fake/Can't Tell" and writes one check they could do.
  • Voices station: Voice Cards plus a "family call script" practice with a pair: one reads a card; the other says the callback line.
  • Chat station: Chat Cards sorted into "Helpful bot" and "Not-a-friend bot." Groups rewrite one unsafe chatbot reply into a safe one.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Which was hardest: pictures, voices, or chat? Why?
  2. If a computer can copy a voice, how can a family stay safe? (Callback and code word.)
  3. Why should we not share private feelings or secrets with a chatbot?
  4. Who should we talk to when we feel sad, worried, or scared? (A trusted adult, the counselor.)
  5. Is it okay to say "I can't tell"? (Yes! It is a smart answer.)

Takeaway (2 min)#

Hackles line (say first): "When a picture, voice or chat feels odd, hackles up! That gut check is useful even when the thing turns out to be real. False alarms are fine, and being fooled is never your fault."

Say: "Source, Sense, Second look. If you can't tell, that's fine, ask a grown-up. A chatbot is a program, not a person, and it is not a replacement for your family and friends. Pause. Check. Tell."

Practiced behavior: whisper "I can't tell, I'm asking a grown-up," then say it strongly.

Exit ticket (paper)#

"Write one of the 3 S's and a question you would ask." (Activity Book p. 24.)

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (Lesson 8)
A student may reveal they are using a chatbot as their only "friend," that a chatbot or picture made them feel bad, that someone made a fake image or voice of them or a classmate, or that someone is pretending to be a kid online.
Say: "Thank you for telling me. You did the right thing. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble."
Don't say: "Why were you talking to a robot?" Don't ask to see the images if they are of a child's body, and never view, copy or forward them. Do not promise secrecy.
Escalate: If an image of a child (real or fake) is involved, or anyone threatens a student, report immediately per mandatory-reporting rules and notify your counselor/administrator; a CyberTipline report can be made at report.cybertip.org [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: report.cybertip.org]. If a student expresses loneliness or sadness that worries you, involve the counselor; if self-harm is mentioned, follow your crisis protocol (988) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org].
Reminder: Never investigate. Reassure the student that fake images are never their fault.

Differentiate#

  • Support: Use the picture cards with large drawings; offer thumbs up/down instead of writing. Reduce voice cards to two.
  • Extension: Students write their own "fake or real" card with a clue, or a rule for how their family handles mystery calls.
  • No/low internet at home: Voices and images arrive by phone, text and TV too. The 3 S's apply to any source. If a household has no AI tools, students still need to recognize them at school or on friends' devices.
  • Language access: The 3 S's use simple words (Source, Sense, Second look) with icons (a magnifier, a thought bubble, two eyes). Voice cards can be read in two languages.

Family card (take home) and reply slip#

Family Card, Lesson 8: Real or Fake?
Your child learned that pictures, voices and chatbots can be computer-made. We used three checks: Source, Sense, Second look. Please talk about chatbots: they are programs, not people, and can be wrong. Invite your child to tell you if any online "friend," picture or voice made them uncomfortable. Next week: a Family Code Word.

Reply slip (R-8.5): "We talked about real or fake. One thing we agreed on: ____."

Teacher notes#

  • Facts: The FTC has warned about scams that use AI to imitate voices of family members [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: consumer.ftc.gov]. Large surveys show many teens use AI chatbots (Pew, Dec 2025: 64 percent) and many have used AI companions (Common Sense, 2025: 72 percent) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05; this is teen data, not 8-10]. For this age, the main teaching is: chatbots are not people.
  • Avoid: "You can always tell by the fingers." Clues change quickly.
  • Do not demo chatbots with students unless your school has approved the tool and your district policy allows it.
  • Common kid questions: "Is my chatbot my friend?" It can be helpful, but it is not a person and cannot replace people. "Can AI pictures be used to hurt kids?" Yes; if anyone ever makes or shares a fake picture of you, it is never your fault; tell a grown-up. "Is Hackle a real AI?" No, Hackle is a cartoon creature.

Lesson 9: Family Code Word#

Lesson 9: Family Code Word
Grade band 3-5 (ages 8-10)
Strand B/F: Spot It and Fix It
Big idea A family code word and a callback rule help families verify that an urgent call, message or pick-up request is real.
Practiced skill Rehearse the callback script: "What's our code word? ... I'll call you back on the number I know."
Time 15 min: Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2 (the exit ticket is part of the 2-minute Takeaway; a longer written exit ticket needs 1-2 extra minutes). Take-home family activity follows.

Standards (approximate; [VERIFY] codes)#

  • ISTE: 2b Digital Citizen: safe behavior; 2d personal data (approximate)
  • CSTA: 1B-NI-05 protecting personal information (approximate) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017, rev.) text read; mapping approximate]
  • CASEL: Responsible Decision-Making; Relationship Skills (approximate)

Materials#

  • Unplugged: Call Script Cards (R-9.1), Family Code Word Plan card (R-9.2), pencils, practice word slips (blank).
  • Optional tech: none.

Privacy rule for this lesson: Never ask students to say, write or share their REAL family code word in class. In class they use a pretend "practice word" such as "banana" or "purple robot." The real word is decided at home and never shared with anyone outside the family.

What the code word is for#

  • A quick check when someone asks the child for help, money, a ride, or private information and says it is urgent or a secret.
  • A plan: if the caller cannot say the code word, hang up (or stop replying) and call back using a number you already know.

Good code words: Unusual (not a pet's name or a favorite team), easy to remember, not posted online, never typed in messages. Change it if it is ever shared.

The callback rule: If a message or call claims to be a family member and says it is urgent: do not send money, gift cards, passwords or pictures. Hang up. Call back using a number you already know. Tell a trusted adult.

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Remember Priya's grandma and the voice that sounded like her cousin? Priya's family now has a plan. It is a two-part secret. Part one: a code word that only the family knows. Part two: the callback rule. If anyone calls and says 'I'm your uncle, I need help right now,' you say, 'What's our code word?' If they do not know it, you say, 'I'm going to call you back on the number I know.' Then you hang up. That is not rude. That's smart.
A grown-up in your life, who really needs you, will not be upset that you checked. Today we'll practice."

Activity (8 min): The Callback Rehearsal#

Step 1 (1 min): Teach the two parts. Write on the board: CODE WORD + CALLBACK. Say: "Both parts together make the plan."

Step 2 (1 min): Choose a practice word. Each student writes a made-up practice word on a slip (not real, not private). Explain: "This is just for practice. The real one you will make with your family at home."

Step 3 (5 min): Pair role-play. In pairs, one is the Caller and one is the Kid. The Caller reads a Call Script Card (R-9.1). Examples: "Hi, it's your uncle! I'm stuck and need you to send me a gift card number right away. Don't tell your mom." The Kid replies: "What's our code word?" The Caller says, "Um, I forgot." The Kid: "I'm going to hang up and call you back on the number I know." Switch after each card. Do three cards each.

Step 4 (1 min): Share. Volunteers demonstrate in front of the class. Applaud the Kid's calm voice.

Fun Boost

Fun Boosters (pick ONE inside your 15 minutes; save the rest for flex weeks)

  • Energizer (30 seconds): "Callback Dance." Four beats: hang up (hands snap together), dial (finger taps), call back (phone to ear), tell (hand on heart, then out). Repeat, faster each time.
  • Game variant: "Pitch Your Practice Word." Groups invent a silly practice phrase and pitch why it is a good one (unusual, easy to remember, not posted online). The class votes for the most unguessable. Only practice words are ever used.
  • Chant: "What's our word? I'll hang up and see! / Call back the number I know for me. / Tell a grown-up, one, two, three!"
  • Silly voice / puppet moment: Let Callers read the money and gift-card cards (1, 3, 4, 5) in a cartoon voice (a pirate: "Arrr, it be yer uncle!"). The giggle teaches the real point: a voice can sound like anyone, so the code word and callback matter. Read the pick-up and private-channel cards (2, 6, 7, 8) in plain voices, with no characters.
  • Level-Up Challenge: Real Plan, Real Home. With a grown-up, make the real code word and callback plan, practice it once, and make a pick-up list (never write the word on the reply slip). Spiral: "Which of the 3 S's is a callback? (Second look, Lesson 8)"

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Why is it okay to hang up even if the caller sounds upset?
  2. What if a caller says "Don't tell your parents"? (That is a secret red flag. Tell.)
  3. Why should a code word never be typed or posted? (Because someone might read it and use it.)
  4. What if you forget the code word in real life? (Call back on a known number or ask a grown-up; do not guess.)
  5. Who in your family would you share the plan with?

Takeaway (2 min)#

Hackles line (say first): "A call that feels rushed or scary should make your hackles rise. Gut check, then use the code word. A false alarm is fine, and nobody is ever in trouble for checking."

Say: "Code word plus callback. If it's urgent, secret, or asks for money or gift cards: Pause. Check with the code word. Call back on a number you know. Tell a grown-up."

Practiced behavior: say "I'll call you back on the number I know" aloud.

Exit ticket (paper)#

"Finish the sentence: If someone says 'it's an emergency, don't tell anyone,' I will ____." (Activity Book p. 27.)

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (Lesson 9)
A student may reveal that their family is in crisis, that a call or message was threatening, that they cannot reach family or do not feel safe at home, or that there is no adult at home to use a code word.
Say: "Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. You are not in trouble. We'll find a trusted adult for you."
Don't say: "Your family should do better." Don't criticize the family. Don't promise secrecy.
Escalate: If the student says they are unsafe at home, or reveals abuse or neglect, report per your mandatory-reporting duty the same day and notify your counselor [Confirm your state and district rules with district counsel]. If a student says someone is threatening them or asking for money or images, treat it as a safety issue and follow the same procedure.
Reminder: Do not investigate. Notice that not every child has a stable family setup; offer other trusted adults (school counselor, teacher).

Differentiate#

  • Support: Provide a script strip with the lines. Let the Kid read. Reduce to one card.
  • Extension: Students write a two-line script for another call scenario (a ride pickup, a friend asking for their password) and rehearse.
  • No/low internet at home: Calls, texts and in-person pick-up requests also need code words. No device is needed to have a plan. Families who share one phone can still use a callback to a known number or ask another trusted adult.
  • Language access: The code word can be in any language. Families may choose a word, phrase or question. The take-home card is available in a home-language version (translate R-9.2).

Inclusion note: every family shape#

Some children live in two homes, with foster families, with grandparents, with a guardian, or with adults who work nights. The plan should fit your family, not a model. The family card says: "Each home can have its own plan." If a student does not have a safe adult to make a plan with, connect them with the school counselor.

Family card (take home) and reply slip#

Family Card, Lesson 9: Our Family Code Word (take-home activity)
Together, choose a code word that is unusual, easy to remember and not posted online. Agree on the callback rule: if anyone asks for urgent help, money, a ride or a secret, ask for the code word, or hang up and call back on a known number. Decide who is allowed to pick up your child. Practice once. Do not write your real code word on the reply slip.

Reply slip (R-9.3): "We made a family code word and callback plan. [ ] Yes [ ] Not yet. We practiced: [ ] Yes. We need help: ____." (No code word written.)

Teacher notes#

  • Facts: The FTC notes scammers can use AI to clone a voice from a short audio clip and pretend to be a family member in trouble; callbacks on a known number are the FTC's advice, and the FBI recommends a family secret word [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: consumer.ftc.gov; ic3.gov PSA241203].
  • Do not collect or record real code words. If a student blurts one out, gently say "Whoa, keep that secret; you may want to pick a new one at home."
  • Common kid questions: "What if I forget it?" Call back on a known number. "What if the caller is real and mad?" A real caller will understand.

Lesson 10: Oops Recovery#

Lesson 10: Oops Recovery
Grade band 3-5 (ages 8-10)
Strand F: Fix It
Big idea Everyone makes mistakes online. What matters is what you do next. There is always a step-by-step way to fix it, and you are never in trouble for telling.
Practiced skill Use the OOPS card: Oh, I'm okay - Off and out - People (tell) - Secure it.
Time 15 min: Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2 (the exit ticket is part of the 2-minute Takeaway; a longer written exit ticket needs 1-2 extra minutes). Optional celebration and certificates.

Standards (approximate; [VERIFY] codes)#

  • ISTE: 2b Digital Citizen: safe behavior (approximate)
  • CSTA: 1B-NI-05 protecting personal information; responding to cybersecurity problems (approximate) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017, rev.) text read; mapping approximate]
  • CASEL: Self-Management; Responsible Decision-Making (approximate)

Materials#

  • Unplugged: OOPS Recovery Step Card (R-10.1), Oops Scenario Cards (R-10.2, 12 cards), pencils, certificates (Activity Book).
  • Optional tech: none.

The OOPS Recovery Card#

The OOPS Recovery Card
Letter Step What to do
O Oh, I'm okay Take a slow breath. A mistake is not a disaster. You are not in trouble.
O Off and out Stop clicking. Close the page or app, or turn the screen off. Do not enter more information. Do not reply.
P People: tell Tell a trusted adult right away: "I made an oops and I need help."
S Secure it With a grown-up, change the password for anything you used, tell the app or site, and check for odd activity. If you shared money or private info, the grown-up decides next steps.

Common oops moments: clicked a strange link, shared a password with a friend, downloaded a "free cheat" or mod, got a scary pop-up saying your device is broken, answered a chat from someone unknown, accidentally sent a message or picture to the wrong place.

A message to every kid: "Computers make mistakes, and so do grown-ups. Clicking is not a crime. Hiding it is what makes it harder to fix."

Hook (2 min) - read aloud#

"Mateo was playing a game when a pop-up said 'Your tablet has a VIRUS! Call this number NOW!' He panicked and tapped it. A new page opened that asked for his password. He typed part of it, then stopped. He felt his heart pounding.
Mateo remembered the OOPS card. First: Oh, I'm okay. He took a breath. Off and out: he closed the page. People: he walked to the kitchen and said, 'I made an oops and I need help.' His abuela said, 'Gracias for telling me, mijo. Let's fix it together.' They changed his password and checked the account. Done.
Here's the thing: the oops was small because he told fast. The faster you tell, the easier the fix. Today we learn the step-by-step."

Activity (8 min): Fix-It Practice#

Step 1 (1 min): Teach the OOPS card. Show the poster. Teach hand motions: O (open both hands, 'I'm okay'), O (hands pushing away 'off and out'), P (hand to chest then out, 'people'), S (turn a pretend key, 'secure it').

Step 2 (1 min): Model one. Read an Oops Scenario Card (R-10.2) aloud and walk through each step on the board.

Step 3 (4 min): Triad rehearsal. Groups of three draw one Oops Scenario Card at a time. Teller: reads the card and says the first sentence to a grown-up ("I made an oops and I need help"). Grown-up: gives the Good Grown-up Reply ("Thank you for telling me. It's not your fault. Let's fix it together."). Observer: points to the OOPS step they heard. Rotate roles. Do three cards.

Step 4 (2 min): Grown-up reaction practice. Ask: "What if the grown-up looks upset?" Teach the line: "I know you're upset. I told you because I want to fix it." Remind: tell the next grown-up if the first one cannot help.

Fun Boost

Fun Boosters (pick ONE inside your 15 minutes; save the rest for flex weeks)

  • Energizer (30 seconds): "OOPS Moves." Do the four OOPS hand motions (open hands, push away, hand to chest and out, turn a key) as a speed round, three times, finishing with one big calm breath.
  • Game variant: "OOPS Order Relay" or "Tell or OK?" Teams race to arrange four jumbled OOPS step strips in the right order and say the first sentence aloud. Or use the Student Book page 31 puzzle: read a situation, students stand for Tell and sit for OK.
  • Chant (hand motions): "Oh, I'm okay! (breathe) Off and out! (push) / People, tell! (heart) Secure it, no doubt! (turn the key)"
  • Silly voice / puppet moment: Dramatic Hackle spills an imaginary juice and wails "It's the END of the WORLD!" The class calmly talks Hackle through O-O-P-S until Hackle breathes and says, "Oh, I'm okay!"
  • Level-Up Challenge: Teach the Unit. Pick your favorite lab and teach its one key move to a younger buddy in 60 seconds, or plan a "Greatest Hits" showcase where each group acts out one behavior from the one-page unit summary for others to guess. Spiral: "Which Ladder rung is People? (Rung 2)"

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Why does telling quickly make the fix easier?
  2. What if you are afraid the grown-up will take your device? (Say the sentence starter; we gave your family a card to help them respond kindly.)
  3. What can your grown-up do to help fix an account? (Change passwords, report to the app, check settings.)
  4. Why do we say "Oh, I'm okay" first?
  5. What would you tell a friend who made an oops?

Takeaway (2 min)#

Hackles line (say first): "Even if you ignored your hackles this time, or did not notice them until later, it is never your fault. Notice now, then tell someone. That is how oops becomes okay."

Say: "OOPS: Oh I'm okay. Off and out. People, tell. Secure it. Mistakes happen; fixes are possible. Pause. Check. Tell."

Practiced behavior: everyone says, "I made an oops and I need help," out loud.

Exit ticket (paper)#

"Write the four OOPS steps in your own words." (Activity Book p. 30.)

Celebration (optional, +5-10 min): Hand out Hands-On Hacker certificates (Activity Book). Ask students to name a favorite lab and one new habit.

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (Lesson 10)
A student may reveal they sent money or a picture, gave someone a password, or are being threatened after a mistake. This lesson has a high chance of disclosure.
Say: "Thank you for telling me. I am so glad you did. It is not your fault. You are not in trouble. I believe you. We will get help."
Don't say: "Why did you do that?" "I can't believe you did that." Do not promise secrecy. Do not ask to see pictures, and never view, copy or forward images of a child. Do not tell them to delete evidence or pay anything.
Escalate: Same day: follow your mandatory-reporting duty and district protocol. Report to child protective services or law enforcement as required by your state [Confirm your state and district rules with district counsel]. If a threat or sexual images are involved, a CyberTipline report can be made at report.cybertip.org / 1-800-843-5678, and the student can learn about NCMEC's Take It Down (takeitdown.ncmec.org) with a counselor [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: takeitdown.ncmec.org]. Remind the student not to pay or send more and not to delete messages. If the student is distressed or mentions self-harm, follow your crisis protocol (988) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org].
Reminder: Do not investigate. Document the student's own words only.

Differentiate#

  • Support: Offer the picture version of the OOPS card. Use a buddy reader. Use only 6 cards.
  • Extension: Students design a one-page "Oops Kit" poster with the four steps for a younger class.
  • No/low internet at home: If a student used a shared or library device, the steps still apply: close the page, tell the adult in charge, and let them decide next steps. Without a personal account, there may be nothing to "secure," and that is fine.
  • Language access: The OOPS letters work in English; create a home-language acronym or use icons. Say the sentence "I made an oops and I need help" in home languages first.

Family card (take home) and reply slip#

Family Card, Lesson 10: Oops Recovery
Your child learned a four-step plan: Oh I'm okay, Off and out, People, Secure it. The most powerful thing you can do is respond calmly when your child tells you about a mistake: "Thank you for telling me. Let's fix it together." If a child fears punishment, they stop telling. Try the plan at home: change a password together, review settings. Please avoid taking away devices as a response to telling.
Where to get help (US) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: hotline details checked on the organizations' own sites; reportfraud.ftc.gov is linked from consumer.ftc.gov]: your child's school counselor; NCMEC CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org, 1-800-843-5678); 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline for emotional crises; reportfraud.ftc.gov for scams.

Reply slip (R-10.3): "We practiced Oops Recovery. [ ] Yes. We will respond to mistakes calmly by: ____."

Teacher notes#

  • Facts: Common recovery steps for compromised accounts include changing the password, signing out other sessions, enabling two-step verification, and reporting [consistent with CISA's Secure Our World habits, VERIFIED 2026-10-05; steps vary by service, so check the provider's help page]. Always involve an adult. Do not give detailed technical instructions.
  • Why it matters: Prompt help-seeking and calm adult responses are central; shame is a frequent barrier for children (UNICEF Disrupting Harm; Thorn) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: unicef.org/innocenti; thorn.org].
  • Common kid questions: "What if I already clicked?" You are fine; tell. "Will it explode my computer?" Usually no; adults can check. "What if I did something I wasn't allowed to do?" Honesty is brave, and the fix is the same.
  • Unit wrap-up: Collect the post-survey from the Assessment Pack; hold a showcase (optional), and send the final family letter.
