About Hackles and Competence Collective#

Hackles is a free K-12 online safety and cybersecurity kit made by Competence Collective. Its core idea is "hackles": the feeling on the back of your neck when something is off. It teaches children to notice that feeling and act on it: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. Because the kit is free, it spreads through people, not purchases. You are how it spreads. Feedback and questions: [CONTACT: to be added].

Pathway note (adult-facing only). Hackles is the first step on a learning path that continues with free resources such as picoCTF and CYBER.ORG, and for college and professional learners tools like Competence Collective's Red Herrings (redherrings.app) and Hotwash (hotwa.sh). This note is for adults and is never shown to students.

1. What a Hackles Mentor is (and is not)#

Try this now (5 minutes): read Micro-PD Session 1 once, aloud, and decide who you would ask to co-lead it with you. A mentor is made by running one 15-minute session, not by reading this guide.

A Hackles Mentor is#

  • A colleague who has taught Hackles lessons and enjoys helping others do it.
  • Someone who can run a 15-minute micro-PD and, with support, a 90-minute or half-day workshop.
  • A coach who uses the rubric "Is the lesson fun, safe, skill-based?" with warmth.
  • A safe adult who listens when a colleague has a worry.
  • A bridge between teachers, the counselor and the principal.

A Hackles Mentor is not#

  • A cybersecurity expert. You do not need to know how networks work. You need to know the routine and the rules.
  • A lawyer. You never give legal advice. You say "[VERIFY] with our administrator and counsel."
  • A therapist or investigator. You listen, reassure, report and hand off.
  • An evaluator. Mentoring is separate from evaluation. You never report on a colleague's teaching to administration without that colleague's consent. [VERIFY with your contract.]
  • Alone. Mentors work in pairs and in a circle.

2. The Hackles learning path: five levels#

2. The Hackles learning path: five levels
Level Name What you can do How you get there
1 Participant Say and use the routine, respond to a child who tells you something, and know your help map Attend the 90-Minute or Half-Day Workshop
2 Practitioner Teach Hackles lessons with confidence, including a sensitive lesson with counselor support Teach at least 3 lessons (including 1 in a band you have not taught); be observed once with H13; attend 3 micro-PD sessions
3 Hackles Mentor Lead micro-PD sessions; observe and coach colleagues; run a Mentor Circle Complete the Mentor Course (Section 4); co-lead two micro-PD sessions; do two peer observations; pass the teach-back
4 Lead Facilitator Run the 90-Minute and Half-Day Workshops, and Parent Night Co-facilitate a workshop with a Lead Facilitator; run one with a safety partner; be observed with the rubric in Section 6
5 Trainer of Mentors Run the Mentor Course for other buildings or districts Lead the Mentor Course once with support; be observed; join the cross-school Mentor Circle

Competency checklists#

Level 1 Participant

  • Checkbox: I can say the routine and do the four hand signals.
  • Checkbox: I can say "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble."
  • Checkbox: I know three things never to do in a disclosure (promise secrecy, ask "why did you," look at an intimate image).
  • Checkbox: I filled in my Help Map (H7).
  • Checkbox: I made one commitment (H15).

Level 2 Practitioner

  • Checkbox: I have taught 3 lessons using Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2.
  • Checkbox: I used the calm opening and closing.
  • Checkbox: I sent the pre-notice and the counselor heads-up for a sensitive lesson, or watched a colleague do so.
  • Checkbox: I was observed once and got two glows and a grow.
  • Checkbox: I observed a colleague once.

Level 3 Hackles Mentor

  • Checkbox: I completed the Mentor Course.
  • Checkbox: I led two micro-PD sessions and was rated "Got it" on most items on the facilitation rubric.
  • Checkbox: I observed and coached two colleagues with warmth and specifics.
  • Checkbox: I can lead a disclosure role-play with safety rails (with a safety partner).
  • Checkbox: I know the 10 non-negotiables by heart.
  • Checkbox: I know the limits of my role.

Level 4 Lead Facilitator

  • Checkbox: I co-facilitated a half-day workshop.
  • Checkbox: I ran one with a safety partner, on time.
  • Checkbox: I handled a "teacher shares an incident" situation (or practiced it).
  • Checkbox: I ran Parent Night with a counselor.
  • Checkbox: I was observed using the rubric in Section 6.

Level 5 Trainer of Mentors

  • Checkbox: I led the Mentor Course once.
  • Checkbox: I trained at least three mentors who then ran sessions.
  • Checkbox: I joined the cross-school Mentor Circle.

3. Who makes a good mentor#

Look for people who:

  • Enjoy teaching adults and will not talk for an hour when five minutes will do.
  • Listen and stay calm. A mentor's calm is contagious.
  • Respect confidentiality without fail.
  • Admit what they do not know and say "let's find out."
  • Like fun: games, props, a silly hat.
  • Care about every child, including the quiet ones and the ones who are hard to love.
  • Are trusted by colleagues.
  • Can receive feedback with grace.

Mentors can be teachers, paraprofessionals, librarians, counselors, tech coordinators, instructional coaches or administrators. The best mentor teams have more than one role (for example a counselor and a teacher, or a librarian and a tech coordinator).

How to invite someone: "I noticed how you handled the Phish Derby. Would you consider helping lead the next staff session? I'll coach you."

4. The 4-session Mentor Course (8 hours)#

Four sessions of two hours each, spaced over four weeks. Run by a Lead Facilitator or Trainer of Mentors, with a counselor for Session B. Small group (4 to 12 people). The course models the very methods it teaches: movement, games, role-play, peer feedback.

Before the course#

  • Checkbox: Participants have attended the Half-Day or 90-Minute Workshop.
  • Checkbox: Each participant has the Facilitator Kit and the Teacher Support Pack.
  • Checkbox: A counselor is booked for Session B.
  • Checkbox: Rooms, materials and snacks are ready.

Session A (2 hours): Facilitation fundamentals and model-the-method#

Outcome: Mentors can run a 15-minute micro-PD that is fun, safe and skill-based.

Session A (2 hours): Facilitation fundamentals and model-the-method
Min Activity Method modeled
0:00-0:10 Welcome and Hackles Up check-in. One word about how you feel about leading others. Opening ritual
0:10-0:25 The shape of a session: Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2. Teach it with a 2-minute demo of Micro-PD Session 1. Modeled lesson
0:25-0:45 Build a hook: each mentor writes a 30-second hook for a micro-PD session. Share in trios. Peer feedback
0:45-1:05 "Pull back the curtain": name the teaching move after each activity. Mentors practice saying it. Metacognition
1:05-1:15 Break
1:15-1:50 Practice facilitation: each mentor leads 3 minutes of a Micro-PD Session activity. The others play teachers. Feedback: two glows and a grow (H12). Micro-teach
1:50-2:00 Takeaway and homework: read Micro-PD Session 3 and prepare to lead the Hook and Activity. Closing

Homework: run one 5-minute "mini-session" with a colleague (any activity). Write one thing you learned.

Session B (2 hours): Safety, disclosure and the hard moments#

Counselor present for the whole session.

Session B (2 hours): Safety, disclosure and the hard moments
Min Activity Method modeled
0:00-0:10 Welcome and agreements (fictional only, pass allowed, step out allowed, traffic-light cards) Safety rails
0:10-0:30 The First 10 Minutes in depth. The counselor walks through H6, the Disclosure and Incident Guide, and the honest limit on secrecy. Q and A. Expert voice
0:30-1:05 Mentor-led disclosure role-play. Mentors practice leading the Segment 6 role-play: the framing, demo, traffic-light cards and de-role. One mentor leads, one plays the safety partner, one observes with a checklist. Rotate. Role-play with rails
1:05-1:15 Break
1:15-1:50 When a teacher shares their own incident. Practice the script from the Facilitator Guide Section 8 in trios. Each person plays the facilitator, the teacher and the observer. Role-play
1:50-2:00 Self-care and takeaway. The counselor leads a breath. Every mentor names a person they will talk to if they need support. Closing

Homework: fill in a personal "Safety Plan for Mentors": who is my safety partner? what is my reporting route? who is my debrief person? [VERIFY]

Session C (2 hours): Coaching with the rubric#

Outcome: Mentors can observe and coach with "Is the lesson fun, safe, skill-based?"

Session C (2 hours): Coaching with the rubric
Min Activity Method modeled
0:00-0:10 Opening game: Hackles Simon Says. Movement

Min Activity

After the course#

  • Each mentor co-leads two micro-PD sessions with a colleague before leading alone.
  • Each mentor observes two colleagues (with H13).
  • Each mentor joins the Mentor Circle (Section 7).

5. The 10 non-negotiables (fidelity checklist)#

These are the rules that protect children and keep the program trustworthy. A mentor never trades these away for convenience, a good laugh or an eager audience.

5. The 10 non-negotiables (fidelity checklist)
# Non-negotiable Why
1 No scare tactics. No alarming statistics or frightening stories as hooks. Fear does not protect; skills do.
2 No demonstrations of attacks. We teach recognition and response. We do not teach harm.
3 Fictional cases only. Nobody tells stories about real children. Privacy, dignity and safety.
4 Pass is always allowed; stepping out needs no explanation. Trauma-informed practice.
5 The honest limit on secrecy is stated. We never promise secrecy we cannot keep. Honesty protects trust.
6 A counselor is involved in every sensitive lesson and in every disclosure role-play. Safety and support.
7 "Never your fault" and "tell, no trouble" are said in every session and lesson. Shame is the biggest obstacle.
8 [VERIFY] flags are respected. We confirm laws, numbers and policies locally. Accuracy.
9 No student accounts and no student data. We do not add tools that collect them. Privacy.
10 No marketing to children. Pathway notes appear only in adult-facing materials. Credit and license are kept. Integrity.

Use this list at the start of every Mentor Circle: "Anyone have a non-negotiable they want to talk through?"

6. The facilitation rubric: Is the training fun, safe, skill-based?#

This is the Peer Coaching rubric adapted for facilitators of adult training. Use it when you observe a micro-PD, workshop or Parent Night.

Rating key: N = Not yet, G = Getting there, Y = Got it.

FUN#

FUN
Look-for Evidence N G Y
F1. Within 3 minutes, most participants are moving, talking or hands-on.
F2. Humor and energy are warm and inclusive. The facilitator uses a prop, a game or a silly voice.
F3. Participants have roles (captain, reader, observer), not just listening.
F4. The pace is brisk. Segments end on time.

SAFE#

SAFE
Look-for Evidence N G Y
S1. No scare tactics or shaming.
S2. Group agreements are stated. Pass and step-out are offered.
S3. Content is fictional. No real stories.
S4. For sensitive content, the safety partner is present, rails are used, and de-role is done.
S5. The honest limit on secrecy is stated when relevant.
S6. The facilitator handled a real disclosure or incident correctly (if one occurred).
S7. [VERIFY] flags are mentioned, not ignored.

SKILL-BASED#

SKILL-BASED
Look-for Evidence N G Y
K1. One clear skill is practiced by participants.
K2. The session ends with one takeaway and Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell.
K3. The facilitator "pulls back the curtain" and names the teaching move.
K4. Participants can use the skill by Friday.

Facilitation craft#

Facilitation craft
Look-for Evidence N G Y
C1. Scripts are read in a natural voice.
C2. The facilitator says "I do not know, let's find out" when needed.
C3. Dominant voices are managed; quiet voices are invited.
C4. Time is managed with the chime, not shouting.
C5. Access needs are anticipated (seated options, large print, interpreter).

Using the rubric: two glows and a grow, as always. No scores are compared. Observation is for growth.

7. The Mentor Circle (monthly, 45 minutes)#

A Mentor Circle keeps mentors sharp, supported and connected. Hold it monthly in-building or across several schools.

Agenda#

Agenda
Min Segment What happens
0-5 Opener Hackles Up signal and a one-word check-in. Read one non-negotiable aloud.
5-15 Win and worry Each mentor shares one win and one worry (no identifying details). The circle responds with "Thank you. Here's an idea."
15-30 Practice Choose one: a role-play (a teacher shares an incident), a micro-teach of a new lesson, or a hard-question practice (using the Teacher FAQ). Use fictional scenarios only.
30-38 Share a resource One mentor shares a tool, a script, a game they tried or a [VERIFY] they resolved.
38-43 Plan Each mentor names one thing they will do before the next circle.
43-45 Takeaway and pledge A one-sentence takeaway. The Hackles pledge.

Circle ground rules#

  • Confidential. What we share about teachers stays here. (Exception: if a child may be in danger, we follow policy.)
  • No student names. No teacher names without permission.
  • Practice, don't preach.
  • Laugh.
  • Support each other's wellbeing.

Practice scenarios (all fictional)#

  1. A teacher interrupts the workshop to say the whole program is "scaremongering."
  2. A teacher in the role-play breaks down and says "That happened to my niece."
  3. A principal wants you to share who is struggling.
  4. A parent at Parent Night says "Tell us the scary stuff."
  5. A teacher wants to demonstrate how to hack a classmate's account "so they get it."
  6. The counselor cannot come to the disclosure block.
  7. A colleague asks you to share your observation notes with the principal.
  8. A volunteer wants to share a real story about a child they know.

For each: what do you say? Who do you call? What do you not do?

9. Facilitation skills for non-experts#

The five habits#

  1. Start with a hook, not a slide. A question, a prop, a laugh.
  2. Talk less than five minutes at a time. Then get people moving, talking or making something.
  3. Ask, don't tell. "What did you notice?" works better than "Here is what to notice."
  4. Pull back the curtain. Name the teaching move out loud so teachers can steal it.
  5. End with one takeaway and the signals.

Scripts for common moments#

Scripts for common moments
Moment Say
You do not know the answer "I do not know. Let's find out. I'll write it on the Parking Lot and get back to you by [date]."
Someone dominates "Thank you. I would like to hear from someone who has not spoken yet."
Someone challenges the program "I hear you. Tell me more about your concern." (Then listen. Then: "Here's what the kit says about that.")
A legal question "I'm not a lawyer. Let me get you an answer from [administrator or counsel]. [VERIFY]"
A participant is upset Pause. Offer the Reset Corner. Safety partner steps over.
Someone shares a real incident See the Facilitator Guide, Section 8: thank, pause, privately, honest limit, no probing, safety partner, follow-up in 24 hours.
Time is short Use the flex plan; never cut the agreements, the de-role or the H6 review.
Energy is low Stand up, Simon Says, a new voice, a new music track.

Fun on purpose: a mentor's toolkit#

  • A silly hat, a stuffed toy "Hackle," a pickle trophy.
  • A chime for transitions.
  • A scoreboard on the wall.
  • A "Phisher" cameo.
  • Team names (The Hackle Hecklers, Pause Palm Pirates).
  • A 90-second movement break.
  • Stickers.

What not to do#

  • Do not read slides aloud.
  • Do not lecture for more than five minutes.
  • Do not embarrass anyone.
  • Do not take over an incident or a conversation.
  • Do not promise outcomes.
  • Do not put anyone on the spot to share personal experience.

10. Handling hard moments (quick guide)#

10. Handling hard moments (quick guide)
Situation Immediate move Follow-up
A participant shares a personal or student incident Thank, pause the group, move privately with the safety partner, state the honest limit Check in within 24 hours; follow the Disclosure Guide if a child may be involved
A participant becomes visibly distressed Offer the Reset Corner and water; the safety partner steps over Private check-in later
A participant misuses the role-play (real story, laughing at the cards) Show red: "Let's pause. Let's use the card." Short private word if needed
A participant demands a hacking demo "We teach recognition and response, not attacks." Offer picoCTF or CYBER.ORG for their own learning
A participant says "kids already know all this" "Some do. Do they know what to say to an adult? Let's find out together." Offer an observation
An administrator asks about a teacher's participation "I keep that confidential. I can share the group's themes." Document the boundary
A teacher reports a possible harm to a child Do not investigate. Follow the reporting route and the Disclosure Guide immediately. Document facts only
A teacher teaches a sensitive lesson without notice Talk privately and kindly; involve the counselor Revisit the timeline
You are exhausted Hand off to a co-mentor; talk to your debrief person Adjust your schedule

Ethics for mentors#

  • Confidentiality. What teachers tell you about their practice and their lives stays confidential, except where a child may be in danger and the law or policy requires action. [VERIFY]
  • Dignity. Treat every colleague as a capable professional.
  • No student data. Do not collect names, accounts, scores or device details. Do not accept student data from others.
  • No marketing to children. The pathway note belongs in adult-facing materials only.
  • Honesty. Mark uncertain facts as [VERIFY]. Correct errors quickly.
  • Inclusion. Swap names and examples so everyone is represented; use "a grown-up you trust."
  • Safety first. If a child may be in danger, follow the reporting route before anything else.
  • Humility. You are not the expert on everything.

13. Self-care for mentors#

Mentoring on these topics can carry weight. Take care of yourself.

  • Have a debrief person (a counselor, a trusted colleague) and use them.
  • Know your EAP and use it. [VERIFY]
  • Say no when you are overloaded. Pass a session to a co-mentor.
  • If you are a survivor yourself, ask a colleague to lead the sensitive parts. You never have to prove anything.
  • Rest and laugh. The Mentor Circle should be restorative.
  • Celebrate. Keep a "Win Wall" of notes from teachers.
