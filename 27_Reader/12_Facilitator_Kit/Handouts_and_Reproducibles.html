How to use this file#

  • Try this now (5 minutes): print H3 and H7 first and fill in H7 with the counselor. If you print nothing else, those two cards carry the day.
  • Print one copy of the Print Plan (below) and tape it to your supply box.
  • Cards are written as quoted boxes. Print, then cut along each box. Print card sets on colored paper so sets never mix (suggested colors in the Print Plan).
  • Everything in the card sets is fictional. No real brands, real people, real schools or real accounts appear. Names like "Zorbly" and "GemCoins" are made up on purpose.
  • Anything marked [VERIFY] must be checked against your own state law, district policy and current official sources before you print.
  • Fill in the blanks on H7 (My School's Help Map) before the workshop. A card with blanks is a card nobody can use at 2 p.m. on a bad day.

H2. Group Agreements Poster and Team Scorecard#

Group Agreements (post on the wall; read aloud at the start)#

OUR HACKLES AGREEMENTS

  1. We work with fictional cases, not real children. No names, no "a student I had once," no guessing who a case is about.
  2. Pass is always allowed. Say "pass" or hold up the green card. No explanation needed.
  3. Step out any time. Some of this material can touch real life. Leave, stretch, get water, find the counselor. Nobody asks why. We will check on you kindly later.
  4. No shame, no gotchas. Every question is welcome. Every mistake is practice.
  5. What we share about ourselves stays here. The one exception: if a child may be in danger or being hurt, we get help, and the law and our policy decide how.
  6. We have fun on purpose. Laughter is a teaching tool. We laugh with, not at.
  7. We keep our phones in our pockets. Texting is for the break.

H3. Pause-Check-Tell and Hackles-Up Card#

Print front and back on goldenrod card stock. Cut to wallet size. Also enlarge to poster size.

Front#

HACKLES UP?
Something feels off: too good, pushy, secret, or scary.
Hand signal: raise a hand and brush it up the back of your neck.

PAUSE: Stop. Breathe. Do not click, reply, pay, or send yet. (Flat palm like a stop sign.)

CHECK: Who is it really? What do they want? How fast? Check a different way than the message tells you to. (Circle fingers like a magnifying glass at your eye.)

TELL: A trusted person, every time, no shame. Keep telling until someone helps. (Hand on chest, then open palm outward.)

Back#

A FALSE ALARM IS FINE.
Noticing is the skill. Nobody gets points for being right every time.

IF YOU MISSED IT OR GOT TRICKED, IT IS NEVER YOUR FAULT. Tell someone.

WHEN A CHILD TELLS YOU:
"Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble."

THREE THINGS TO NEVER DO: promise secrecy, ask "why did you...?", look at or forward an intimate image.

Trust your hackles. Pause · Check · Tell.

H4. SPOT and FLAGS Quick Reference Card#

SPOT is used in grades 3-5. FLAGS is used in grades 6-8. Teens and adults use the Pause-Check-Tell decision habit. All three describe the same signals.

H4. SPOT and FLAGS Quick Reference Card
Signal SPOT (grades 3-5) FLAGS (grades 6-8) In adult words
Secrecy or asking for private things Secrets asked Login ask, Gotta keep it secret Wants a password, code, address, picture, or says "don't tell"
Pressure Pressure Fast Deadline, threat, "last chance"
Too good Offer too good Amazing offer Free money, prize, special deal, flattery
Moves you somewhere Trick to move you Strange sender Click this, move to another app, buy gift cards, unfamiliar or lookalike sender

Rule of thumb: one signal means Pause and check. Two or more means probably a trick.

How to Check safely (the "own door" rule): do not use the link, number or contact in the message. Open the app or site yourself, call a number you already had, or ask the person face to face.

If you already clicked: that is an oops, not a disaster. Stop, tell someone, and change the password from a safe device (and anywhere else you used it). If you typed in a card number, call your bank on the number printed on the card. Turn on two-step verification. Oops Recovery is a lesson in grades 3-5 because everyone needs it.

H5. Say This, Not That Card#

H5. Say This, Not That Card
Say this Not that Why
"Thank you for telling me." "Why did you do that?" "Why" sounds like blame. Thanking makes the next telling easier.
"This is not your fault." "You should have known better." Shame is the biggest reason kids stay silent.
"You are not in trouble." "I'm going to have to take your phone." Fear of losing a device stops kids from telling.
"I can't keep this secret, because I want to get you help. I will tell you who I tell." "I won't tell anyone." You cannot promise secrecy. Honesty builds trust.
"Tell me more." / "What happened next?" Detailed who, what, why questioning Open questions only. Investigators investigate.
"Good gut check. Hackles up is never wrong to say." "That's nothing, don't worry." Praise the noticing, including false alarms.
"Keep telling until someone helps." "Just tell your teacher." One adult may not help. We teach persistence.
"A grown-up you trust." "Your parents." Not every child has parents at home.
"That sounds like a trick, and these tricks fool everyone." "Only careless kids fall for that." Anyone can be tricked.
"I'm not sure. Let's find out." Guessing Honest uncertainty models Check.

H6. First 10 Minutes Card (disclosure)#

A short version of the First 10 Minutes card in the Disclosure and Incident Guide (Teacher Support Pack, Document 3). Your district policy and state law come first. [VERIFY]

H6. First 10 Minutes Card (disclosure)
Do Do not
Stay calm. Soften your voice. Keep your face neutral. Show shock, disgust, anger or panic.
Move somewhere private that is still visible to other adults. Be alone behind a closed door with a child.
Say: "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble." Say: "Why did you...?" "How could you...?"
Listen. Use open prompts: "Tell me more." Ask leading questions or press for detail.
Be honest: "I can't keep this a secret. I will tell you who I tell." Promise secrecy or promise outcomes.
Write down the child's exact words afterward. Investigate, confront anyone, or contact the person involved.
Report personally and promptly as law and policy require. Assume telling the principal satisfies your duty. [VERIFY]
If a device holds images: do not look, copy, forward or screenshot. Secure it per policy. Ask to see the images.
If the child may harm themselves: stay with them, call the counselor or administrator, follow your crisis protocol, 988 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org]. Leave a child at risk alone.

In front of the class? "Thank you. Let's talk about this in a moment, just us." Then keep going calmly, and meet privately the same day with another adult aware.

S1. Passphrase Pit (Station Host card)#

Key message: Length beats cleverness. Random beats clever. A long random passphrase is easy to remember and hard to guess.

Materials: Word cards W1 to W40 (below), a timer, scrap paper, the Crack-Timer Chart (below). No real passwords are ever used. Say it out loud: "These are pretend."

Steps

  1. Each player draws four word cards at random without looking. Lay them in a row. That is your passphrase. Say it out loud.
  2. Look at the Crack-Timer Chart. How long would a computer guessing at random take for each kind of password? Read the row aloud.
  3. Now the challenge: your team builds the silliest-yet-longest passphrase from 6 cards. Say it as a team in a silly voice. (Remember: our small deck is for fun.)
  4. Compare with the cards labelled "Clever" (see below). Why does the clever one lose?
  5. Say the key message to the host in your own words.

Word cards W1 to W40 (print as small cards): pickle, tractor, glitter, volcano, noodle, trombone, pancake, lantern, walrus, cactus, marble, bubble, compass, jellybean, rocket, mitten, giraffe, sneaker, thunder, pretzel, sailboat, wobble, popcorn, mustard, spaghetti, kazoo, igloo, banjo, hammock, turnip, yodel, waffle, button, pirate, crayon, pudding, scooter, meteor, ukulele, zeppelin

Crack-Timer Chart (illustrative only; actual times depend on the attack and the system) [VERIFY with a current source before presenting as fact]

How these numbers were made: the worst case, in which a thief has stolen a site's password file and guesses offline at 10 billion guesses per second, trying every possibility. "Random characters" means picked at random from the 94 keyboard symbols. "Random words" means picked at random from a large 7,776-word list. Times shown are for trying every possibility; on average a thief finds it in half that.

Read the chart honestly. (1) Each extra random word multiplies the work by about 7,776, so length is what matters. (2) A password manager can make and remember long random passwords for you, so that is the best answer for most accounts. (3) A four-word passphrase is a fair minimum for everyday accounts; use five or six for your email and for a password manager. (4) Well-run sites make real guessing far slower than this chart, and most limit guesses, so these are worst-case numbers. (5) Our 40-word card deck is for fun only. Four words from 40 cards is only 2,560,000 possibilities, which a computer tries in a blink. Real passphrases come from a password manager or a big word list.

H11. Disclosure Role-Play Cards DR1 to DR7#

SAFETY RAILS (read before you play): this is practice, not therapy.

  • All cards are fictional. Do not tell a story from your own life or from a real child. If you slip into one, say "traffic light red" and stop. Nobody asks why.
  • Everyone has Traffic-Light Cards: GREEN = I'm fine, keep going. YELLOW = slow down, I need a minute. RED = pause the scene right now. Anyone can show a card at any time, no explanation.
  • Nobody has to take part. Observers are a full role.
  • No points. No timers shown. No audience. Practice in trios, quietly, spread around the room.
  • The facilitator and a counselor (or designated adult) are in the room or just outside. Use H7 to find them.
  • When the scene ends, everyone says "I'm [real name], that was pretend," and shakes out their hands.

The three roles#

  • Teller: reads the card, then stays in character. Does not invent graphic details. If the listener asks for more than the card says, the teller says "I don't want to say more right now."
  • Listener: a teacher or staff member who practices the skills on the Observer Checklist.
  • Observer: follows the Observer Checklist, notes what went well, and calls the time.

H12. Micro-Teach Cards MT1 to MT12 and Feedback Sheet#

Yellow paper. Each card is a 5-minute "speed" version of a 15-minute Hackles lesson (Hook 1, Activity 2:30, Takeaway 1:30). These cards intentionally avoid the sensitive lessons listed in the Counselor Heads-Up and Opt-Out Toolkit: sensitive lessons are never micro-taught in a workshop. How a speed-teach works#

Time versions: the Half-Day uses the full 5 minutes below. The 90-Minute Version uses 3 minutes (Hook 30 seconds, Activity 1:45, Takeaway 45 seconds). Micro-PD Session 8 uses 90 seconds (the Hook plus the start of the Activity).

  1. Hook (1 min). Say the hook line from the card in a voice that wakes the room up.
  2. Activity (2 min 30 s). Run the one-sentence activity. Everyone on their feet or hands busy.
  3. Takeaway (1 min 30 s). Say the takeaway line, then lead the Hackles-up hand signal and "Pause. Check. Tell."
  4. Audience plays students (silly is fine; nobody plays a real child's real experience). Feedback follows on the Feedback Sheet.

Activity: Each "student" draws a picture card from three bags (animal, food, action) and makes a pretend password. Say it aloud once, whisper it once, then put it in a paper lock. Pretend only.
