About Hackles#

Hackles is a free K-12 online safety and cybersecurity kit (quillsup.org), built on one idea. Your hackles are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off, and children can learn to notice that feeling and act on it: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. Every lesson takes 15 minutes, runs on paper and pencil, and comes with word-for-word scripts, which is why a principal, counselor, librarian or tech coordinator can use this Facilitator Kit to run the teacher training with no cybersecurity background at all. Feedback and corrections: [CONTACT: to be added].

Pathway note (adult-facing only). Hackles is the first step on a path that continues with free resources such as picoCTF and CYBER.ORG. This note is for adults and is never shown to students.

1. Start here: what this workshop is#

In one paragraph#

Three hours, a room of teachers, and ideally a few support staff. The workshop works by making teachers do the things they will ask students to do. Nobody sits through a lecture on how to teach active lessons, which would be a strange way to make the point.

Learning outcomes#

By the end of the workshop, participants will:

  1. Explain why skills-based teaching beats scare tactics and one-off assemblies, and describe the Hackles routine in their own words.
  2. Sort real-looking messages into Hackles Up or Hackles Down, naming the clue and the next move (Pause, Check, Tell).
  3. Run a 15-minute Hackles lesson structure: Hook (2), Activity (8), Discuss (3), Takeaway (2).
  4. Respond to a child's disclosure using the First 10 Minutes steps, including the honest limit on secrecy.
  5. Use their own school's help map (H7) and reporting route.
  6. Observe a lesson with the rubric "Is the lesson fun, safe, skill-based?"

The rules of the road (for you, the facilitator)#

  • Model the methods. Every segment uses a teaching method teachers can steal: movement, competition, role-play, stations, peer feedback. After each segment, spend 15 to 30 seconds on "Pull back the curtain" and name the move you just used.
  • Fun and rigor are not opposites. Games make the practice stick, and rigor makes the games worth playing.
  • No scare tactics. Ever. Don't open with alarming statistics or frightening stories. It's the most common mistake in online-safety training. The kit's research note says that skills-based, repeated teaching is more promising than one-off scare assemblies (Finkelhor and colleagues, 2021 [citation VERIFIED 2026-10-05: Finkelhor, Walsh, Jones, Mitchell and Collier, Trauma, Violence, & Abuse 22(5), 1233-1247; that review found mismatches between program messages and research; the "skills-based, repeated teaching" idea is general prevention-education practice, so do not attribute it to that paper alone]).
  • No demonstrations of attacks. We teach recognition and response. Don't show participants how to build a phish, crack a password, or find a child's private information.
  • Nobody tells a story about a real child. Everything is fictional. That protects privacy, protects children, and protects the room.
  • Every active segment has an off-ramp. Anyone can pass, sit out or step out, and nobody has to explain.
  • You are not the expert on everything. Say "I do not know. Let's find out" at least once, on purpose. That is the Check step in action.
  • You are not a lawyer. Anything about reporting duties, notice, opt-out or discipline comes with [VERIFY], so send participants to your administrator and district counsel. This kit is educational guidance, not legal advice.

2. Your team and the roles#

You don't have to run this alone, and it goes better if you don't. The best version has three people. A solo version is described afterward.

2. Your team and the roles
Role Who often does this well Jobs
Lead facilitator Principal, assistant principal, instructional coach, or librarian Runs the agenda, reads the scripts, owns the room's energy
Safety partner School counselor (strongly recommended) or school psychologist or social worker Co-opens Segment 6, is in the room or just outside for the whole disclosure block, handles any participant who needs support, checks the First 10 Minutes content, answers local policy questions
Tech and logistics Tech coordinator or library aide Slides, music, timer, printing, name tents, snacks, scoreboard (totals scores during Segments 8 and 9), collects exit tickets

If you are the only person: run the 90-Minute Version, or run this one with the counselor on call by phone and next door during Segment 6, a participant running the timer, and three stations. Don't skip the counselor arrangement. Ask the counselor: "Can you join us for Segment 6 (1:40-2:10)? If not, can you be reachable, and where can participants who need support go?"

A note on you. You may be a survivor of something, or the parent of a child who went through something. This material can touch real life. You're allowed to hand a segment to your safety partner or step out, and you should plan now who covers for you.

4. Room setup#

The layout (up to 30 participants)#

The layout (up to 30 participants)
Zone What goes there
Front wall Slide screen and the big poster: HACKLES UP? PAUSE. CHECK. TELL.
Side walls Parking Lot (sticky notes for questions); Group Agreements poster (H2)
Middle 4 to 6 team tables, 5 or 6 chairs each, each with a team sign. They double as stations 1 to 3.
Station 4 Help Ladder: tape three paper rungs on the floor
Back wall Snack table, sign-in table with H1, name tents, Reset Corner
Open wall (8 by 15 feet) Space for the Line-Up and Simon Says. Tape a "Hackles Down" sign at one end and a "Hackles Up" sign at the other, 12 feet apart.
  • Scoreboard (whiteboard or poster) with team names and a sticky-note "Hackle" mascot.
  • Reset Corner: a chair and water, with the sign "Use any time. No explanation needed." Keep the safety partner nearby.
  • Segment 6: spread trios across the room so conversations are private. If the room is small, use the hallway and library with permission.
  • Access: clear paths for wheelchairs and walkers, chairs for anyone who cannot stand, large print on request. Phones away except at the break, with exceptions for medical or family needs.

Music and sound. A short playlist helps: upbeat instrumental for arrival, quieter for stations, one high-energy song for the break. A bell or chime for "time" works better than shouting. A silly "boing" for "Hackles UP!" is worth the thirty seconds it takes to find.

Supplies#

  • Name tents, markers, sticky notes, index cards, painter's tape, a large visible timer, a bell or chime, a small speaker, chart paper.
  • Props: a silly hat and clipboard for the Phisher; a paper "Oops Button" and a "Stop sign" paddle for the micro-teach model (MT2, MT3); an optional stuffed "Hackle."
  • Four bags or bowls for station card sets; a fidget basket and water.
  • Snacks with ingredient labels (check allergies) [VERIFY any school food rules]; a small prize (stickers, a pickle-jar trophy), non-edible if your policy is cautious.

No devices needed. Every active segment runs on paper (slides are optional: see the Workshop Slide Deck Outline). No participant data is collected beyond the anonymous exit ticket and the sign-in sheet.

Segment 2. Why this, why us, and the Hackles routine (0:12-0:22)
Min What happens
0:12-0:16 Why this, why us (script below, about 1.5 minutes spoken) plus the "your role" slide.
0:16-0:19 Group agreements. Read aloud with a "thumbs up" check.
0:19-0:22 Teach the routine with hand signals (slow, fast, whisper). Pair-share (60 seconds).

SAY (why this, why us):

"Here is the heart of it. Children today are online earlier and more often than most of us were. Most are fine. Most use technology safely most of the time. But some will be tricked, some will be treated unkindly, and a few will be targeted by people who are good at it. What protects them is not a fear speech. Research on prevention suggests that skills-based teaching, repeated over time, works better than a single scary assembly (Finkelhor and colleagues, 2021 [VERIFY the citation]). Skills mean practice: rehearsing what to say and who to tell before they need it. And the biggest obstacle is not what they know, it is shame. Kids stay silent because they believe they will be blamed, punished, or have their phone taken away. So the single most powerful sentence in this entire kit is: 'Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault.' Everything today aims at making that sentence automatic."

SAY (agreements):

"Let's agree how we'll work. Thumbs up if you can live with each one. One: fictional cases, not real children. Two: pass is always allowed. Three: step out any time, no explanation; the Reset Corner is right there. Four: no shame, no gotchas. Five: what we share about ourselves stays here, except that if a child may be in danger or being hurt, we get help, and the law and our policy decide how. Six: fun on purpose. Seven: phones in pockets until break."

SAY (routine):

"Here is the one routine in every Hackles lesson. Hackles up? That's noticing. 'Hackles' are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off: too good, pushy, secret, or scary. (Do the signal: raise your hand and brush it up the back of your neck. Everyone copies.) Pause. (Flat palm.) Stop. Breathe. Do not click, reply, pay or send yet. Check. (Circle fingers at your eye.) Who is it, really? What do they want? How fast? Tell. (Hand on chest, then open palm outward.) A trusted person. Every time. No shame. Let's say it together: slow, then fast, then whispered. Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell."

Two promises (say aloud; teachers will say them to students):

"A false alarm is fine and welcome. And if you missed it or got tricked, it is never your fault. Tell someone."

Pair-share (60 seconds): "Turn to a neighbor. When might a student need this? When might you?" Cold-call two pairs.

Pull back the curtain: "Four minutes of talk, one chant, three gestures and a partner chat. Teach it in under five, then get them moving."

If a participant objects ("this is not my job"): "You're right that teachers can't do everything. Your role is recognition and response: notice, listen, reassure, report, hand off. You're not an investigator or a therapist."

0:22-0:24 Rules (script below).

SAY (rules):

"Welcome to the Fake Phish Derby! Each team has a deck of twelve messages. Fictional, silly brand names, no real accounts anywhere. For each card, decide: Hackles Up (something is off) or Hackles Down (looks fine). Then name the clue. We'll use SPOT: Secrets asked, Pressure, Offer too good, Trick to move you. While teams work: circulate and ask "What made your hackles go up?" and "Where would you check?" Do not give answers. Change the card-reader every three cards. If a team is stuck: "Sort fast and argue later."

The Live Phish Drop (script). Plan this with your Phisher in advance, and give a warning beforehand ("there will be a loud guest in a few minutes"). At about 0:30 the Phisher bursts in wearing the silly hat, waving a clipboard.

PHISHER (loud, cheerful, pushy): "CONGRATULATIONS, TABLE FOUR! You have WON a mystery prize! But hurry, hurry, only ten seconds, I need your team's secret password RIGHT NOW or it goes to another team! Shhh, don't tell the facilitator, it's a surprise!"

FACILITATOR (to the room): "Hackles up? Everyone! Show me the signal!" (Everyone brushes up the back of the neck.) "Pause! Palm up!" (Phisher keeps pressing.) "Check! What do we see?" (Participants call out: pressure, prize, secret, wants a password.) "Tell! Who do we tell?" (The room points at the facilitator.) "Thank you, Mr. Phisher, you have been Paused, Checked and Told. Please enjoy your seat."

Applaud the Phisher. This is the whole routine in 45 seconds, and it is funny.

Safe use of the Live Phish Drop:

  • The Phisher is a colleague who knows it is a game. No real passwords, no real money, no real data ever.
  • Do not send fake emails or texts to participants' real accounts or phones. Do not use a real person's name, a real brand or a real district email address. [VERIFY your district's policy on phishing simulations; many districts run these only through the IT department.]
  • Do not shame anyone who is startled by the Phisher.

SAY (reveal):

"On three, hold up your Hackles Up pile. One, two, three! Now let's look at the cards that surprised us."

For each key card, ask a team that got it right to give the clue and the move; ask a team that missed it, "What made it look fine?" Respond warmly: "That is exactly why these work."

SAY (after D12):

"This is the one I love. A text with a login code. If you just asked to log in, it's fine and boring. If you did not ask, someone else is trying to get into your account, so your hackles should go up: don't share the code, change your password, tell someone. Same message, two answers. That's why we check, and why we don't teach kids 'always scary' or 'never scary.' We teach them to notice and check."

SAY (D15, the boring real card): "Hackles Down is a real answer. If every card makes us panic, kids learn to ignore the signal. Calm is allowed."

Pull back the curtain: "Sorting, a deadline, a competition, a surprise, and a debrief where I asked questions instead of lecturing. Also, a pickle trophy."

If someone says "I'd never fall for that": "Many smart people have. That's why we never say 'you should have known better.'" If a participant mentions being scammed recently: see Section 8.

Segment 5. The Say-It-Right Relay (1:27-1:40)#

Purpose: practice the actual words adults use when a child tells them something hard, so they come out automatically.
Method modeled: relay race plus rewrite and presentation (language practice).
Materials: H5 card, H10 relay cards, scorecards, a bell.

Segment 5. The Say-It-Right Relay (1:27-1:40)
Min What happens
1:27-1:28 Rules (script below). Teams get a shuffled deck of 16 phrase cards (R1 to R16).
1:28-1:32 Round 1: Sort. HELPFUL and NOT HELPFUL. Up to 8 points (0.5 per correctly sorted card).
1:32-1:36 Round 2: Rewrite relay. The team pulls four NOT HELPFUL cards. One person at a time, passing the marker, writes a better version of each. Facilitator scores each rewrite 0, 1 or 2 (up to 8 points).
1:36-1:38 Round 3: Thank-You Showdown. One volunteer per team says "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble." in the best possible voice (calm, warm, steady). The room claps. Award 4, 3, 2 and 1 points for loudest clap by audience.
1:38-1:40 Key debrief points (below), about 30 seconds each.

SAY (rules):

"This is a relay race for words. Sixteen cards, each with something an adult might say to a child who tells them something hard. Round One: sort them into HELPFUL and NOT HELPFUL. Round Two: pull four of the not-helpfuls and rewrite each into something a child would want to hear. You pass the marker, one person at a time. Round Three: one volunteer per team delivers the single most important sentence in the whole kit, and the room votes by applause. Ready?"

Key debrief points:

  • "Why did you click?" sounds like blame. "Tell me what happened" does not.
  • "I won't tell anyone" can never be promised. Replace with: "I can't keep this a secret, because I want to get you help. I will tell you who I tell."
  • "Let me see the pictures" is never right if the content may be an intimate image. Do not view, copy, forward or screenshot. Hand the device to the right person per policy. [VERIFY policy]
  • "That's nothing" teaches kids not to tell next time.

Pull back the curtain: "You said the words out loud, in a silly voice, with a score. That's how a phrase gets from your head to your mouth."

Round 3 voices: "Best Wizard Voice," "Best Gentle Grandparent," "Best Calm Pilot." Humor lowers the stakes without lowering the importance.
If a participant defends "I won't tell anyone": "I understand the instinct. A child's trust matters enormously. But promising secrecy is a promise you may be legally and ethically unable to keep, and breaking it later costs trust. The honest version keeps trust." Do not argue; move on.

Purpose: let teachers rehearse the first minutes of a child's disclosure in a safe, structured way, so that their first real response is not their first practice.

This is the one segment with no points, no timers shown and no audience. Do not turn it into a game.

Before you begin: safety checklist

  • Checkbox: The safety partner (counselor) is in the room or just outside, and participants know who and where.
  • Checkbox: You have said the content note and the "step out" promise (script below).
  • Checkbox: Traffic-light cards are out.
  • Checkbox: All cards are fictional and non-graphic (they are).
  • Checkbox: You have a plan for any participant who needs support (a quiet room, a kind word, the EAP number [VERIFY]).
  • Checkbox: You reminded the room that if anyone says something that suggests a real, current child may be in danger, you will talk with them privately and the law and policy decide the next step.

SAY (framing):

"We're about to practice the most important moment in this entire workshop: the first minutes after a child tells you something hard. Let me say three things before we begin. One: content. Everything we use is fictional and non-graphic. No cards describe harm in detail. But this subject can touch real life, for any of us. Two: you are in charge of your own participation. You each have three traffic-light cards. Green means keep going. Yellow means slow down, I need a minute. Red means pause the scene right now, with no explanation. Anyone can show red at any time, and the whole trio will stop. You can also step out for the whole segment and join us when you're ready. [Safety partner's name] is here, and we'll check on you kindly afterward. Three: no real stories. If you find yourself telling a story from your own life or about a real child, show a red card. This is practice, not therapy."

SAY (demonstration, with the co-facilitator or safety partner):

"Let me show you what we are practicing, with a silly low-stakes example. I'm the teacher. [Co-facilitator] is a child who has just lost their game account to a trick. First, the version we're trying not to do."

(Co-facilitator, as child, in a small voice: "I think I messed up. I clicked a link and my account is doing weird stuff. I'm going to be in so much trouble.")

(Facilitator, over-the-top: "WHAT?! Why on EARTH did you click that?! Didn't we TALK about this?! Give me that tablet!")

"Notice how the child went quiet. Now let's try again."

(Co-facilitator repeats the line.)

(Facilitator, calm, kneeling or sitting at an angle: "Thank you for telling me. You're not in trouble. This happens to smart people all the time. Let's figure out what to do. Tell me what happened.")

"Hear the difference? That is the entire skill. I did not ask 'why,' I did not promise a secret, and I thanked the child first. That example was the only funny one. The rest are serious, so we're gentle with each other."

SAY (setting up trios):

"Find two other people. The Teller reads the card and stays in character. The Listener is you, doing your best. The Observer has a checklist and keeps time. Everyone plays each role once. Tellers: if the listener asks for more than your card says, say 'I don't want to say more right now.' Don't invent details. Listeners: open questions only. Observers: share one thing done well and one to try."

During Rounds 1 to 3, walk the room quietly. Do not eavesdrop. Watch body language. If someone shows a yellow or red card, move toward them calmly. If someone leaves, the safety partner follows a few minutes later with a gentle check-in.

SAY (de-role and debrief):

"Okay, everybody stand up. Shake out your hands. Say to your trio: 'I'm [your real name]. That was pretend.' Take one slow breath. In through your nose, out through your mouth. (Pause.) Good. Let's debrief as a whole room, skills only, no stories. First: what is one phrase you plan to keep? (Take three answers.) Second: what was the hardest thing to avoid saying? (Take two.) Third: who in this room needs to look at their Help Map right now and check one number? (Hands.) Please fill that in before you leave today."

Debrief key points (say each in your own words): (1) Thank first, then no blame. (2) Open questions only: "Tell me more." (3) Never promise secrecy: "I can't keep this a secret, because I want to get you help. I will tell you who I tell." (4) Don't investigate: no details, no asking to see messages or images, no confronting anyone. (5) Report personally and promptly as law and policy require; telling the principal may not be enough. [Confirm your state law and district policy with district counsel.] (6) Document the child's own words, the date and time, and who you told. (7) Look after yourself: debrief with the counselor the same day.

Close with a safety partner moment (counselor, 1 minute):

"If anything in this block brought something up for you, today or later, I'm available, and so is [EAP or other resource, VERIFY]. You don't need to say what. Just come find me."

Pull back the curtain: "Notice the safety moves in that block: fictional cards, traffic-light cards, an observer role so no one performs for an audience, a de-role ritual, and a counselor on hand. When you teach a sensitive lesson to children, you use the same moves: the calm opening, the pass, the closing breath."

If it goes sideways: see Section 9 and Section 8.

Post this: "I noticed _ at _. That made me _." (glow) · "What if you tried _?" (grow) · "I stole your ____ and I'm using it Monday." (steal)

While circulating, watch for: Fun (are the "students" moving and laughing? suggest a prop or partner talk); Safe (is anyone asked to share something personal? "Let's use the character, not a real story."); Skill (one skill practiced aloud, ending with Pause-Check-Tell); Time (long teachers can cut the Discuss).

Pull back the curtain: "You just did peer observation in seven minutes per teacher. You don't need an expert, only a colleague and a rubric."

Timing note: for a large room, use groups of four (one observer in each round) and keep 5 minutes of prep. If you are 5 minutes behind, cut to two rounds.
If a participant teaches a sensitive topic anyway: "Let's use one of the cards for now; for the sensitive lessons we'll plan carefully with the counselor." If a participant lectures: "What would the students be doing with their hands in minute two?"

8. When a teacher shares their own incident#

This will happen sometimes. A participant may say, in the middle of the day, "That happened to me" or "That happened to my child" or "I had a student who..." That is not a derailment. It is a sign the training is working, and it needs a calm, kind, structured response. Practice this out loud with your safety partner before the workshop.

The principles#

Thank, slow down and move private; do not promise secrecy (you may have a reporting duty [VERIFY with your administrator and district policy]); do not probe; offer support (the safety partner and the EAP [VERIFY EAP]), not solutions; protect the room, because others may be listening who have their own stories; follow up within 24 hours.

Step-by-step script#

Step 1: In the moment (15 seconds). Calm voice.

"Thank you for trusting us with that. I want to give it the attention it deserves, and not in front of the whole group. Let's take a short pause. Everyone, stand and stretch for one minute. [Name], would you be willing to step over here with [safety partner] and me for a moment?"

Move to a private space that is visible (door open or window). Have the safety partner come with you. Do not be alone behind a closed door with anyone, and keep another adult aware.

Step 2: The honest limit (before they say more).

"Before you tell me more, I want to be honest with you about something. As school staff we may have a legal duty to report certain things, especially if a child may be in danger, and I can't promise to keep everything private. I will always tell you who I tell. If what you want to share is about you personally, or about something long ago, you decide how much to say. Would you like to talk here, or would you rather meet with [safety partner] later today?"

Step 3: Listen and respond. Use the same skills as for a child: "Thank you for telling me." "That sounds really hard." "You are not at fault." Do not ask "why." Do not ask for details.

Step 5: Return to the group (if the teacher wishes). Offer a face-saving path: "Would you like to rejoin the group, step into the Reset Corner, or take the rest of the day off? Whatever you choose is fine." If they rejoin, a simple nod, no spotlight.

Step 6: Close the loop with the room (only if the incident was public).

"Thank you for giving us that moment. I just want to say, to everyone: the moments where somebody trusts us with something are the whole reason we are here. If something in this workshop is bringing anything up for you, [safety partner] and I are available at the break."

Step 7: After the workshop. Within 24 hours, the safety partner or the lead checks in privately. Document only as policy requires, factually, and store it securely. Do not discuss it with other staff beyond need-to-know. Debrief yourself with a trusted colleague, without identifying details.

What not to do#

  • Do not say "me too, let me tell you what happened to me" in front of the group.
  • Do not allow the group to pile on, speculate or ask questions.
  • Do not promise to keep things secret.
  • Do not minimize ("it's okay, that happens to everyone").
  • Do not ask for details to "verify."
  • Do not look at, ask for or accept any images.

Appendix B: Facilitator cheat sheet (one page)#

Before you start: counselor ready. H7 filled. Reset Corner signed. Scoreboard up. Music on. Phisher briefed. Deep breath.

Promises: no scare, no personal stories, pass allowed, step out allowed, fun on purpose. Routine: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. A false alarm is fine; if you got tricked, it is never your fault. The sentence: "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble." Never-dos: never promise secrecy, never ask "why did you," never look at, copy or forward an intimate image.

Lesson shape: Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2. Rubric: fun, safe, skill-based. Checkpoints: 0:47, 1:40, 2:15. After each segment: name the teaching move.

If someone shares an incident: thank, pause, privately, honest limit on secrecy, no probing, safety partner, follow-up in 24 hours. Say "I don't know, let's find out" once, on purpose. After: thank-you in 24 hours, Parking Lot answers in 1 week, first peer observation in 3 weeks.
