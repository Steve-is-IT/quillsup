At a glance. You've learned the content, the craft, the response and the inclusion. Now the question is how you know it works, and how you keep getting better. You'll learn simple ways to measure learning (pre and post, rubrics, exit tickets), how to notice growth in children and in yourself, how to do a teach-back, and how to write a personal action plan. Then you assemble your capstone. This is the last module, so congratulations on getting here.

Learning objectives#

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  1. Choose simple, honest measures of whether your teaching is working, including a help-seeking ("Tell-Readiness") indicator.
  2. Use pre and post surveys, exit tickets and skill rubrics without collecting any more data than you need, which is less than most people assume.
  3. Notice growth in students (and in yourself) and describe it accurately, with humility about what you can claim.
  4. Run a teach-back to check understanding and spread learning among colleagues.
  5. Write an action plan you will actually follow.
  6. Complete the Academy capstone.

Welcome: the fitness tracker for teaching#

You don't need a lab coat to know whether your teaching is working. You need a few simple, honest measures and the willingness to look at them without flinching.

There is not yet strong, rigorous evidence for a modern K-12 curriculum on topics like sextortion and deepfakes. Module 1 said so, and the evidence document behind Hackles says so as well. [VERIFY] That is a reason to measure what you can, to be modest about your claims, and to share what you learn. As in teaching, skills and help-seeking matter more than facts: measure whether children can do the thing and whether they would tell, and don't stop at whether they can repeat a rule.

Part 1: What to measure (and what not to)#

The three questions#

The three questions
Question What it asks Simple tools
1. Did they learn? Knowledge and skills Exit tickets, quick sorts, role-play observation
2. Would they act? Intentions and confidence Pre and post survey items (confidence, willingness to tell)
3. Is it safe to tell? Climate and trust The Tell-Readiness indicator; notice who tells you about small things

Number 3 is the most important and the hardest to measure. You will never know the full answer. But a classroom in which children bring you small "oops" moments is a classroom in which a big one might also be shared.

What not to measure#

  • Do not measure by asking children to disclose personal experiences. Surveys never ask whether a child has been harmed.
  • Do not collect names unless you need matched pairs, and then follow your district's data rules. Hackles collects no student accounts and no student data, ever. Anything you keep is paper, local, short-lived and destroyed according to your district's policy. [VERIFY retention rules]
  • Do not use results to rank children, teachers or classes.
  • Do not report tiny groups (for example, fewer than ten students in a subgroup) because individuals might be identified. [VERIFY your district's suppression rule]

Part 2: The Hackles measurement toolkit#

The Hackles Assessment Pack (kit folder 07_Assessment) has full pre/post surveys by band, scoring keys, a skill rubric, a role-play checklist and a class tally sheet. Here is the short tour.

Part 2: The Hackles measurement toolkit
Tool When Time What you get
Exit ticket End of each lesson 1 minute Did the big idea land?
Pre/post survey (by band) Before Lesson 1, after Lesson 10 5 to 10 minutes each Change in knowledge, confidence, and willingness to tell
Skill rubric (Hackles up, Pause, Check, Tell, Help Ladder, Response Drill, No-Blame Mindset) During role-plays While you circulate Skill levels from Beginning to Extending
Role-play observation checklist During practice While you observe Which steps students do
Class tally sheet After the post survey 20 minutes Class-level changes in one place
Teacher feedback form End of unit 10 minutes Your own reflection on how it went

The Tell-Readiness indicator#

Because help-seeking is the central outcome, the Assessment Pack tracks one simple indicator per band: the percentage of students who give the best answer to two or three core items (for example, "would you tell a trusted adult?" and "would you be in trouble for telling?"). It is, in the Assessment Pack's words, the most meaningful number you will have.

How to read it:

  • If the percentage goes up from pre to post, good. Say "went up," not "my lessons caused it."
  • If it stays flat, ask why. Did students already feel safe? Did the lessons rush the Tell?
  • If it goes down, do not panic. Sometimes children become more honest after learning about the topic, and sometimes the post survey is harder. Look at the answers, then ask students (anonymously, safely) what would make telling easier.

Honest claims#

Honest claims
Say this Not this
"More of my students said they would tell a trusted adult after the lessons." "My lessons prevented abuse."
"Students can name three signs of a phish." "My students are now safe from phishing."
"Scores rose from 52 to 74 percent on the core items." "It was a huge success!" (without numbers)
"We do not know yet whether this changes behavior over the long term." "Research proves this works."

Try This Now

Try This Now (3 minutes). Write one honest claim about your own teaching so far, using real or invented numbers. Read it to a partner, who answers "What is your evidence?" Rewrite the claim until it says only what the evidence supports.

Part 3: A simple pre/post analysis (in five steps)#

You do not need statistics software. A pencil and a class list (with no names) will do.

  1. Gather all pre and post surveys. Count how many of each.
  2. Tally for each core item the number of students who chose the best answer, pre and post.
  3. Convert to percentages (number with best answer divided by number of surveys, times 100).
  4. Compare pre and post. Subtract.
  5. Look at the answers, not just the scores. Which wrong answer was most popular? What does it suggest? ("Many chose 'Ignore it.' Maybe we need more practice on what to do next.")

Worked example (invented data). A fourth-grade class of 24 completes the pre-survey and 22 complete the post-survey. On the item "If something online felt wrong, who would you tell?":

Part 3: A simple pre/post analysis (in five steps)
Best answer Total surveys Percentage
Pre 11 24 46%
Post 17 22 77%

The change is +31 percentage points. An honest summary: "The share of students choosing a trusted adult rose from about 46% to about 77%. Class sizes differed slightly, and we do not know why individual children changed, but it is encouraging."

A reasonableness check. Did the post survey happen soon after an exciting lesson? (Students may give the "right" answer immediately after instruction more often than weeks later.) Plan a short "booster" re-check a month or two later if you can. Also remember size: with 24 students, one child is about 4 percentage points, so small changes may be noise. Treat results as signals, not proof.

Part 4: Noticing growth#

Numbers are not the only evidence. Much of teaching is noticing. Here is a list of growth signals to watch for, in your students and in you.

In students#

Watch for: language shifts (students say "hackles up," "pause," "check" and "tell" unprompted); small tellings (a child brings you a mean message or a weird pop-up, a sign of trust); helping peers ("Pause first, then ask"); less blame ("it is not their fault"); deeper questions ("What if the grown-up does not believe me?"); families reporting it ("My daughter told me about a code word"); and calmer false alarms ("Probably a false alarm, but I wanted to check").

In you#

Watch for: you catch yourself before a shaming phrase; a surprising question no longer rattles you; your Contact Card is on your wall; you ask about a new game and like the answer; you teach the 15 minutes and go home lighter than before.

The "glow and grow" habit#

After every lesson, write two lines:

  • Glow: one thing that worked.
  • Grow: one thing to try next time.

Thirty seconds. Over ten weeks you will have your own personal research report.

Part 5: The teach-back#

A teach-back is one of the most powerful ways to check understanding and to spread learning. You explain what you learned to someone else, in your own words, and they ask questions. It works for kids and adults.

Teach-back with students#

At the end of a lesson, ask a student (or a pair) to teach the skill to a partner or to Hackle the puppet. "Teach Hackle what you do when your hackles go up." Listen for: the skill, the phrase, the "tell," the "not your fault." Misunderstandings are easy to spot. (If a child tells Hackle, "Click it and then tell," you have your next mini-lesson.)

Teach-back with colleagues (the Mentor move)#

The Academy spreads best from teacher to teacher. A 20-minute colleague teach-back works like this:

Teach-back with colleagues (the Mentor move)
Minutes Step
3 You explain the Hackles routine (Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell.) and the three promises.
5 You teach a 3-minute micro-lesson and your colleague plays a student.
5 You walk through the First 10 Minutes card together.
5 The colleague teaches back what they heard, and you give two glows and a grow.
2 Agree on one action each.

If you complete two colleague teach-backs, you are on the way to the Hackles Mentor badge (see the Certificate and Badges document).

Teach-back with families#

At a family night or in a newsletter, invite a parent to teach back what they will say at home. "If your child tells you something scary, what is the first thing you will say?" (Answer: "Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. You are not in trouble.")

Part 6: Your personal action plan#

The best plans are small. A plan with twenty items will live in a drawer. A plan with three items might change your year.

The 30-60-90 Action Plan#

The 30-60-90 Action Plan
Time Focus Example action Your action
First 30 days Get ready Fill in the Contact Card. Tell the counselor your lesson dates. Teach Lesson 1. Do 2-step verification on email.
Next 60 days Teach and notice Teach lessons 2 to 5. Run the glow-and-grow notes. Send Family Letter A. Do one Hackles check-in.
By 90 days Measure and share Teach the remaining lessons. Do the post survey and class tally. Do one colleague teach-back.

Plan for your own growth areas#

Look back at your Honesty Map from Module 1. Which items did you circle? Which have moved to the left? Which still need work? Choose one for each time block.

Plan for obstacles (an "if... then..." list)#

People who plan for obstacles are more likely to carry out their plans. Write three.

Plan for obstacles (an "if... then..." list)
If this happens... Then I will...
I run out of time for the lesson Cut a round of practice; keep the takeaway and the open door.
A parent is upset Use L.E.A.R.N. and involve the counselor.
A child discloses Use the First 10 Minutes card and my Contact Card.
I feel overwhelmed Debrief with my buddy; take a break; use my care plan.
I forget to measure Put "tally" on the calendar after Lesson 10.

Part 7: The Capstone#

The capstone is not a test. It is a small portfolio that proves, to you and to a reviewer, that you can do the work. It takes about 60 to 90 minutes if you have done the Academy activities, because you will mostly be gathering what you have already made.

Four pieces#

Four pieces
Piece What to submit Competencies shown
1. Lesson Plan and Micro-Teach Evidence Your one-page lesson plan from Activity 5.3, the completed rubric from your micro-teach (peer-scored or self-scored), and two glows and one grow. D (Teach)
2. Response Readiness Pack Your completed Contact Card, your one-sentence reporting plan, and a short reflection (about 150 words) on your First 10 Minutes role-play (Activity 6.3). E (Respond)
3. Inclusion and Family Pack Your Inclusion Audit (Activity 7.1) with three adaptations, and one short, warm family message in your own words (for example, an introduction to the unit or a reply to a concern). F (Include)
4. Action Plan and Reflection Your 30-60-90 Action Plan, a one-line summary of how your Honesty Map from Module 1 changed (you never have to show the map itself), and a reflection (about 200 words) on your own growth. Include one thing you still want to learn. A, B, C, F

How competencies map to the rubric#

The rubric (in Academy_Assessments) has six rows, one per competency area: A Know, B Protect, C Connect, D Teach, E Respond, F Include and grow. Pieces 1 to 4 give evidence for each. For rows A, B and C, your module activities (such as Activity 2.3's settings stroll and Activity 3.1's curiosity interview) count too; add a brief note such as "Evidence for row B: I turned on 2-step verification and updated my devices."

Who reviews it#

  • Self-review (required): score yourself on the rubric first.
  • Reviewer (required for the certificate): a principal, coach, counselor, learning buddy, or mentor who reads your four pieces (about 20 minutes), scores them, and signs the certificate. The reviewer does not need to be a tech expert.
  • Hackles Mentor candidates: include the notes from your two colleague teach-backs.

Privacy in the capstone#

  • Do not include real student names, work, photos or any information that could identify a child.
  • Do not include any real password, account name or personal security detail.
  • Do not include any description of a real disclosure. If you are including a reflection on a real event, describe it only in general terms. Reflect on your practice, not on a child's story.

The final step: the post-course survey#

Take the Post-Course Confidence Survey (Academy_Assessments) and compare with your pre-course answers. Look at the difference. Then do something nice for yourself.

Do it yourself#

Activity 8.1: Build Your Mini-Tally (15 minutes)#

You will practice the pre/post analysis using invented data.

A seventh-grade class took the pre- and post-survey. Use the numbers below to complete the table.

Activity 8.2: The Teach-Back (20 minutes, with a colleague)#

  1. Choose one core idea from the Academy: the Hackles routine, the First 10 Minutes, or the three promises.
  2. Teach it to your partner in 5 minutes without notes.
  3. Ask your partner to teach it back to you in their own words.
  4. Notice: what did they get? What got lost? What does that tell you about how you explained it?
  5. Swap.

Write down one change you will make to how you explain it.

Activity 8.3: Write Your 30-60-90 Plan (15 minutes)#

Use the template in Part 6. Fill in all three time blocks, write three if-then obstacle plans, and set two dates on a calendar: the date of your first lesson, and the date you will review your plan.

Then share it with your learning buddy or a colleague. Ask them to check in with you on that review date. (Accountability is a kindness.)

Scenario Studio#

Scenario 8.A: The scores went down#

Your fifth graders took the pre-survey in September and the post-survey in December. On the key item "Would you be in trouble for telling a grown-up?", fewer students chose the best answer ("No") in December than in September. Your principal asks for the results.

Discuss: What might explain a drop? What do you do with the numbers, and how do you talk honestly with your principal?

Expert-response guidance. A drop does not mean the lessons failed. Possible explanations: the pre-survey was taken casually and the post more thoughtfully; students became more honest as they thought about the topic; a different group completed the surveys; an event happened in the class or school; or the lessons really missed something. Check individual answers and ask whether the lessons clearly said "you will never be in trouble for telling." Revisit the Tell lessons. Tell your principal plainly: "The numbers went down on this item. Here is what I think it means and what I plan to do." Consider asking students anonymously what would make telling easier. Do not hide the number.

Scenario 8.B: "Our measures say we are great, so we are done"#

After one successful year, your school's leadership says, "The results are good. We can retire the lessons and move on to something else."

Discuss: What is the risk in stopping? What evidence would you share, and how do you make the case for continuing?

Expert-response guidance. Remind them of the spiral: a skill taught once, in one grade, tends to fade, and every year brings new students, new apps and new threats. Share your own data and growth signals, and the research on dosage and repetition (Module 1, Module 4). [VERIFY] Propose a light-touch plan: a short "Hackles check-in" each month, a refresher at the start of each year, new teachers onboarded through the Academy, and the annual post survey. Present it as maintenance, not expansion: "The goal is not more time. It is steady time."

Quick Card: Module 8, Measure, Reflect, Grow#

Three questions: Did they learn? Would they act? Is it safe to tell? The Tell-Readiness indicator is the share of students giving the best answers on "would you tell?" items.

What not to measure: never ask children to disclose experiences; no names unless you need matched pairs (then follow district rules); no ranking of children, teachers or classes; do not report groups under about 10. [VERIFY your district rule]

Five-step pre/post: gather; tally best answers; convert to percentages (best answers divided by surveys, times 100); subtract; read the wrong answers.

Example: pre 11 of 24 = 46 percent; post 17 of 22 = 77 percent; change +31 percentage points. One child in 24 is about 4 points, so treat small changes as signals, not proof.

Honest claims: "More of my students said they would tell a trusted adult." Not "My lessons prevented abuse." If a score drops, check the answers, the context and the timing, and say so plainly.

Growth signals: students say "hackles up" unprompted; small tellings; peers helping peers; less blame; calmer false alarms. In you: you catch a shaming phrase; your Contact Card is on the wall.

Glow and grow: after every lesson, one thing that worked and one to try (30 seconds).

Colleague teach-back (20 minutes): 3 explain the routine; 5 teach a micro-lesson; 5 walk through the First 10 Minutes; 5 they teach back and you give feedback; 2 agree an action each.

30-60-90 plan

  • 30 days: Contact Card, tell your counselor your dates, teach Lesson 1, 2-step on email.
  • 60 days: lessons 2 to 5, glow-and-grow notes, Family Letter A.
  • 90 days: remaining lessons, post survey and tally, one teach-back.

Capstone (four pieces): (1) lesson plan and micro-teach rubric; (2) Response Readiness Pack; (3) Inclusion and Family Pack; (4) Action Plan and reflection. Privacy: no student names, work or photos; no real passwords; no real disclosures.

Review: self-score first; the reviewer scores and signs; Row E (Respond) must be Hackles-Ready or above.
