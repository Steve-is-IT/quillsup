At a glance. This is the "do the thing" module. You'll learn the anatomy of a 15-minute Hackles lesson (Hook, Activity, Discuss, Takeaway plus exit ticket), the facilitation, questioning and management moves that make it run smoothly, and the common mistakes. Then you'll teach a micro-lesson to a partner and be scored on a rubric. Quick Start learners: read "Anatomy of a 15-minute lesson" and do Activity 5.3 in its "Mini" version (7 minutes).

Learning objectives#

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  1. Lay out the four-part, 15-minute Hackles lesson structure and time it accurately.
  2. Use facilitation moves (modeling, think-pair-share, wait time, choral response, hand signals) to keep every child active, including the one in the back row who is working very hard at being invisible.
  3. Ask open, layered questions and respond to wrong or surprising answers without shaming.
  4. Manage a lively class and a sensitive moment at the same time.
  5. Teach a micro-lesson to a peer and score it with the rubric.
  6. Recognize and avoid the mistakes that matter most.

Welcome: the 15-minute promise#

Many online safety programs ask for a full period, a special assembly, or a half day. Hackles asks for fifteen minutes, once a week, for ten weeks. The reasons are practical: teachers are busy and a lesson that can't fit between math and lunch won't happen, attention is finite, repetition beats length (the spiral from Module 4), and a short lesson leaves room to react if a child discloses or the lesson goes sideways. Fifteen minutes is tight. No rambling. This module shows you how to use every minute.

Part 1: Anatomy of a 15-minute lesson#

Every lesson in the Hackles Teacher Guides (K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12) uses the same skeleton. Once you learn it, you can teach any of them, and you can build your own.

Part 1: Anatomy of a 15-minute lesson
Part Time Purpose You do Students do
Hook 2 min Wake curiosity, set a calm tone, name the skill Story, riddle, question, or character Listen, guess, react
Activity 8 min Practice the skill Set up, explain, circulate, coach Do, say, move, sort, role-play
Discuss 3 min Make sense of it Ask 3 to 5 questions; listen Share, compare, connect
Takeaway 2 min Lock in the one big idea; include "never your fault" and "tell" Say it, gesture it, repeat it Repeat, gesture, exit ticket

Total: 15 minutes. (Band guides sometimes call the last block "Takeaway plus exit ticket.")

The skeleton's secret: one skill, one phrase, one gesture#

  • One skill: "Say I need help." "Pause before you click." "Check another way."
  • One phrase: a sentence kids can repeat. ("Hackles up? Pause, Check, Tell.")
  • One gesture: Hackles Up, Pause Palm, Check Eyes, Tell Wave.

If your lesson cannot be summarized by one skill, one phrase and one gesture, it is probably two lessons.

The Hook (2 minutes)#

Goal: make children want to be here, and make the skill feel doable and a little fun.

Good hooks:

  • A character (Hackle puppet for K-5; a case-file card for 6-8; a short scenario for 9-12).
  • A riddle ("It is something you own that you cannot see. Thieves want it. You have more than ten. What is it?" Answer: accounts.)
  • A quick poll ("Thumbs up if you have ever gotten a message you were not sure about.")
  • A surprising fact (calm, from a [VERIFY]-checked source).

Hook rules:

  • Do not scare. No "imagine something terrible happens."
  • Do not exceed two minutes. If you are still talking at minute three, you have lost them.
  • Say out loud: what you will practice today.

Sample hook (grades 3-5, "Scam Spotters"): Hold up a phone-sized card showing a fake message. "Detectives, this just arrived. It says: 'You won 10,000 coins! Click now! Do not tell anyone!' Thumbs up if you think it is real. (Pause.) Thumbs down if you think it is a trick. (Pause.) I see some of you in the middle. Great detective instinct. Today we will learn a quick checklist that tells us for sure. And here is a secret: even grown-up detectives get fooled, and it is never their fault."

The Activity (8 minutes)#

Goal: every student does the skill at least twice.

Activity design rules: everyone does something (if only five students are active, redesign); say the instructions in 20 seconds and show an example, then go; use pairs and small groups (think-pair-share, partner scripts, tables of 4 to 6); give roles other than victim (helper, detective, analyst, upstander); circulate, coach and note moments for the Discuss phase; keep materials simple (cards, paper, chart paper, no tech).

The "I do, we do, you do" pattern. I do (1 min): you model the skill. We do (2 min): the class tries it together. You do (5 min): pairs or groups practice, twice.

The Discuss (3 minutes)#

Goal: turn an activity into learning. Without discussion, an activity is just a game.

Use a question ladder (more in Part 3):

  1. What happened? ("What did you notice?")
  2. So what? ("Why did that matter? How did it feel?")
  3. Now what? ("What will you do if it happens to you or a friend?")

Ask 3 to 5 questions. Accept short answers. If a discussion is hot, pause it and say, "Let's keep going; I will check in later."

The Takeaway (2 minutes)#

Goal: one big idea, in kid-words, with the Hackles promise.

Formula: Skill + Gesture + Promise.

"Today's big idea: Pause before you click. (Pause Palm.) If your hackles go up, you do not have to figure it out alone: you can tell a grown-up you trust. And remember: a false alarm is fine, and it is never your fault."

Then the exit ticket (about a minute, can overlap): a quick check. Examples: a thumbs-up/middle/down on "I could do this"; a one-sentence note; a drawing; a quick sort.

Then the open door: "If anything today made you think of something you want to talk about, tell me, the counselor, or write a note. You are never in trouble for telling."

Part 2: Facilitation moves (your toolkit)#

These are small moves that make a big difference. Learn two this week and add one a week.

Part 2: Facilitation moves (your toolkit)
Move What it is Why it helps Example
Model it Do the skill first, thinking aloud Shows what "good" looks like "Watch: I see a free-coin message, my hackles go up, I pause."
Think-pair-share Think alone, tell a partner, then share Every voice is used; shy students warm up "Take 20 seconds. Tell your partner."
Wait time Silent 3 to 5 seconds after a question Raises quality and participation Count to five in your head.
Choral response Whole class answers together Safe participation; good for phrases "What do we say? ... I need help!"
Hand signals Hackles Up, Pause Palm, Check Eyes, Tell Wave Quick, silent, inclusive "Show me what you do when hackles go up."
Thumbs check Up, sideways, down Instant feel for understanding "Thumbs: how sure are you?"
Anonymous tools Sticky notes, a box, a show-of-hands with eyes closed Safe for sensitive items "Write one question. No names."
The pass card Any student may say "pass" Preserves safety and dignity Always available.

Keeping the energy right. Younger children: short bursts, movement and a calm routine at the end. Older children: real cases, choices and no cartoonish tone. Teens: be brief, direct and credible, and invite their expertise.

Part 3: Questioning that works#

Open, not closed#

Open questions invite explanation. Ask "What made you think it was a scam?" rather than "Was that a scam?" and "Who would you tell, and what would you say?" rather than "Did you tell an adult?"

The question ladder (lower to higher)#

The question ladder (lower to higher)
Level Question stem Example
Recall What did we notice? "What were the hackles-raisers?"
Explain Why does it matter? "Why do scammers use urgency?"
Apply What would you do? "If you got this tomorrow, what would you do?"
Evaluate Which is better, and why? "Which reply is safer, A or B?"
Create How could we improve it? "How could we help a friend who got this?"

Responding to answers#

Responding to answers
Student says... You say...
A correct answer "Yes. How did you figure that out?" (Ask for reasoning.)
A partly right answer "You are on to something. Can someone add to that?"
A wrong answer "Interesting. Let's test it. What would happen if...?" (No shaming; use curiosity.)
A silly answer Smile, then redirect: "Ha! Okay, and what would be a real way?"
A worrying answer ("I would just send it") Calm: "Thanks for being honest. Lots of people feel that pressure. Let's practice a different response." Then follow up privately.
A disclosure "Thank you. I want to talk with you more privately. Let's do that in a moment." Then follow Module 6.
Silence Wait. Offer think-pair-share.

The golden question for any misstep: "What would help you do something different next time?" It treats the error as a design problem, not a character flaw.

Part 4: Classroom management during safety lessons#

Sensitive topics can raise energy, giggles and anxiety. Here is a short plan.

Before the lesson#

  • Set group agreements (three is enough): Respect each other. Share only what you choose. What happens in role-plays stays in role-plays. (But you can always tell a trusted adult.)
  • Explain the pass card and the open door.
  • Decide your signal for attention (a chime, a raised hand, a clap pattern).
  • Plan your seating to allow pairs and circulation.

During the lesson#

  • Giggling is often nervous energy, not disrespect. Name it: "A lot of us laugh when things feel awkward. That is okay. Let's keep going."
  • Over-sharing: If a child begins to share a personal experience in front of the class, gently redirect: "Thank you for trusting us. Let's keep the details between us and talk privately after." (Then do follow up.)
  • Naming and blaming: Do not allow students to name names in role-plays or scenarios. "We talk about situations, not people."
  • Know-it-alls and jokers: Give them a role (see Module 4).
  • A child becomes upset: Offer a pause or a quiet corner. Check in privately at the first chance. Alert the counselor if needed.
  • Tech-free default: Hackles lessons are unplugged by default. No student should need to log in.

After the lesson#

  • Close with a calm routine (breath, stretch, a sentence of gratitude).
  • Be visible and available for a few minutes.
  • Note anything that needs follow-up and act on it the same day.
  • Check your exit tickets for any worrying content the same day. [VERIFY your school's policy about handling student notes that might contain disclosures]

Time rescue: if you are running over#

Time rescue: if you are running over
If you are at Do this
5 minutes in, still on the hook Skip to the activity: "Let me show you instead."
12 minutes, activity not done Cut one round; keep the takeaway.
14 minutes, discussion is rich Say, "Let's pick this up tomorrow." Never cut the takeaway and the open door.

Part 5: A full worked example#

Here is a complete, original sample lesson in the Hackles style, so you can see all four parts together. It is not a replacement for the official lessons. It is a model for your micro-teach.

Lesson: "Pause Palm" (Grades 2 to 4, 15 minutes)
Skill: Pause before clicking or answering when something online feels off.
Phrase: "Hackles up? Pause."
Gesture: Pause Palm (hand out, like "stop").
Materials: Four pop-up cards (hand-drawn), Hackle puppet or picture.

Hook (2 min). Hold up the puppet. "This is Hackle. Hackle has spikes that pop up when something feels funny. Today, a pop-up appeared on Hackle's screen: 'Free coins! Click NOW!' Hackle's spikes popped up. What do you think Hackle should do first? (Take two guesses.) Hackle says: 'First, I do nothing. I pause.' Everybody try it with me: Pause Palm."

Activity (8 min): "Pop-Up Parade."

  1. (1 min) I do. Show Card 1. Say: "Free coins! Click NOW! My spikes went up. Pause Palm. I do not click. I look: is there a rush? Is there a secret? Does it ask for something? Yes, rush. I will tell a grown-up."
  2. (2 min) We do. Show Card 2 ("Your computer is sick! Call this number!"). Class shows Pause Palm together and says the checks aloud.
  3. (4 min) You do. Partners get Cards 3 and 4 (one is harmless: "Your teacher shared a worksheet"). They decide: hackles up or not? Show Pause Palm if up. Each pair explains one card to a neighbor pair.
  4. (1 min) Circulate and note. Note one pair that makes a great point.

Discuss (3 min).

  1. "What made your hackles go up?" (rush, secret, ask)
  2. "What if the message was harmless? Is a false alarm okay?" (Yes!)
  3. "Who could you tell?"
  4. "What if you clicked and then felt worried?" (It is not your fault. Tell someone.)

Takeaway (2 min). "Today's big idea: When hackles go up, pause. And if you need help, you tell a grown-up. A false alarm is fine, and it is never your fault." Everyone does Pause Palm and Tell Wave. Exit ticket: draw one grown-up you would tell. Open door: "If anything made you think of something, tell me or the counselor."

Notice what is not in the lesson: no scary story, no jargon, no shaming, no tech.

Part 6: Four mistakes to avoid above all#

Lecturing, skipping the rehearsal and rushing the takeaway are covered above. These four matter most:

Part 6: Four mistakes to avoid above all
Mistake Quick fix
Making a child the "example" Use invented characters.
Promising secrecy ("I won't tell anyone") Say, "I may need to tell people whose job is to help."
Ignoring the open door Always close with it.
Teaching without prep for disclosures Know your counselor and policy first.

Part 7: The Micro-Teach Rubric#

This rubric is used in Activity 5.3 and in the capstone (Module 8). It is for growth, not for catching you out. Score each row 1 to 4.

Part 7: The Micro-Teach Rubric
Row 1: Beginning 2: Developing 3: Hackles-Ready 4: Mentor-level
1. Skill focus The skill is unclear or there are several. A skill exists but is not stated. One clear skill, stated to the class. One clear skill; students can restate it in their own words.
2. Structure and timing No clear structure; runs far over or under. Some structure; timing uneven. Hook, Activity, Discuss, Takeaway; finishes within about a minute of 15. Smooth transitions; adjusts timing on the fly without losing the takeaway.
3. Active practice Mostly teacher talk. Some student activity, not everyone. Every student does the skill at least twice. Practice is varied, engaging and tailored; students coach each other.
4. Tone and language Includes fear or shaming language. Mostly calm; occasional slip. Calm, warm, shame-free; the three promises are said. Tone is consistent and models curiosity; the promises feel natural, not scripted.
5. Questioning and facilitation Mostly closed questions; little wait time. Mix of questions; some wait time. Open questions; wait time; responds to answers without shaming. Layered questions; uses student answers to deepen; handles surprises gracefully.
6. Safety and closure No open door, no help-seeking message. Mentions telling, but briefly. Clear "who to tell," the open door, and a calm close. Anticipates sensitive moments; offers a private way to disclose; closes regulated and calm.

Scoring guide. 6 to 11 total: keep practicing with a partner. 12 to 17: solid; refine two rows. 18 to 24: Hackles-Ready (see the Certificate document for the exact capstone requirement).

Do it yourself#

Activity 5.1: The Timing Card Sort (10 minutes)#

You need: scissors (or just a pencil), and the cards below.

Number these actions in the right order and assign a time to each (the four parts add to 15).

Card Action Part Minutes

Now add one time-rescue plan: if your activity is running 3 minutes over, what exactly will you cut? Write it in one sentence.

Activity 5.2: The Question Ladder Workout (10 minutes)#

You need: a partner (or write alone).

Choose a topic from the Hackles kit (for example, "Group Chat Dynamics," or "Passphrase Lab"). Write one question at each level of the ladder (Recall, Explain, Apply, Evaluate, Create). Then:

  1. Ask your partner each question and count the seconds of wait time you give.
  2. Practice one response move from the table: revoice, ask for reasoning, or "tell me more."
  3. Practice responding to a wrong answer you invent ("I would just click it to see") using curiosity rather than correction.

Reflect: Which moves felt natural? Which felt awkward? (Awkward is a sign you are learning.)

Activity 5.3: The Micro-Teach (25 minutes per teacher, with a partner or small group)#

This is the Academy's centerpiece. It is practice, so nobody gets grades from the world: just from a rubric you use together.

Choose your version.

  • Full: 15 minutes (use if you have a full practice slot).
  • Mini: 7 minutes (Quick Start). Hook 1, Activity 4, Discuss 1, Takeaway 1.

Roles (rotate if you have three or four people).

Activity 5.3: The Micro-Teach (25 minutes per teacher, with a partner or small group)
Role Job
Teacher Teaches the lesson.
Students Act as students of the chosen age. Be cooperative, with a few gentle "typical" moments: a giggle, a worrying answer, a silent kid.
Observer Scores with the rubric (Part 7) and writes two glows (what worked) and one grow (what to try next).

Only invented moments are used. Nobody draws on a real child's experience or their own, and anyone may say "pause" to stop.

Step 1: Choose. Pick a real Hackles lesson from your grade band, or use the "Pause Palm" model above. Read the lesson once.

Step 2: Plan (5 minutes). Fill in your one-page plan:

Element Your plan
Skill (verb)
Phrase
Gesture

Step 3: Teach. Use a timer. Stay within the structure. If something goes wrong, keep going. You are allowed to make mistakes. The learning is in noticing them.

Step 4: The "typical moment" cards. The students draw one of these and use it once during the lesson. Teachers respond with the Module 5 moves:

  • "I would just click it to see." (Practice curiosity.)
  • A student giggles uncontrollably. (Practice naming the nervousness.)
  • A student gives a long personal story. (Practice a gentle redirect.)
  • A student says "this is boring." (Practice invitation and a role.)
  • A student says "my friend had this happen." (Practice open-door.)
  • A student is silent and looks down. (Practice a quiet check-in after.)

Step 6: Record. Keep your rubric sheet for the capstone.

Specialist Challenge: Co-teach the lesson with a classroom teacher: you do the technical demonstration, they lead the discussion. Debrief how your styles complement.

Scenario Studio#

Scenario 5.A: The activity that flopped#

You run a card-sort activity with a class of fourth-graders. After two minutes the room is chaos. Two groups are arguing, one is off task, and you are at minute 7 with no discussion yet.

Discuss: What might have gone wrong in the set-up? What can you do right now to rescue the lesson, and what will you change next time?

Expert-response guidance. The most common cause is unclear instructions and too much freedom. Right now: use your attention signal, reset with a 20-second restatement and a model ("I do"), then shorten the task to one round. Salvage by moving to Discuss: ask each group to share one thing. Next time: model first, give written or pictured steps, assign roles, and shorten. Do not blame the students. Do blame the plan, kindly.

Scenario 5.B: The note on the exit ticket#

You collect exit tickets from a fifth-grade class. One says: "I do not have a grown up to tell. Nobody believes me when I tell them stuff." There is no name. The handwriting is distinctive.

Discuss: What might the note mean? What do you do today, and how do you balance privacy and care?

Expert-response guidance. Take it seriously, same day. You do not need to decide what it means. Tell your counselor or designated contact today, and follow your school's process for notes that may contain a disclosure. [VERIFY your policy] Approach the child privately and gently, without exposing them: "I read your note. Thank you for writing. You are never in trouble for telling." Do not interrogate. Do not promise secrecy. The child has told you something important: a possible lack of safe adults, and a pattern of not being believed. Make sure the child knows several school helpers, and keep the door open. If anything in the conversation suggests abuse, neglect or danger, follow Module 6 and report as required.

Co-teaching works well here. Quick Card: Module 5, Running a 15-Minute Lesson#

Four parts

Quick Card: Module 5, Running a 15-Minute Lesson
Part Minutes Notes
Hook 2 Character, riddle, poll or calm fact. Never scary. Name the skill.
Activity 8 I do (1), we do (2), you do (5, twice). Everyone active.
Discuss 3 What happened? So what? Now what?
Takeaway 2 Skill + gesture + promise, then exit ticket and open door.

One skill, one phrase, one gesture. If you cannot say it in one each, it is two lessons.

Takeaway formula: "Today's big idea: Pause before you click. If your hackles go up, you can tell a grown-up you trust. A false alarm is fine, and it is never your fault."

Open door (never cut): "If anything today made you think of something, tell me, the counselor, or write a note. You are never in trouble for telling."

Facilitation moves: model it; think-pair-share; wait 3 to 5 seconds; choral response; gentle cold call with a pass; hand signals; anonymous notes; revoice.

Responding

  • Wrong answer: "Let's test it. What would happen if...?"
  • Worrying answer ("I would just send it"): "Thanks for being honest. Lots of people feel that pressure." Follow up privately.
  • Disclosure: "Thank you. Let's talk more privately in a moment." Then Module 6.

Management: three group agreements (respect; share only what you choose; role-plays stay in role-plays, but you can always tell a trusted adult). Name giggles as nerves. Talk about situations, not people. Offer a quiet corner and a pass card. Read exit tickets for worrying notes the same day. [VERIFY your school policy on student notes]

Running over: minute 5 and still hooking: go to the activity. Minute 12: cut one round. Minute 14: continue tomorrow. Never cut the takeaway or the open door.

Rubric rows (1 to 4 each; 18 or more is Hackles-Ready): skill focus; structure and timing; active practice; tone and language; questioning; safety and closure.

Four mistakes to avoid above all: making a child the example; promising secrecy; skipping the open door; teaching a sensitive lesson without knowing your counselor and policy.

Before you teach: tell your counselor the topic and date.
