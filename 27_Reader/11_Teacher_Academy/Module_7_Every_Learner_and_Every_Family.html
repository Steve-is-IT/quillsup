At a glance. A lesson that works for some children and leaves others out isn't finished. This module covers inclusion across language, disability, culture, family structure, home internet access and trauma history, and it shows you how to partner with families, including the ones who push back. It follows the Hackles Inclusion and Equity Guide and Family Engagement Pack. Quick Start learners may skip this module for now, but come back before your first sensitive lesson.

Learning objectives#

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  1. Apply five inclusion principles to any Hackles lesson.
  2. Adapt a lesson for English learners, students with disabilities, and students in low-internet or no-device homes.
  3. Teach with cultural humility and avoid assuming what a "normal" family looks like.
  4. Use trauma-informed language and practices.
  5. Partner with families through clear, warm, two-way communication.
  6. Respond to parent concerns and pushback without defensiveness.

Welcome: the empty chair#

Picture a great lesson. The Hook is charming, the Activity is active, the Discuss is rich. Then you notice an empty chair. It isn't a missing child. It's the child who is in the room and not included: the newcomer who can't follow the English, the student whose hands can't do the card sort, the child whose family has no internet or no adult home in the evenings, the child with no grown-up they trust who is being asked to name three.

Online safety education has a special risk here: the children most at risk are often the ones a standard lesson serves least. Students who are isolated, in unstable housing, in foster care, learning English, LGBTQ+ or living with disabilities may be targeted more often and believed less. [VERIFY statistics before quoting; see the Inclusion and Equity Guide.] The good news is that inclusive design comes down to a handful of habits you build into every lesson, and they make the lesson better for everyone.

Part 1: Five principles for every lesson#

These principles reflect the Hackles Inclusion and Equity Guide. Print them and put them where you plan.

Part 1: Five principles for every lesson
# Principle What it means in practice
1 Many ways in Offer more than one way to take part: speak, point, draw, act, sort, write.
2 No one is singled out Never ask a child to reveal their home, family, language or experiences in front of peers.
3 Everyone has someone Make sure every child can name a safe adult, and that school helpers count.
4 Assume good intentions in families Families vary in values, languages and routines. Start with respect.
5 Safety first, always Inclusion never means lowering safeguards or skipping reporting.

Try This Now

Try This Now (3 minutes). Open your next lesson plan. Find one step that assumes a device, a quiet home, fluent English, fine-motor skills or a grown-up at home. Circle it and write one alternative beside it.

Part 2: Language and multilingual learners#

In the classroom#

  • Teach "I need help" in every home language in your class. Children and families can be your teachers, and it shows that every home language is an asset. [VERIFY translations with a fluent speaker or district translation service]
  • Pair with a bilingual buddy (a choice, not an obligation, and rotate so no one becomes the permanent translator).
  • Use pictures, gestures and the four hand signals, which do not depend on English. Preteach key words ("password," "trick," "trust," "private") and avoid idioms or explain them.
  • Simplify without dumbing down: short sentences, concrete words, visuals and a clear model (I do, we do, you do).
  • Let children respond in their strongest language, by drawing, pointing or whispering to a buddy.

With families#

  • Send letters in home languages where possible, and use a district translation service or professional interpreter for important conversations. [Translations pending in the Family Engagement Pack; ask your district.] The Family Engagement Pack includes a Spanish-translation-ready note.
  • Do not use a child as the interpreter for a sensitive conversation (such as a concern about their safety or a disclosure).
  • Use plain language (short, active sentences, and one idea per paragraph).

With disclosures#

A child who is learning English may find it far harder to disclose. Offer a drawing, a note in any language, a trusted bilingual adult (not a classmate), or an interpreter service for the reporting process. Do not rely on a sibling or friend to interpret.

Part 3: Students with disabilities#

Practical adjustments (apply across disability types)#

Practical adjustments (apply across disability types)
Need What you can do
Vision Large print, high contrast, read aloud, tactile cards, verbal descriptions of pictures.
Hearing (Deaf or hard of hearing) Visual supports, captions, written scripts, an interpreter if the child uses one, and a sign for HELP.
Motor or fine-motor Alternatives to cutting and writing: sorting by pointing, verbal responses, partner support, larger cards.
Speech or communication A printable "I need help" picture card, picture choices, assistive communication devices; allow all forms of communication as valid.
Attention or executive function Short chunks (the 15-minute structure helps), visual timers, clear routines, movement breaks.
Sensory sensitivity or autism A calm environment, forewarning of changes, no surprise loud sounds, a quiet corner, literal language, concrete scripts.
Intellectual disability Simple language, repetition, role-play with props, visuals, real-life practice.
Anxiety or trauma The pass card, the quiet corner, predictable structure, warnings before sensitive content.

Always check the student's IEP or 504 plan and talk with the special education team about adaptations before sensitive lessons. [VERIFY your school's procedures]

Extra safety teaching points#

Students with disabilities are, according to several studies, at higher risk of being targeted for abuse and bullying. [VERIFY; see the Inclusion and Equity Guide, Section 8.] Consider these extra points:

  • Clear, concrete rules and scripts work well: "If someone asks for a secret, say 'I will tell my helper.'"
  • Teach consent and body autonomy in age-appropriate terms in partnership with specialists.
  • Name more than one trusted adult, including adults who understand the student's communication style.
  • Notice behavior changes, because some children communicate distress through behavior, not words.
  • Make sure reporting channels are accessible (for example, a student who cannot speak can point to a picture card).

Part 4: Culture, family and belief#

What to keep in mind#

  • Families differ in who raises children, what is discussed openly, and how adults and children talk. In some homes, bodies, relationships or privacy are uncomfortable topics; in some, a child is not expected to disagree with an adult.
  • Trusted adults look different: a grandparent, aunt, coach, faith leader, older sibling or neighbor. Say "the people in your life," "a grown-up you trust," "family" and "caregiver," not only "mom and dad" (foster, kin care, same-sex parents and guardians all exist).
  • Do not stereotype. Learn from the families in front of you, and keep lessons about safety and skills, not beliefs.
  • Do not assume technology. Not every home has devices or a quiet space, and not every family is "tech-fluent" or "tech-fearful."

When a child is afraid of being "outed" or of their family's reaction#

Some children fear that telling will expose their identity, relationships or activities. You cannot promise secrecy where reporting duties apply, but you can say: "I will tell only the people who need to know, and I'll tell you who. I'll make sure your safety comes first." Involve the counselor early. [Consult policy on parental notification.]

Children who have no safe adult at home#

  • Never ask a child to "name a grown-up at home" in front of the class.
  • Make the school helpers the focus: teacher, counselor, nurse, principal, librarian, bus driver, lunch helper, recess helper.
  • Say: "Your teacher, your counselor and the nurse are on your team."

Identity-based harm#

LGBTQ+ students, students of color, students with disabilities, and students of particular religions can be targeted online in ways that combine bullying with identity. Challenge identity-based bullying right away and follow your bullying policy, which may also trigger additional federal and state duties for protected-class harassment. [VERIFY with counsel and your policy] Make sure students know that being targeted for who they are is never their fault.

Part 4b: Trauma-informed practice#

Many children have lived through hard things. A lesson about safety can touch a nerve. You do not need to know who. You teach as if any child might be affected.

Six practices#

Six practices
Practice What it looks like
Predictability Same routine every lesson. Say what is coming.
Choice Pass card, quiet corner, options for participation.
Safety No graphic content, no forced disclosure, no unannounced topic shifts.
Calm closings Breath, stretch, an anchoring sentence.
Warmth and respect A steady, kind tone; no sarcasm.
Collaboration Ask students what would help.

Language that helps, and language that harms#

Helps: "You have choices." "Take the time you need." "You did the right thing by telling." "Many people feel this." "It is okay to pass."

Harms: "Just get over it." "You are fine." "Why do you always..." "Everyone else can do it." "Stop being dramatic."

Remember#

Reactions that look like misbehavior (laughing, withdrawing, anger, shutting down) may be a child coping with a heavy topic. Respond with calm and curiosity, then check in privately.

Part 5: Low-internet and no-device homes#

Not every child has a device at home, and many families share one. The Hackles kit is unplugged by default (paper, cards, role-play) so that no child is disadvantaged. Still, you need to consider this at all times.

Part 5: Low-internet and no-device homes
Challenge What to do
No device at home Do all practice in class. Send paper take-homes. Never assign tasks that need a device or the internet at home.
Shared device Do not ask students to "check your own settings at home" unless a trusted adult can help and the family agrees.
Limited data, or no quiet or private space No video or large downloads as homework. Keep activities short and fine to do in a common area.
Family distrust of technology or school Start with respect; give choices; offer a brief meeting.
Public devices (library, school computers) Use them for guided practice, not homework. Teach the habits that matter: log out, do not save passwords, think before logging in.

A mindset shift: low-internet does not mean low-risk. Children without devices at home may spend more time on friends' devices, or in less supervised public spaces, and they often face scams aimed at families with less access to support. Teach everyone the same skills. Do not make low-device homes a "problem."

Part 6: Partnering with families#

Why it matters#

Families are a child's first and most lasting teachers. When home and school say the same thing (Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. You will never be in trouble for telling), children hear it more. And when a parent responds calmly to a small "oops," the next, bigger disclosure is far more likely.

Four principles of family partnership#

  1. Respect. Families love their children and want the best for them.
  2. Plain language. No jargon, no acronyms.
  3. Two-way. Ask for their ideas, questions and concerns.
  4. Transparency. Share what you will teach, when, and why. Make materials available.

Your toolkit (from the Family Engagement Pack)#

Take-home Letter A (general introduction, before you begin the unit); Letter B (sensitive-topic pre-notice, a week or so before higher-care lessons [Confirm your district's timeline with district counsel]); grade-band conversation starters (a family card at each lesson); an optional Family Tech Agreement template; the Family Code Word guide (for emergencies and voice-clone scams); reply slips for feedback; the "What to do if your child tells you something scary" one-pager; and the opt-out process (see the Counselor Heads-Up and Opt-Out Toolkit) for families who wish to opt a child out of sensitive lessons.

What to say to a family: three simple messages#

  1. "We teach children skills, not fear: to notice when something feels off, pause, check, and tell a trusted adult."
  2. "Your child will never be in trouble for telling. We hope you will say that at home, too."
  3. "Your child's lessons are kept free from graphic detail. We never ask students to share personal experiences. We collect no student accounts or data."

Sharing news of a concern#

When you contact a family about a small, non-urgent concern (for example, your child mentioned a game with a chat feature), keep it simple: warm opening, specific and neutral observation, a suggestion, an invitation. Do not do this when a child's safety may be at stake, or when a family member could be involved: in those cases, follow the Disclosure and Incident Guide and your policy before contacting any family.

Part 7: Handling parent pushback#

Some parents will be concerned, skeptical or upset. Most are acting out of love. A few have had hard experiences. Your calm is the most important tool you have.

The five-step response (L.E.A.R.N.)#

The five-step response (L.E.A.R.N.)
Step Meaning Example
Listen Let them finish; take notes "Tell me more about your worry."
Empathize Name the care behind the concern "I can tell how much you care about your daughter."
Agree where you can Find common ground "We both want children to be safe."
Respond with facts and options Explain what the lesson is, offer to share materials "Here is what we do. I would be glad to show you the plan."
Next step Offer a concrete action "Would you like a call with our counselor?"

Common concerns, with calm replies#

Common concerns, with calm replies
Concern A calm reply
"This is too much for young children." "I understand. The youngest children get no scary details: they practice knowing who to tell and saying 'I need help.' I'd be happy to share the plan."
"You are putting ideas in their heads." "That is a thoughtful worry. Research on prevention education suggests clear, age-appropriate skill teaching helps children, and the lessons avoid graphic detail. [VERIFY with a prevention source before citing] Children usually meet these things online already; we are giving them tools."
"This is my job, not the school's." "You are absolutely your child's first teacher. We support what you do at home, and you are welcome to review everything."
"I don't want my child taught about [a sensitive topic]." "Thank you for telling me. Families can opt a child out of specific lessons; the counselor can explain the process and the alternative activity." (See the Opt-Out Toolkit.)
"What about my values?" "These lessons focus on safety skills, not beliefs. I am glad to hear your perspective and to share what each lesson covers."
"Why are you collecting data on my child?" "We are not. Hackles collects no student accounts and no student data. Lessons are unplugged."
"My child says she is scared of the internet now." "Thank you for telling me. That is not what we want. Let me check in with her. We stress that most online experiences are fine and that telling is always okay."
"You are not an expert." "You are right, and I do not need to be. I am teaching a simple routine and making sure kids know who to tell."

What not to do#

Do not argue statistics in the moment, get defensive or sarcastic, promise things you cannot control, share another child's information, or discuss a disclosure with a family before consulting your policy. Do not send the matter to administration without telling the parent what you are doing (unless policy or safety requires otherwise).

When it escalates#

If a parent becomes angry, abusive or threatening, stay calm, set a boundary ("I want to help, and I will continue this when we can talk calmly"), end the conversation, and tell your administrator. You do not need to absorb abuse.

Scenario Studio#

Scenario 7.A: The newcomer and the poster#

A new student, recently arrived and still learning English, sits silently during the lesson. When asked to name three trusted adults, he looks stricken and shakes his head. Another student says loudly, "He doesn't have any!"

Discuss: What might be happening for him? How do you respond to the other student, and how can you redesign the activity to include him?

Expert-response guidance. Protect his dignity at once: "It is okay. We are all still learning who our helpers are." Do not ask him to name adults in front of the class again, and redirect the other student: "We do not talk about each other's families." Change the activity for everyone who finds it hard: give a picture card of school helpers and let children circle rather than name. Check in privately with a bilingual adult or interpreter, make sure he knows his helpers at school, and tell the counselor (not in alarm, so his support is thoughtful). Learn "I need help" in his home language.

Scenario 7.B: The family without internet#

You assign the family card with a link to a website. A student, Dani, returns it unread and says quietly, "We do not have internet." Others heard. A classmate says, "Everybody has internet."

Discuss: What is the issue with your assignment, how do you handle the moment, and how do you prevent it from recurring?

Expert-response guidance. The mistake was yours, not Dani's. Own it lightly: "Thank you for telling me. That is on me. I should not have assumed." Provide the paper version. To the class: "Lots of families have different setups. We make sure everyone can take part." Going forward, make every take-home printable and device-free, and never make an example of Dani. Consider quietly letting the counselor know if there are signs of broader hardship, so the family can be offered supports. [VERIFY your school's resources]

Scenario 7.C: The angry parent#

A father emails the principal and you: "Your 'safety' lesson told my daughter to keep secrets from her parents and tell the counselor. You are undermining me." The email is angry and threatening to go to the school board.

Discuss: What is the underlying worry, and what is right or wrong in his claim? How do you respond in writing, what do you do first, and what if he asks you to confirm what his daughter said in class?

Expert-response guidance. First inform your administrator and counselor, and agree on a coordinated reply. Be calm and warm in writing: acknowledge his love for his daughter; clarify that the lesson says the opposite of "keep secrets": it teaches that telling a trusted adult is always okay, and that parents are among the first people to tell. Attach the lesson outline. Offer a meeting or call, with the counselor present. Do not argue, and do not disclose anything his daughter said. If the daughter's words suggest a concern about home, follow the Disclosure and Incident Guide and your policy on whether and when to inform the family. [VERIFY policy] Document the exchange factually.

Quick Card: Module 7, Every Learner and Every Family#

Five principles: (1) many ways in (speak, point, draw, act, sort, write); (2) no one is singled out; (3) everyone has someone (school helpers count); (4) assume good intentions in families; (5) safety first: inclusion never lowers safeguards or skips reporting.

Quick adaptations

  • English learners: teach "I need help" in home languages (verify translations); use pictures and the four hand signals; preteach words; accept drawing, pointing or whispering. Never use a child as interpreter for a sensitive conversation.
  • Disability: check the IEP or 504 plan and talk with the special education team; offer alternatives to cutting and writing; picture cards, visual timers, a quiet corner, warning before changes; make reporting accessible.
  • Families: say "family," "caregiver," "grown-up." Never ask a child to name a grown-up at home in front of peers; focus on school helpers.
  • No device at home: lessons are unplugged; take-homes are printable and device-free; no "check your settings at home" unless a trusted adult can help.

Trauma-informed six: predictability, choice, safety (no graphic content, no forced disclosure), calm closings, warmth, collaboration. Laughing, withdrawing or anger may be coping.

Three family messages

  1. We teach skills, not fear.
  2. Your child will never be in trouble for telling.
  3. No graphic detail, no personal sharing, no student data collected.

L.E.A.R.N. for pushback: Listen. Empathize. Agree where you can. Respond with facts and options. Next step.

Never: argue statistics, get defensive, share another child's information, or discuss a disclosure with a family before consulting your policy.

If a child's safety may be at stake, or a family member could be involved: follow your policy and the Disclosure and Incident Guide before contacting any family.

Angry or threatening parent: set a boundary ("I will continue when we can talk calmly"), end the conversation, tell your administrator.

Opt-out request: thank them, explain the process and the alternative activity, involve the counselor.
