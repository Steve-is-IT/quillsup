At a glance. This is the pedagogy module: how to teach online safety so that it sticks. You'll learn the five rules of safety teaching that works, see "good versus weak" lesson examples side by side, practice shame-free language, plan a spiral across the year, and add games, songs and movement. Quick Start learners: read the five rules and the "Good vs weak" table, and skip the rest for now.

Learning objectives#

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  1. State the five rules of safety teaching that works and give an example of each.
  2. Tell a good lesson from a weak one, and rewrite a weak one.
  3. Use shame-free language and avoid fear appeals.
  4. Design a role-play for any skill.
  5. Explain how a spiral curriculum works across grades K-12 and across a single school year, so that nothing taught in September has to be re-explained from scratch in May.
  6. Add games, songs and movement that serve the learning goal.

Welcome: the CPR lesson#

Nobody learns CPR from a lecture on cardiac arrest. They kneel on the floor, push on a plastic torso to the rhythm of "Stayin' Alive," and practice until their hands know what to do. When the real moment comes, people don't rise to the occasion. They fall to the level of their practice.

That is the whole of this module. A child being pressured online at 9:40 on a Tuesday night will not recall a slide. She might recall a phrase she practiced, a hand signal she did with a giggle, or the face of a teacher who said "you will never be in trouble for telling me." So for any lesson, ask: What will the child be able to DO afterwards, and have they practiced doing it?

Part 1: The five rules of safety teaching that works#

These rules draw on prevention science (see Module 1). Where evidence is adjacent rather than direct, we say so. [VERIFY specifics when citing publicly]

Rule 1: Skills over information#

Information tells a child what is true. A skill is something they can do. Good safety lessons teach a small number of facts and a concrete skill to practice.

Rule 1: Skills over information
Information (necessary but not enough) Skill (what makes the difference)
"Scammers pretend to be someone you know." "Check another way: call or ask a trusted adult before you click or pay."
"Don't share personal information." "When someone asks for a password, say 'No, I'll check with my grown-up.'"
"Cyberbullying is harmful." "Be an upstander: check in privately, say 'not cool,' save the evidence, tell an adult."
"Pictures you share can be misused." "If someone threatens you, STOP, SAVE, TELL, REPORT."

SAVE means keep the messages, usernames and dates; it never means saving or sharing images (see Module 6).

Teacher test: for every lesson, finish the sentence "After this, students can ____ (a verb they can show)."

Rule 2: Rehearse it, out loud, with your body#

Children learn skills by practicing them, ideally several times and in different ways. In Hackles, practice includes role-play, scripts, hand signals ("Hackles Up," Pause Palm, Check Eyes, Tell Wave), card sorts, short games and partner scripts. The research on school-based abuse prevention, which has been better studied than online safety, finds that active skill practice is better supported than lecture. [VERIFY, Finkelhor et al., 2021, and related reviews]

Why role-play works: it makes the words available when the real moment comes. A child who has said "I need help" ten times in a silly voice can say it once in a hard moment.

Teaching tips:

  • Keep role-plays short (1 to 3 minutes), scripted at first, and never with real personal details.
  • Use puppets, stuffed animals or character cards for younger children, so no child plays the "bad guy."
  • Use "the adult plays the difficult person" with a clear signal, such as a hat, for when you step out of character.
  • Let students play the helper, not the victim.

Rule 3: Calm, hopeful, no fear appeals#

A fear appeal is a message that tries to change behavior by frightening people ("Predators are everywhere!"). The general research on fear appeals is mixed, and they tend to work only when people also feel there is something they can effectively do. There is limited direct evidence on online safety. [VERIFY before saying fear appeals are proven to backfire in this field.] What we do know from practice: scary stories raise anxiety in some children, make others tune out, and can make the later act of telling feel riskier.

Replace the threat with the tool:

Rule 3: Calm, hopeful, no fear appeals
Fear framing Hackles framing
"If you talk to a stranger, something terrible could happen." "If anyone asks for secrets or pictures, your hackles go up. Pause and tell."
"You will be hacked!" "Two locks are better than one."
"Your life will be ruined." "If a mistake happens, we fix it together. Telling is always okay."

Rule 4: Shame-free language, every time#

Shame is the biggest obstacle to help-seeking. So the words matter. Hackles language promises three things:

  1. A false alarm is fine and welcome.
  2. It is never your fault.
  3. You will not be in trouble for telling.

Say them out loud often. Children need to hear them many times, especially older ones, who assume the opposite.

Language to use and avoid

Rule 4: Shame-free language, every time
Avoid Use instead
"Don't be silly, it's obvious." "These tricks are designed to fool everyone."
"You should have known better." "I am glad you told me. Let's figure out next steps."
"Why did you click it?" "What happened next?"
"Be careful, or else." "If something feels off, here is what to do."
"Good kids don't do that." "Lots of people make mistakes. What matters is what we do next."
"Victim" (to a child) "The person it happened to" or just "you."
"Predator," "stranger danger" "Someone who is pressuring you" or "someone you do not know well."
"Stupid," "dumb," "naive" Never.

A subtle one: "never" language. "Never share your name!" invites failure, because children inevitably do. Prefer "think first," "ask a trusted adult," "check first."

Rule 5: Spiral, repeat, and connect#

One lesson does not do it. Skills need to be revisited, a little deeper each time, over months and years. That is the idea of a spiral curriculum.

The Hackles spiral. Each grade band returns to the same core routine (Hackles up? Pause, Check, Tell), and the same strands (locking accounts, spotting tricks, protecting privacy, handling pressure, being kind, getting help), at a more advanced level.

Rule 5: Spiral, repeat, and connect
Strand K-2 3-5 6-8 9-12
Tell / get help "My Tell-Someone Team" "The Help Ladder" "Help Ladder: Real Practice" "Be the Helper"
Passwords and accounts "Secret Passwords" "Passphrase Lab" "Account Defense" "Credentials and Passkeys"
Pressure and grooming "Online Friends vs. Real Friends" "Game Chat Red Flags" "Grooming Patterns," "Pressure Online" "Coercion in Relationships Online," "Sextortion and Image Abuse"

Three of the eight strands, as examples. The Teacher Guides carry them all.

In a school year. Teach one 15-minute lesson a week for ten weeks, then revisit key skills with 3-minute "warm-ups" in the following months ("Hackles check-ins").

Connect to your curriculum. Weave the routine into ELA (evaluating sources), math (password combinations), science, social studies and advisory. A skill practiced in four classes is practiced four times.

Part 2: "Good vs weak" lesson examples#

Here are three pairs of lessons on the same topic. Notice what separates them: not budget or gadgets, but what students do.

Pair 1: Passwords (Grade 4)#

Pair 1: Passwords (Grade 4)
Weak lesson Good lesson
Format Teacher shows a slide on "10 password rules" and reads each one. Students compete in small groups to build the funniest 4-word passphrase, then use a "crack timer" card to see how long it would take to guess.
What students do Listen. Copy rules. Create, compare, discuss, decide.
Tone "If you use a bad password you could lose everything." "Silly and long beats short and clever."
Ending Quiz on the rules. Exit ticket: "Tell a partner your new rule for passwords. When do you tell a grown-up?"
Why it works Active, memorable, skill plus reason, calm.

Pair 2: Telling an adult (Grade 1)#

Pair 2: Telling an adult (Grade 1)
Weak lesson Good lesson
Format "If anyone ever makes you feel uncomfortable, tell a trusted adult." (Said once.) Children trace a hand, name three trusted adults on three fingers, practice saying "I need help" to a partner, and practice what to do if the first adult is too busy ("keep telling").
What students do Nod. Draw, speak, rehearse.
Why it works Names real people. Rehearses the words. Builds "keep telling."

Pair 3: Sensitive topic: pressure online (Grade 8)#

Pair 3: Sensitive topic: pressure online (Grade 8)
Weak lesson Good lesson
Format Graphic stories about what happens to children who share pictures. Calm case files about a fictional character; students identify the pattern (flattery, secrecy, the move, the ask), rehearse STOP, SAVE, TELL, REPORT, and name a trusted adult.
Tone "Don't send pictures or you will regret it." "If someone threatens you, it is never your fault. Here is what you do."
Risk Shame, fear, silence. Shame-free, hopeful, a path.
Why it works No graphic detail. Skill. Help-seeking.

The "good lesson" checklist#

A good Hackles lesson:

  • Checkbox: Has one clear skill students will practice.
  • Checkbox: Includes a rehearsal, not only a discussion.
  • Checkbox: Uses no fear, no shame, no graphic detail.
  • Checkbox: Gives students a role other than "victim."
  • Checkbox: Ends with who to tell and "you are never in trouble for telling."
  • Checkbox: Takes about 15 minutes and is repeatable.
  • Checkbox: Includes a way to check what students learned (exit ticket, thumbs, a sort).
  • Checkbox: Offers a way for a child to disclose safely, and the teacher is ready for it.

Part 3: Age-appropriate by band#

Part 3: Age-appropriate by band
Band What it sounds like What to avoid
K-2 (5 to 7) Concrete, physical, silly. Puppets, hand signals, chants. One idea per lesson. "I need help." Abstract rules, internet jargon, detail about harm.
3-5 (8 to 10) Labs, detective roles, games, cards. Structured group work. Introduces reasons ("why"). Fear stories, long lectures, shaming.
6-8 (11 to 13) Case Files, analyst roles, peer dynamics, real-world patterns. Respect for their expertise. Talking down; acting as if they know nothing.
9-12 (14 to 18) Threat modeling, ethics, autonomy, relationships, careers. Adult tone, plain language. Lecturing; moralizing; hypocrisy.

Hackles' Hackle mascot appears in K-5 materials and only as a small corner icon from grade 6 on. Teens do not want a cartoon talking to them, and they will notice if you do not respect that.

Part 4: Fun is not a luxury#

Teachers sometimes worry that games and songs make the topic seem unserious. The opposite is true: fun is what makes a topic repeatable, and repetition is what makes it stick. The rule is simple: play with the skill, never with the harm. We make games about spotting a fake message, never about a victim's experience.

Songs and chants (original, free to use)#

Songs help young children memorize a routine. Keep them short. Here are two original ones for you to adapt.

The Hackles Chant (to a clapping beat; K-3)

Hackles up! (hand up the back of the neck)
Something's funny, something's weird!
Pause! (palm out)
Don't click yet, we'll be steered!
Check! (point eyes with two fingers)
Who is this and what's the ask?
Tell! (hand on heart, reach out)
A grown-up's help is not a task!

The Passphrase Song (to the tune of "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star"; K-3)

Four silly words are strong and long,
Put them in a crazy song.
Keep them secret, keep them mine,
Share them with a grown-up kind.
Four silly words are strong and long,
Now you know it, sing along!

Older students? Skip the songs, unless it is ironic. Use a chant, a rhythm or a catchphrase instead: "Pause. Check. Tell." works. For teens, a one-line "rule of thumb" delivered with a straight face is the equivalent.

Movement#

  • Four corners: Label corners "Hackles up," "Not sure," "Probably fine," "Tell someone." Students walk to a corner for each scenario.
  • Human spectrum: Line students up between two signs ("I would pause" and "I would ask for help") using invented scenarios. Let students pass, and never ask anyone to explain a personal choice.
  • Stretch and breath: End sensitive lessons with a calm ritual (a deep breath, a shake-out, a stretch) so children leave regulated.

One caution about "gamification"#

Avoid competitive games that let children lose in front of peers on sensitive content, and games that reward children for disclosing information. Collaboration (teams, class totals) usually works better than leaderboards.

Part 5: A lesson design recipe#

  1. Pick the skill (one verb) and the signal that should make hackles go up.
  2. Hook (2 minutes): a warm story, riddle or question, never scary.
  3. Rehearsal (8 minutes): a game, role-play, card sort or script.
  4. Takeaway (2 minutes): one sentence and one gesture, with "never your fault" and "tell."
  5. Check: an exit ticket or thumbs. Did it land?

Module 5 turns this into the full 15-minute structure.

Do it yourself#

Activity 4.1: Fix the Fear Lesson (15 minutes)#

You need: paper or a document.

Below is a weak lesson plan. Rewrite it as a good lesson using the five rules.

Weak lesson: "Internet Safety Assembly" (Grade 5, 30 minutes). The teacher shows a PowerPoint with 20 slides. Slide 1: "Predators are online!" Slides 2 to 10: lists of rules (never share your name, never talk to strangers, never post pictures, never meet anyone). Slide 11: a news story about a child who was harmed. Slides 12 to 20: more rules. The teacher ends with "Any questions?" Nobody asks. The teacher says "good, because you know the rules now."

Your rewrite should include: (1) one skill, (2) a short hook without fear, (3) an 8-minute rehearsal, (4) a takeaway with "never your fault" and "tell," (5) a check.

Sample rewrite. Skill: "Say 'I'll check with my grown-up' and tell when someone online asks for something." Hook (2 min): a short story about Hackle noticing a "free coins" pop-up. Rehearsal (8 min): partner role-play with a card that says "Free coins! Give me your password." Partner A practices Pause Palm and the words, Partner B plays the pop-up with a silly voice, then swap. Discuss (3 min): "How did your body feel? What is a safe thing to do?" Takeaway (2 min): "When my hackles go up: Pause. Check. Tell. It is never my fault, and a false alarm is fine." Check: exit ticket, "Write or draw one grown-up you would tell."

Activity 4.2: Shame-Free Translator (10 minutes)#

Translate each shaming sentence into a shame-free one.

Activity 4.2: Shame-Free Translator (10 minutes)
Shaming sentence Your shame-free version
"How could you click that link?"
"I told you this would happen."
"You are old enough to know better."
"Now your account is gone, are you happy?"
"Don't be dramatic, it is only a message."

Sample answers. "Thank you for telling me. Let's check what happened." / "I am glad you came to me. Let's fix it." / "These tricks fool grown-ups, too. Say each one out loud, in your calmest voice. How would your face look? Your face matters as much as your words.

Scenario Studio#

Scenario 4.A: "Can't we just show a video?"#

A colleague says, "I do not have time for activities. Can I just show the video from last year?" The video features dramatic reenactments of a teen being tricked and ends with a stern warning.

Discuss: What are the real constraints behind this request? What is wrong with a video as the entire lesson, and what is a 5-minute compromise?

Expert-response guidance. Acknowledge the time pressure honestly. A video can be a hook, but not the lesson. Offer a compromise: show a short clip (or none), then run a 5-minute rehearsal: partners practice the Pause and Check script. And add the three promises (false alarm fine, not your fault, no trouble for telling). Share that you are happy to co-plan. Do not disparage the colleague's effort.

Scenario 4.B: The kid who "already knows it all"#

In your grade 7 class, a confident student says, "This is baby stuff. Everyone knows not to click scam links." Several classmates laugh.

Discuss: What is the student's need? How do you keep the activity valuable without a power struggle, and how might you use the student's confidence?

Expert-response guidance. Honor expertise: "You may well be right, and I would love your help. Can you be our lead analyst on this case?" Give the student a role (an expert panel, a "red team" that writes the trickiest fake message). Raise the challenge: use a more subtle example, such as a message from a "friend's" hacked account. Gently reinforce: "Even experts get caught when they are tired or rushed. That is why we practice a routine and not just knowledge." Avoid the power struggle; invite, do not argue.

Scenario 4.C: The giggling role-play#

During a role-play on "someone asks you to move to a private app," two students giggle and make it a joke. One says, "I would just send the pic lol." The room goes quiet.

Discussion questions

  1. What are the possible meanings of "I would just send the pic"?
  2. How do you respond in the moment, without humiliating anyone?
  3. What follow-up is needed?

Expert-response guidance. In the moment: calm, brief, no shaming. "Thanks. Lots of people feel pressure about that. That is exactly why we practice. Let's try another response together." Redirect to the skill: practice STOP, SAVE, TELL, REPORT or the "No, I'm going to tell" script. Treat the comment as possibly meaningful: follow up privately and casually after class. "I noticed what you said. Is there anything you want to talk about?" Do not interrogate. If anything suggests a child has sent or been pressured to send images, or is being threatened, follow Module 6 and your reporting policy; do not view any image. Quick Card: Module 4, Teaching Safety That Works#

Five rules

  1. Skills over information. Finish: "After this, students can ____."
  2. Rehearse it out loud, with your body. Role-plays are 1 to 3 minutes with invented details; students play the helper; an adult or puppet plays the difficult part, with a signal for stepping out of character.
  3. Calm and hopeful. Replace the threat with the tool.
  4. Shame-free language, every time.
  5. Spiral: repeat across weeks and years.

Three promises (say them often): A false alarm is fine and welcome. It is never your fault. You will not be in trouble for telling.

Say / avoid

Say Avoid
"I am glad you told me. Let's figure out next steps." "You should have known better."
"What happened next?" "Why did you click it?"
"These tricks are designed to fool everyone." "Don't be silly."
"Someone who is pressuring you" "Predator," "stranger danger"
"Think first," "check first" "Never share your name!"

STOP, SAVE, TELL, REPORT (threats): stop replying and never pay; save messages, usernames and dates (never images); tell a trusted adult; report.

Good lesson checklist: one skill; a rehearsal; no fear, shame or graphic detail; a role other than victim; ends with who to tell; about 15 minutes; a quick check; a safe way to disclose and a teacher ready for it.

Gestures: Hackles Up, Pause Palm, Check Eyes, Tell Wave.

Games: Phish or Real card hold-up; Hackles Up Freeze; Tell Chain; Two Truths and a Scam. Play with the skill, never with the harm. No leaderboards, and no games that let a child lose in front of peers on sensitive content.

Design recipe: skill and signal; hook (2 min); rehearsal (8); takeaway (2: skill, gesture, promise); check.

Rhythm: one 15-minute lesson a week for ten weeks, then 3-minute check-ins.

Never in class: live attacks on real services, or attacks on school systems or each other.
