About Hackles and Competence Collective#

Hackles comes from Competence Collective, a small group of educators and practitioners who think every child and teen should learn to stay safe and kind online, and that nobody should have to pay for the lesson. So it's free to print, share and adapt for non-commercial use, with credit. The license line is on the title page.

The name comes from a feeling you already know. Your hackles are the hairs that rise on the back of your neck when something is off. Hackles teaches children to notice that feeling and act on it: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell. False alarms are welcome. And if the feeling got ignored, or someone was fooled anyway, it is never the child's fault. The next step is the same every time: tell someone.

Hackles is the first step on a path that continues with free resources such as picoCTF and CYBER.ORG. College and professional learners can go on to Competence Collective's Red Herrings (redherrings.app) and Hotwash (hotwa.sh).

Welcome, Teacher#

You don't need to know anything about technology to teach this. You need a calm voice and a few sheets of paper. You also need to be willing to say one sentence until you're sick of it: "If something online feels yucky, confusing, or scary, it is never your fault, and you can tell a grown-up who helps."

Everything is scripted, so if you can read a picture book aloud you can teach these lessons, and since nothing in the program needs a screen you won't be fighting a projector either. Each lesson ends with something the children do, and not only something they heard.

What this program believes#

  • Skills, not scares. Children this age learn by doing: saying the words, making the motions, sorting the cards. We practice the response ("I need help"). We don't rehearse the danger.
  • Never your fault. Every lesson says it out loud. A child who expects blame stays quiet. A child who expects help tells.
  • Trust your hackles. "Hackles" is our name for the tingly uh-oh feeling when something seems odd, too good to be true, pushy, secret, or scary. Children learn to notice it and act. A false alarm is welcome, and nobody gets in trouble for one.
  • Never shame. No "bad kids," no "dumb mistakes." Everyone clicks the wrong thing sometimes, grown-ups included.
  • Teach the behavior, not the app. Scenarios say "a game chat" or "a video," never a brand name. Apps change. The skills don't.
  • Telling is the goal. If a child finishes Year 1 of this program knowing three trusted adults and one sentence to say, it worked. That's the whole bar.

What this program does NOT do#

  • It doesn't explain in detail how scammers, predators, or hackers work.
  • Children never play the person who causes harm. They play the one who tells or the one who pauses, and sometimes the friend who helps.
  • Nobody has to share their own experiences. Sharing is invited, never required.
  • It collects nothing from children and needs no accounts or devices. No student accounts, no student data collected, ever.

How to Use This Guide#

The 15-minute rhythm#

Same five parts every time. The children always know what comes next.

The 15-minute rhythm
Part Time What happens
Hook 2 min You read a short word-for-word script, often with a puppet or character card.
Activity 8 min Children move, sort, draw, or role-play. The skill is practiced here.
Discuss 3 min You ask 3 to 5 simple questions. Thumbs, pointing, and short answers all count.
Takeaway + Exit ticket 2 min One-line summary linked to Pause, Check, Tell. Children mark a one-question paper exit ticket.
Total 15 min

A few tips on the rhythm:

  • Use a visible timer or a chime. A change of part should never come as a surprise.
  • If the Activity runs short, run it again with a new volunteer pair. If it runs long, trim the Discuss questions. Never trim the Takeaway.
  • You can split a lesson over two days (Hook and Activity one day, Discuss, Takeaway and Exit ticket the next). Just don't skip the Takeaway.
  • Sit with the children, on the carpet if you have one. Low and slow.

Fun Boosters, and keeping 15 minutes real with 25 to 30 little ones#

  • Every lesson has a boxed Fun Boosters section right after the Activity: an energizer, a game variant, a chant or song line (all original), a silly-voice or puppet moment, and a Level-Up Challenge. They are optional, and they are not extra minutes. Pick ONE and fit it inside your 15: the energizer can stand in for a wiggly transition, the puppet moment can take over part of the Hook, and everything you didn't use waits for an "Encore" (the first 2 minutes of the next lesson, a rainy-day 5 minutes, a free-choice station). One only.
  • Songs and chants: Sing to any tune you like, or use a clap-stomp beat. Nobody is grading your singing. Silly is the goal.
  • Reset in 10 seconds: Say "Hackles Down!" Children shake out their spikes, sit on their spot, and put their hands in their laps. Use it between every part. Yes, every one.
  • Card Captains: In sorting lessons (3, 5, 6, 7, 8), give each table a "Card Captain" to pass out and collect cards. Rotate the job each lesson. It saves about a minute of clean-up.
  • Hackle puppet rule: Hackle is curious and silly, and now and then makes a funny, harmless mistake so the children can catch it ("Pause, Hackle!"). Hackle never plays someone who hurts, tricks, or pressures anyone. In the sensitive lessons (6 and 9), Hackle stays calm and helpful.

Before you begin (once, about 30 minutes)#

  1. Read the Disclosure Boxes in this guide and your school's child-protection policy. Learn the name and room number of your designated person (counselor, administrator, or designated safeguarding lead) and write it here: ____
  2. Find out your school's rules and your state's law on mandatory reporting. In nearly every US state, teachers are mandatory reporters of suspected child abuse or neglect. Deadlines and who you call differ by state and district. [VERIFY with your district and state statute.]
  3. Ask whether your district requires parent notification or opt-out for Lesson 9 (My Body Is Mine) or for body-safety instruction in general. Some do. [VERIFY.] Send the notice at least a week ahead of Lesson 9.
  4. Print and cut the Reproducibles you need (each lesson's Materials section lists them) and copy the Student Activity Book pages.
  5. Hang the Hackles poster (Hackles up? Pause-Check-Tell), described below, where every child can see it.
  6. Pick a spot for a child who needs a quiet moment mid-lesson: a calm corner, or a chair near you. Tell the children about it in Lesson 1.

Be ready for children who disclose#

Young children tell things at odd moments: during a story, in the lunch line, halfway through a drawing with a crayon in each hand. This program teaches them to tell. Some will. Every lesson has a boxed "If a student discloses" section. Read all ten before you teach Lesson 1. The core points hold every time:

If a student discloses

The five core points for any disclosure

  • Stay calm and listen. Your face and voice tell the child how big this is. Aim for warm and steady.
  • Say: "Thank you for telling me." "I believe you." "It is not your fault." "I am glad you told me." "I'm going to get help from someone whose job is to keep kids safe."
  • Do not promise to keep it secret. Do not promise "everything will be okay." Do not ask leading questions, press for details, or ask the child to repeat the story. Do not investigate. Do not confront anyone. Do not ask the child to write it down.
  • Write down the child's exact words and what happened, as soon as you can, privately. Facts only: what you heard and saw, date and time. No guesses.
  • Report right away to your school's designated person and follow your state's mandatory-reporting law, which means reporting promptly yourself and not leaving the decision to someone else (your state sets the deadline). [Confirm your state's rules and deadline with district counsel.]
  • If a child is in immediate danger, call 911.

You don't decide whether a disclosure is "true," and you don't need proof. Your job: listen, be kind, pass it on to the people trained to take it from there.

The Hackles Routine: Hackles up? Pause, Check, Tell#

Every lesson in the K-12 program uses this same routine. It starts with a feeling, then three action words. In K-2 there are four hand signals as well, so children learn the whole thing with their bodies long before they can read a word of it.

What are hackles? For ages 5 to 7, say it this way: "Hackles are the tingly uh-oh feeling. It is what happens when something feels funny, yucky, too good to be true, pushy, secret, or a little scary." Hackle the mascot has tangerine spikes that stand up when something is off, just like the feeling. Children don't have to explain it. They only have to notice it and know what to do next.

Two promises to repeat often: (1) A false alarm is fine. If the tingly feeling turns out to be nothing, good. Checking is never silly. (2) It is never your fault. A child who felt the tingle and didn't stop, or got tricked anyway, did nothing wrong. They tell a grown-up, and the grown-up helps.

Why Hackle? Hackle is a friendly baby hedgehog whose quills stand up when something feels off and lie down again when a grown-up helps. That is the noticing in one picture. A hedgehog also curls up to Pause, peeks out to Check and goes to the family for the Tell, so the whole routine fits in one gentle, silly character for children who get upset by scary images. "Hackles" are the raised hair or neck feathers of an animal that senses danger, and a hedgehog's quills stand up the way hackles do. Hackle is not a dog and not a cartoon hedgehog you may have seen on a screen: no blue, no sneakers, no racing. If you need a pronoun, Hackle uses "they/them."

5. Activity (8 min): "My Helper Hand"#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Group size: whole class on the carpet for the first minute, then at tables in groups of 4 to 6 (5 to 7 tables for 25-30 children). One adult circulates (a second adult or aide helps if available).

Steps:

  1. (1 min) Teach the three kinds of helpers. Show the poster. "A trusted grown-up is someone who listens to you, helps you, and keeps you safe. They might be someone in your family. They might be a teacher, a nurse, a counselor, or a principal. They might be a bus driver or a grandmother or a neighbor you know well with your family."
  2. (1 min) Name school helpers together. On chart paper, draw or write each helper the children name: teacher, school counselor, nurse, principal, librarian, bus driver, lunch helper, recess helper. Point to each as you say it. Say: "Any of these people can help you. You can always tell any grown-up at school."
  3. (3 min) Fill the Helper Hand. Children use the hand already printed on the Lesson 1 sheet. (Tracing their own hand is a lovely free-time extra, but with 25 to 30 five- and six-year-olds it can swallow the whole lesson.)     • "I need help." (Say it with a hand reaching out.)
    • Then the longer one: "Something happened and I need help."
    • Partner says back: "Thank you for telling me. I will help."
      Then switch roles. Take two turns each.

9. If a student discloses#

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (this lesson is the one most likely to prompt it)
Say:

  • "Thank you for telling me. I am glad you told me."
  • "I believe you."
  • "It is not your fault."
  • "I am going to get help from someone whose job is to keep kids safe."

Don't say:

  • "I promise I won't tell anyone." (You cannot keep that promise.)
  • "Why didn't you tell me sooner?" or "Are you sure?" (this sounds like blame or doubt)
  • Any leading question ("Did he touch you?"). Ask nothing beyond "Is there anything else you want to tell me?"

Do: Stay calm. Listen. Keep it short and private. Do not investigate or confront anyone. Write the child's own words and the time as soon as you can.
Escalate: Tell your school's designated person (counselor/administrator) immediately, and file the report the law requires. Call 911 if the child is in immediate danger.
Mandatory-reporting reminder: In nearly every US state, teachers must report suspected abuse or neglect right away, often the same day, directly and not by passing it along. You do not need proof. [Confirm your state and district rules with district counsel.] Know who your reporting person is before teaching this lesson.

Why this lesson is higher risk: Children are being invited to think about who they would tell. Some have already been trying to tell. Plan 5 to 10 minutes after the lesson, if possible, when you or a colleague can be available.

  • Support: Pre-draw faces on the fingers and let children circle three. Pair with a buddy. Allow pointing to a card of school helpers instead of drawing.

  • Language access: Teach "I need help" in each home language in your classroom (children and families can teach you). Use the picture of the helpers with labels in home languages.   2. (1 min) Show what NOT to use. Hold up three cards: your real name, your birthday (pretend), "1234." Say: "These are easy to guess. We don't use them." Children shake heads. (Do not use any real child's name or details.)
  3. (3 min) Build a silly password. At each table, children each pick one card from each pile: a Color/Feeling, an Animal/Thing, and a Food/Silly word. They line the three cards up in a row and say them aloud. Examples: "sleepy / robot / noodle," "green / elephant / pancake." Each child then draws their silly password as a picture on the Lesson 2 page (three little pictures in a row, not the words). Say clearly: "This is a pretend password for practice. Don't use it for real. We'll make a real one with our grown-up at home."
  4. (2 min) Passwords are private game. Children stand in a circle. Teacher holds the treasure chest and says: "Who may know my password?" Children whisper: "Only me and my grown-up!" Then teacher mimes a sneaky friend asking: "Tell me your password!" Children respond with the Pause Palm and say: "Passwords are private!" Practice 3 times with a different "asker" each time: a friend, someone in a game chat (teacher holds up a pretend screen), a person who says "I'm from the game company, tell me your password." For every one, children say: "Passwords are private. I'll tell my grown-up."
  5. (1 min) Lock the chest. A child volunteer puts the key into the chest and closes it. Say: "Click! Locked. Now our things are safe."

Fun Boost

12. Teacher notes#

  • Why three random words? Long passphrases made of several unrelated words are widely recommended by security guidance (for example, the UK's National Cyber Security Centre "three random words": "Combine three random words to create a password that's 'long enough and strong enough'") [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: ncsc.gov.uk].   • Pretend passwords only. Do not collect, record, or have children say aloud any real password. If a child starts to say their real password, gently stop them: "That one is real. Keep it private. We're using pretend ones."
  • School logins. Many K-2 classrooms use shared or picture logins. Teach children that "private" here means from other children and from outsiders. Teachers and parents may know. [VERIFY your school's login policy.]

    • "What if I forget it?" "Ask your grown-up. They can help."
    • "My mom says my password is 1111." "A longer, silly one is stronger. You can tell your mom what we learned!"

Unplugged (default):

  • Pop-up cards, 8 to 10 (Reproducibles, Section 3). Some are "pop-ups to Pause" (e.g., "YOU WON! TAP HERE!", "FREE GEMS!", "YOUR TABLET IS BROKEN! CALL NOW!"), and some are "okay screens" (e.g., "Time for a story," "Level 2 loading").

4. Hook (2 min)#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Hold up the empty screen frame.

"This is a pretend tablet screen. Let's imagine Maya is playing a game with her dad right next to her. Suddenly, POP! (pop the card up from behind the frame with a loud "POP!" and a little jump: 'YOU WON! FREE GEMS! TAP HERE!' Children will jump or giggle. That is the point: pop-ups are made to startle us.) Something pops up on the screen! It is flashing. It says she won something free. It says, 'Tap here now!'

"Maya is very curious. She wants to tap it! But then her hackles go up, that tingly uh-oh feeling, and she remembers Hackle. What does Hackle say? (Children: 'Pause!') Maya puts up her hand like a stop sign. She does not tap. She turns to her dad and says, 'Dad, something popped up. Can you check?' And her dad says, 'Thank you for asking me. That's exactly right.'

"A pop-up is a message that jumps onto a screen. Some are okay. Some are tricky. Tricky ones say things like 'FREE!' and 'YOU WON!' and 'HURRY!' They want you to tap fast, without thinking. We will practice not tapping fast. We will pause and ask."

5. Activity (8 min): "Pop-Up Parade"#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Group size: whole class seated or standing in a circle. Teacher is the "screen." For 25-30 children, play the full class together, then do a second round in pairs.

Steps:

  1. (1 min) Teach the signal. Practice: "When something pops up and I'm not sure, I...?" Children do Pause Palm and say "Pause!" Then: "Then I...?" "Ask a grown-up!" (Check Eyes, look for a grown-up.)
  2. (4 min) Pop-Up Parade. Hold up the screen frame. Slip a pop-up card behind it so children see it. For each card, children hold up red (Pause and ask!) or green (okay to keep going, with the grown-up still nearby). After each, ask: "What made you choose red/green?" Look for answers like "it says free," "it is flashing," "it says hurry." Gently confirm. For the "okay" cards (e.g., a loading screen, a story), say: "This one is fine. And even when it's fine, a grown-up is still close by."
    • Run 6 to 8 cards. Mix pop-ups to Pause (60%) with okay screens (40%).
    • Never show or describe a real scary or inappropriate image. All cards are cartoon-simple and gentle.
  3. (2 min) Tricky words chant. Write three tricky words large on chart paper: FREE! WON! HURRY! Say: "When we see these on a screen, we don't tap. We Pause." Children chant: "Free? Won? Hurry? Pause and ask!" with the hand signals.
  4. (1 min) The grown-up responds. Pick a volunteer to play "the grown-up." The child with the "screen" says: "Something popped up. Can you check?" The volunteer answers (teacher whispers it): "Thank you for pausing and asking. Let's look together." Switch two more times so several children play each role. Celebrate: "Great pause! Great asking!"

Fun Boost

Fun Boosters (pick ONE inside your 15 minutes; save the rest for an Encore)

  • Energizer (30 seconds): "Pop-Up Pop!" Children crouch like sleeping pop-ups. When you flash a red-border card they POP up, freeze in a Pause Palm and say "Ask!" On a green-border card they stay crouched and hum calmly. Everyone moves on every card.
  • Game variant: "Red Light, Green Light, Pop-Up!" Children march in place. Red card: freeze with a Pause Palm. Green card: keep marching, waving to the grown-up who is "still near." Nobody is ever out: a child who keeps marching on a red card gets a friendly "Ooh, Pause Palm!" and rejoins. Mistakes are practice.
  • Chant (stomp-clap): "Free? Won? Hurry, hurry! / Hold on, wait, there's no need to worry! / Palm up high, a breath for me, / Ask a grown-up, one, two, three!"
  • Silly voice / puppet moment: Read each pop-up card in an over-the-top game-show voice ("YOU'VE WON A MILLION FLUFFY SOCKS!") and every time a tricky word appears, Hackle's quills go "BOING!" (children make the sound). Then Hackle says in a tiny calm voice, "Hmm. Hackles up. Pause."
  • Level-Up Challenge: Tricky-Word Hunt. 1. Header, table 4
Title The Oops Button
Grade band K-2 (ages 5-7)

Big idea If I tap something yucky, scary, or by mistake, I say "Oops!" and tell a grown-up. I am not in trouble.
Practiced skill Say "Oops, I need help" right away; receive a calm "Thank you for telling me"

2. Standards (primary only, approximate)#

  • ISTE: 2 Digital Citizen (approx. 2.b) [2016 numbering shown; current ISTE v4.02 equivalent: 1.2.a, 1.2.b; approximate. VERIFIED 2026-10-05: ISTE Standards v4.02 PDF]

3. Materials#

Unplugged (default):

  • The Oops Button card, one per child (Reproducibles, Section 5), printed on cardstock if possible
  • A big red paper "Oops Button" (a circle with "OOPS!" on it) for the teacher to hold up
  • A puppet or picture of Hackle; character cards for Dev and Maya
  • Two "reaction" signs: a calm face labeled "Thank you for telling me" and a crossed-out frowning face (used only in step 2 to show what we do NOT say)
  • Student Activity Book, Lesson 4 page
  • Crayons

4. Hook (2 min)#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Hold the big red Oops Button.

"Friends, who has ever made a mistake? Not on purpose, just a mistake? Maybe you spilled juice. Maybe you knocked over a block tower. (Wait for nods.) Everybody makes mistakes. I do, too. Grown-ups do, Hackle does, and so does everyone in our story.

"Dev was playing a game on the family tablet. His grandmother was in the kitchen. Dev tapped something by mistake, and a picture came up that was yucky. It made his tummy feel funny. He wanted to hide the tablet. He wanted to close his eyes and pretend it never happened. Have you ever wanted to do that? (Pause.) That feeling is normal.

"But Dev remembered the Oops Button. It is not a real button on a screen. It is a button in our heart. When we tap it, we say: 'Oops! I need help.' And do you know what Dev's grandmother said? She said, 'Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. Let's fix it together.'

"Today we practice pressing the Oops Button. Here's the best part: you will never be in trouble for pressing it."

5. Activity (8 min): "Press the Oops Button"#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Group size: whole class on the carpet, then pairs. For 25-30 children you will have 12-15 pairs; one adult circulates and listens.

Steps:

  1. (1 min) Introduce the Oops Button card. Hand each child an Oops Button card. Say: "This is your very own Oops Button. It has a big red button and three words: Oops. Tell. Fixed. (point to each). Oops (I tap it). Tell (I tell a grown-up). Fixed (we fix it together)."
  2. (2 min) Teacher models the good response and the not-good response.
    • Teacher with Hackle puppet: "Oops! I need help!" Teacher (as grown-up) responds calmly: "Thank you for telling me. It's not your fault. Let's fix it together." Children give a thumbs up.
    • Quick contrast, kept light and not scary: teacher shows the crossed-out frowning face sign and says, "A grown-up should not say 'Why did you do that?' or 'You're in trouble.' If a grown-up ever says that, it is their mistake, and you can still tell another helper." Put the sign away.
  3. (3 min) Pair practice, three rounds. Pairs take turns being "the child" and "the grown-up." The child holds up their Oops Button card, says "Oops! I need help," and the other child answers, "Thank you for telling me. Let's fix it together." Teacher gives three pretend situations out loud. After each, partners switch roles:
    • Round 1: "You tapped something that popped up on the screen, and it was confusing."
    • Round 2: "A picture came up that made your tummy feel funny."
    • Round 3: "You accidentally closed a game and the screen went dark."
      (Keep situations simple. Do not elaborate on what the "picture" was.)
  4. (1 min) What the grown-up does next. Show a simple three-picture strip on chart paper: (1) grown-up looks at the screen calmly, (2) they close or turn off the screen together, (3) they give a hug or high-five. Say: "Together we turn it off, we take a breath, and we feel better."
  5. (1 min) Body feelings. Teach: "When something yucky happens, our body might tell us. Tummy feels funny. Face feels hot. Heart beats fast. That's our body saying, 'Pause. Tell someone.' Put your hand on your tummy. Take a deep breath, like smelling a flower. Blow it out, like blowing a candle."

Fun Boost

Fun Boosters (pick ONE inside your 15 minutes; save the rest for an Encore)

  • Energizer (30 seconds): "Oops, Tell, Fixed!" Crouch with hands on cheeks and say "Oops!" Stand with hand on heart and reach out: "Tell!" Then spin or wiggle with arms up: "Fixed!" Do it three times, faster each time, and finish with one calm breath.
  • Game variant: "Say It Like a..." In pairs, the "grown-up" says "Thank you for telling me" in different kind voices: a sleepy sloth, a whispering mouse, a big gentle bear. The "child" gives a thumbs up to each. Children learn that a good reply is calm and kind in any voice.
  • Chant (hands on cheeks, then heart, then cheer): "Oops! Tell! Fixed! / No trouble, no blame: I tell, that's the game!"
  • Silly voice / puppet moment: Hackle pretends to hide under a scarf or your sleeve: "Nobody can see me! It never happened!" Children call, "Hackle! Press your Oops Button!" Hackle peeks out: "Oops! I need help!" You answer warmly, "Thank you for telling me." Hackle's quills settle down with a big "phhhh." (A silly, safe echo of Dev wanting to hide.)
  • Level-Up Challenge: Oops at Home. Think of three small oopsies that are not about screens (spilled milk, a wobbly block tower) and what a helper says back: "Thank you for telling me!" Grade 2: tonight, teach one grown-up the three-word reply and see if they can say it in a silly voice. Spiral: "Who is on my Helper Hand that I would tell?"

4. Hook (2 min)#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Hold up the "flying pig" card.

"Look at this picture. A flying pig! Is that real? (Children: 'No!') You're right. Somebody drew it, or a computer made it. It is a pretend picture. Pretend pictures can be fun, like in a cartoon or a storybook.

5. Activity (8 min): "Real or Pretend Parade"#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Group size: whole class on the carpet. For 25-30 children, hold up cards one at a time, and have everyone vote with their body. Then pairs for the mystery voice.

Steps:

  1. (1 min) Set up. Place the Real and Pretend floor spots. Teach the body vote: Real = hands on heart; Pretend = hands wiggle like a magic spell. Practice once.
  2. (3 min) Card parade. Hold up each of the 10 cards. Children vote with their bodies. Pick a child to explain why. Reveal the answer. Ask: "How do you know?" (Time saver: with 10 cards in 3 minutes, ask "why" only for the flying pig and the two hard cards. The rest are quick body votes.) Look for clues: a flying pig can't be real; a rainbow cat is a cartoon. For hard cards, say: "This one looks real, but it is a pretend picture a computer made. See? Even a good guess can be tricky. That's why we Check with a grown-up."
    • Say clearly at least once: "It's okay not to know. Even grown-ups can't always tell. That's why we check together."
  3. (2 min) The mystery voice. Ask a child (or you) to stand behind the paper bag or a screen (a book works) and speak in a silly robot voice: "Hello, friends! I am your friend Robo Pal. Tell me your name and where you live!" Children respond with Pause Palm and say: "Pause! Check with a grown-up!" Then reveal the voice is the teacher or a classmate. Say: "A computer can make a voice that sounds just like someone you know, like a grown-up in your family. If a voice ever asks for something strange, we don't trust it right away. We Pause, and we Check with a grown-up. That's what the family code word is for. Your family might choose a silly secret word that only your family knows."

  4. (1 min) The "Check" ideas. Write three Check ideas with pictures on the board:
    • Ask a grown-up.
    • Look for clues (does it look silly or too perfect? does it ask for something strange?)
    • Use the family code word.

5. Activity (8 min): "Say No, Go, Tell"#

    • TELL: Hand on heart, reach out. Say: "I'm going to tell."
  2. (2 min) Whole-class rehearsal. Say the three moves in order three times with the chant: "No! Go! Tell!" Let children do it slowly and loudly at the same volume as your voice.   3. (2 min) Scenario cards (teacher reads, class responds). Hold up 3 cards. For each, read the card aloud calmly and ask: "What does the child do?" Children answer: "No, Go, Tell!"

Hackles line: "If my body feels uh-oh about something, my hackles are telling me. I trust them, I say No, I Go, I Tell, and it is never my fault."

8. Exit ticket#

Illustration note A child with a bubble around them; below, three small pictures: a stop-sign hand, two small footsteps, and a heart with an outstretched hand. "Trace the three pictures in order: No. Go. Tell." Children trace a line from one picture to the next. Then each child circles one helper from their Helper Hand to say who they would tell. Collect. Do not ask children to write personal information.

9. If a student discloses#

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (this lesson is the most likely to prompt a disclosure)
Say, calmly, in a low steady voice:

  • "Thank you for telling me."
  • "I believe you."
  • "It is not your fault."
  • "I'm so glad you told me."
  • "I'm going to get help from someone whose job is to keep kids safe."

Don't say:

  • "I promise I won't tell." (You can't promise that.)
  • "Are you sure?" "Why didn't you tell me sooner?" "Did he/she/they touch you?" (leading or blaming)
  • Don't press for details. Don't ask the child to repeat the story to others. Don't ask the child to write or draw it. Don't show shock, anger, or disgust.
  • Do not investigate. Do not call or confront the person, the family, or the platform.

Do:

  • Keep the child with you or with a trusted adult; keep it private and short.
  • Write down the child's exact words, the date and time, and what led to the disclosure, as soon as you can. Facts only.

Escalate (the same day, or immediately):

  • Tell your school's designated person (counselor, administrator, or designated safeguarding lead) immediately and make the report required by your state, usually to child protective services or law enforcement. Do not leave it to someone else.
  • If the child is in immediate danger, call 911.

Mandatory-reporting reminder: Every US state has a mandatory reporting law, and most name school staff as reporters (about 17 states make any person a reporter). Timelines and procedures differ by state, so ask your administrator for yours. You do not need proof or certainty, only to suspect or have reason to believe a child has been harmed [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: childwelfare.gov mandatory reporting statutes summary, current through May 2023]. [VERIFY your state's statute, timeline, and your district's procedure.]
If an online request is disclosed: A child who says someone online asked for pictures should be handled the same way. It may also be reportable to the NCMEC CyberTipline. Your designated person decides. Do not view, copy, forward, or save any images. [VERIFY your district's procedure.]
Support yourself: Hearing a disclosure is hard. Talk to your counselor or administrator afterward.

10. Differentiate#

  • Support: Keep the lesson to the Body Rules and "No, Go, Tell." Skip the party card. Use pictures only. Allow children to say "No, Go, Tell" with a buddy. Provide the "I need help" card from the Reproducibles for children who find speaking hard.
  • Extension: Second graders add a sentence: "If a grown-up is busy, I tell another." Ask: "Who would be a second helper?" Have them trace the helper hand again and add a name.
  • Low or no internet at home: The Body Rules apply everywhere: at home, at school, in person, online. The online example (Card A) is one case; the lesson does not require a device.
  • Language access: Use pictures and the body signals. Teach "No, Go, Tell" in home languages. [VERIFY translations.] Provide the family card in translation and, where possible, a family conversation phone line or a bilingual counselor. Be mindful that terms for body parts and cultural comfort with the topic vary. Use the district-approved language and let the counselor assist.
  • Children with disabilities: Children with disabilities are at higher risk of abuse and may rely on intimate care from many adults. [VERIFY with a child-protection source.] Reinforce that it is always okay to say "No" or "stop" about anything that feels wrong, and to tell. For nonspeaking students, ensure they have a way to tell (picture cards, AAC, a trusted communication partner). For students who use physical care, teach "My body is mine. Care helpers help me stay healthy. Nobody asks me to keep a secret."
  • Children who have experienced abuse: This lesson may be upsetting. Offer a calm corner, a drawing activity, or a break. Do not force participation. Notify the counselor beforehand if you know a child has a relevant history.
  • Opt-out students: Provide an alternate calm activity, and still ensure they know their helper hand (Lesson 1).
  • Cultural and family values: Respect all families, but the Body Rules and the "tell" message are not negotiable.

One-liner: "Today we taught the Body Rules: my body is mine, nobody needs a picture of it, and if anyone ever asks or makes me uncomfortable I can say No, go, and tell a trusted adult. Please tell your child: 'You can tell me anything, and you will never be in trouble.'"

Reply slip (return to teacher):

One grown-up our child said they would tell: ______

Include on the back of the family card: one or two child-safety helplines. For example: Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: childhelphotline.org]; NCMEC Take It Down (takeitdown.ncmec.org) and CyberTipline report.cybertip.org [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: report.cybertip.org]; local resources. Check numbers before printing.

12. Teacher notes#

  • Consistency with best practice: This lesson follows widely used child-abuse-prevention concepts: body ownership, private parts, "no" and "go," no secrets about bodies, "tell a trusted adult until someone helps," and "it is never your fault." [VERIFY against your state-approved or counselor-approved curriculum; examples of such frameworks include Darkness to Light "Stewards of Children," NSPCC "PANTS," and Erin's Law curricula.] This guide is not a substitute for your district's required curriculum or counselor input.
  • Who harms children? Most children who are sexually abused are harmed by someone they know (a widely reported finding in child-abuse research; the primary source was not checked in Round 3B, so do not quote a percentage). That is why we teach the behavior (asking for pictures, secrets, touching private parts) rather than "stay away from strangers."
  • Online link: In current threat data, online enticement and sextortion reports include children of all ages. [NCMEC 2025 data: VERIFIED 2026-10-05; see evidence_and_threats.md.] For this age, the lesson is simple and calm: no one needs pictures of your body; tell until someone helps.
  • Do not use fear. Keep your voice calm, your pace steady, and finish the lesson with the empowering message and a comforting activity (a song, a stretch).
  • Do not teach "how to avoid being abused." The responsibility belongs to adults. Our message: it is never the child's fault.
  • Hugs and touch: Teaching children they can say no to hugs, even from family, supports body ownership. Offer high fives, waves, and fist bumps as alternatives.
  • Common kid questions:
    • "What if someone I love asks?" "Anyone, even someone you love or know, can be told 'No.' You tell a trusted adult."
    • "What if I'm scared to tell?" "That's a normal feeling. You can tell any helper. You will not be in trouble."
    • "What if they say they'll hurt me or my family?" "Thank you for telling me. That is something a grown-up must know. We'll keep you safe." (Escalate immediately.)
    • "What if I already did something?" "It is not your fault. Thank you for telling me. Let's get help." (Escalate.)

Lesson 10: Screen Balance#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

1. Header#

1. Header, table 10
Title Screen Balance
Grade band K-2 (ages 5-7)
Strand E: Kind & Well (bullying, upstanding, wellbeing, AI companions)
Big idea Screens can be fun, and so can many other things. I notice how I feel, and I can choose a break. If screens make me sad, mad, worried, or tired, I tell a grown-up.
Practiced skill Name a feeling; choose a break activity; say "I need a break"
Time Hook 2 min, Activity 8 min, Discuss 3 min, Takeaway + Exit ticket 2 min = 15 min

2. Standards (primary only, approximate)#

  • ISTE: 2 Digital Citizen (approx. 2.c, balance and well-being) [2016 numbering shown; current ISTE v4.02 equivalent: 1.2.c; approximate. VERIFIED 2026-10-05: ISTE Standards v4.02 PDF]
  • CSTA: 1A-IC-16 / Impacts of Computing (approximate) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017, rev.) text read; mapping approximate]
  • CASEL: Self-Awareness and Self-Management [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: competency names match casel.org framework]

3. Materials#

Unplugged (default):

  • Feelings face cards, 6 (happy, calm, excited, tired, worried, mad/sad) (Reproducibles, Section 11)
  • Break Menu cards, 8 (play outside, draw, read a book, sing, help cook, stretch, play with a pet or toy, talk to a grown-up) (Reproducibles, Section 11)
  • A paper plate per child ("My Balance Plate") divided into 4 wedges; crayons. (No time to pre-draw 30 plates? Use the printed Balance Plate on page 2 of the Lesson 10 Activity Book pages instead. It is the same activity and zero prep.)
  • Student Activity Book, Lesson 10 page, plus the Hackles Certificate (last page)
  • A chime or soft bell
  • Character cards: Lily, Dev, and Hackle

Optional tech version: none.

4. Hook (2 min)#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Hold up the Lily card and a feelings face card.

"Lily loves her tablet. She loves videos and music and games. But sometimes, after a long time on the tablet, her tummy felt grumpy. Her eyes felt tired. She felt cranky, and she did not even know why. Have you ever felt grumpy like that? (Pause, accept nods.) That's normal. It means our body wants something different.

"Hackle says: screens can be fun, and so can other things. Playing outside, drawing, singing, hugging a pet, eating a snack with family: these are all things that make us feel good. Hackle needs sun, water, food, sleep, and friends. So do we. Today we will practice noticing how we feel and choosing a break.

"And here's a secret that's not a secret: if a screen ever makes you feel sad, mad, worried, or scared, you can tell a grown-up. It is not your fault, and you will not be in trouble."

5. Activity (8 min): "My Balance Plate"#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

Group size: whole class on the carpet for the opening, then at tables of 4 to 6 for the plate. For 25-30 children, 5 to 7 tables.

Steps:

  1. (2 min) Feelings check-in. Hold up each feelings face. Children copy the face and say how they might feel after a long time playing a game: "Excited? Tired? Grumpy?" Teach: "Our feelings are clues. If we feel tired or grumpy, our body might be asking for a break."
  2. (2 min) The Break Menu. Show the 8 Break Menu cards. Say each one, and have children act it out quickly (stretch, sing, draw in the air, pet a pretend puppy). Say: "A break menu is a list of things we can choose. We pick a break." Teach the phrase: "I need a break."
  3. (3 min) Make a Balance Plate. Each child gets a paper plate divided into four wedges (teacher pre-draws the lines). In each wedge, they draw something they enjoy: one for moving/playing, one for people (family or friends), one for quiet things (reading, drawing, sleep), and one for screens (a favorite video or game). Say: "Our plate has lots of good things. Screens are one wedge, not the whole plate." Circulate and praise.
  4. (1 min) "Chime break." Ring the chime. Everyone puts down crayons, takes three slow breaths (smell the flower, blow the candle), and stretches. Say: "This is a break. See how it helps!"

Fun Boost

Fun Boosters (pick ONE inside your 15 minutes; save the rest for an Encore)

  • Energizer (30 seconds): "Plate Dance." Call a wedge and everyone does it for 10 seconds: Move (run in place), People (wave to a neighbor), Quiet (stretch like a tree and breathe), Screen (freeze like a robot and blink slowly). End with a wobbly one-foot "balance!"
  • Game variant: "Champ Review Relay." (A spiral review of the whole program.) Say a lesson clue and children do its move: Helper Hand wave (1), turn a key (2), Pause Palm at a pop-up (3), Oops-Tell-Fixed (4), arms wide or hug yourself (5), Surprise jump or Tell wave (6), kind heart (7), real or pretend hands (8), and the calm "No, Go, Tell" hand moves with no scenarios (9). Finish with the whole chant: Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell.
  • Chant (clap-stomp): "Screen is one slice, not the whole plate! / Move, play, quiet, friends: that's great! / Grumpy, tired, worried, or sad? / Take a break, or tell. I'm glad!"
  • Silly voice / puppet moment: Sleepy Hackle. Hackle "falls asleep" on the tablet with a giant snore. Children wake Hackle with breaks from the Break Menu (stretch, sing, draw in the air) until the quills perk up and Hackle says, "I needed a break! Thank you!"
  • Level-Up Challenge: Family Break. Pick one Break Menu idea to do with a grown-up this week. Grade 2: be a Hackles Champ teacher and teach the chant (Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell.) to someone at home. Spiral: "Who is on my Tell-Someone Team?"

6. Discuss (3 min)#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min
  1. "How can your body tell you it's time for a break?" (tired eyes, grumpy, wiggly)
  2. "What is one thing on your Break Menu you like?" (accept all)
  3. "Is a screen always bad?" (No! Screens can be fun and helpful. We need a balance.)
  4. "What if a screen makes you feel sad, mad, or worried?" (Tell a grown-up. It's not my fault.)
  5. "What if it's hard to stop?" (It's okay to feel grumpy. Say 'I need help to stop,' and pick a break.)

7. Takeaway (2 min)#

Hook 2 minActivity 8 minDiscuss 3 minTakeaway 2 min

"Today's lesson: Screens are one part of my day. I notice how I feel and choose a break. If something online makes me worried, I tell a grown-up." Do the chant. Then: "Pause and notice my feelings. Check: do I need a break? Tell a grown-up if I feel worried."

Hackles line: "If something online makes my hackles go up, I notice, Pause, and tell. Trust your hackles!"

Hackles Certificate: Close the lesson, and the program, with the certificate ceremony (see Teacher notes). Say: "You have learned so much. You are officially a Hackles Champ!" Applaud. Hand out the certificates (Activity Book last page; children can write their own names while you sign, or pre-fill the names if you have time).

8. Exit ticket#

Illustration note A row of six small feelings faces and one small picture of a child holding a tablet. "Circle the face that shows how you feel when you've been on a screen too long. Then draw a break you would pick." Collect to see which breaks children chose.

9. If a student discloses#

If a student discloses

If a student discloses
Common in this lesson: "I stay up all night on the tablet," "Nobody's home at night," "My grown-up gets mad when I stop," "I get scared at bedtime," "A video scared me," or "I play with someone online late and they say not to tell."
Say: "Thank you for telling me. I believe you. It is not your fault. I am glad you told me. I am going to get help from someone whose job is to keep kids safe."
Don't say: "Your parents shouldn't do that." Don't judge the family. Don't promise secrecy. Don't ask leading questions.
Do: Listen. Write the child's words and the time.
Escalate: Tell your designated person the same day. If a child describes being left alone for long periods, hunger, fear at home, someone online asking for secrets or pictures, or any harm, treat it as a possible neglect or abuse report. If it's an ordinary screen-time struggle, send the family card kindly and mention it to the counselor.
Mandatory-reporting reminder: In nearly every US state, teachers must report suspected abuse, neglect, or exploitation promptly; your state sets the exact deadline, so do not wait. You do not need proof. [Confirm your state and district rules with district counsel.]

10. Differentiate#

  • Support: Use only three feelings faces (happy, tired, worried) and four Break Menu cards. Let children place stickers on the plate wedges. Offer help to draw.
  • Extension: Second graders write or draw "My Break Plan" with three breaks in order. Ask: "How would you tell a grown-up that you need help to stop?" Have them practice a sentence.
  • Low or no internet at home: Many children have little or no screen time at home; some have lots. Do not judge. Say: "Everyone's day is different. A balanced day has lots of different things." The lesson is unplugged; the plate wedges can show any activity.
  • Language access: Use faces and pictures. Teach "I need a break" and feelings words in home languages. Invite families to share a favorite offline activity from home.
  • Children with sensory or attention needs: The movement breaks double as regulation tools. Let children choose the break. Offer a quiet corner.
  • Children who have little free time or care for siblings: Break menus should include small breaks (stretch, breathe) rather than only "go play outside."
  • Cultural differences: Family and community activities (cooking, chores, prayer, caring for elders) are valuable parts of balance; invite children to draw those.

11. Family card#

One-liner: "Today we made a Balance Plate: screens are one part of a day, and we notice our feelings and choose breaks. Please ask your child: 'How does your body feel after a screen? What break would you choose?' And tell them: if anything online ever worries them, they can tell you."

Reply slip (return to teacher):

[ ] We made a break plan together. [ ] We want ideas for screen-free activities.
Our child's favorite break: ______

12. Teacher notes#

  • Be careful about screen-time rules. Guidance from health organizations (for example, American Academy of Pediatrics and WHO) exists for young children but changes and varies by age. The AAP now emphasizes a family media plan (screen-free zones, one screen at a time, quality content) rather than one number [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: healthychildren.org]. Check the current WHO and AAP pages before citing specific limits. This lesson avoids numbers and teaches self-awareness.
  • Do not shame families. Many families have good reasons for screen use (work, caregiving, entertainment). Teach balance, not guilt.
  • Persuasive design: For K-2, we do not use that term. Older bands teach "built to keep you scrolling." For this age, the lesson is noticing feelings and choosing breaks.
  • AI companions and chatbots: This age group should not be using chatbots or AI companions independently. [Not verified in Round 3B; check current guidance, for example from Common Sense Media, before citing.] If children mention talking to a computer friend, say: "A computer voice can be fun, but it's not a person. Tell a grown-up if it ever says something that feels strange."
  • Hackles Certificate ceremony: A short ceremony makes the program memorable. Call each child's name, hand the certificate with a smile, and say: "Thank you for being a Hackles Champ. You know how to Pause, Check, and Tell." Invite families (optional) or hold it at a school event. Remind children of the Tell-Someone Team one last time. Revisit the Helper Hand and Oops Button.
  • After the program: If you use the Assessment Pack, give the K-2 post-check now. Review reply slips for follow-up with families.
  • Common kid questions:
    • "Why can't I play all day?" "Your body and brain need lots of different things. Screens are one part."
    • "What if I'm mad when it's time to stop?" "That's okay. Mad is a normal feeling. Pick a break from your menu."
    • "What if there's nothing to do?" "Look at your Break Menu! We can think of more together."
    • "What if my grown-up is on their phone all the time?" "Grown-ups need breaks too! You can ask them to play with you. And you can always tell a helper at school."
