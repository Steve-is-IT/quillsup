About Hackles#

Hackles is a free kit made by a small group of educators and security practitioners. It is free because every young person should be able to learn to spot trouble and ask for help, whatever their school's budget. There are no accounts and no student data, and the whole kit is at quillsup.org. Your hackles are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off. The kit teaches students to notice that feeling and act on it. Hackles up? Pause. Check. Tell.

Feedback, corrections and stories from your classroom are welcome: [CONTACT: to be added].

Pathway note (for adults): Hackles is the first step on a path that continues with free resources such as picoCTF and CYBER.ORG.

No student accounts, no student data collected, ever.

Teacher Guide, Ages 11-13#

Ten core 15-minute lessons, plus three optional Plus lessons (P1 to P3) for states such as Florida that ask for more. No student accounts, no student data collected, ever. No devices required. Scripts included.

Part 1. Start Here#

What this unit is#

"Case Files" is a ten-lesson unit for students aged 11-13. Each lesson takes 15 minutes, runs on paper and pencil, and comes with word-for-word scripts. Students play analysts. They read a short, fictional case file about a made-up young person, spot the pattern, decide what the character should do next, and then practice the one skill that matters most: getting help.

What this unit is not#

  • It is not a scare program. No frightening statistics as a hook, no graphic detail, no "this could happen to you." Students this age respond to respect, clear steps and practice, and they can smell a scare tactic from the back row.
  • It is not an attack-methods class. Students learn to recognize and respond. They never learn to build a phishing page, run a scam, break into an account, groom a person, extort a person, or make a fake image.
  • It is not victim-blaming. Every lesson repeats one sentence in the teacher's own words: it is never your fault. Responsibility belongs to the person who pressures, tricks, threatens, or harms.

The research behind the design#

These design choices follow the published prevention literature on youth internet safety education (Finkelhor, Walsh, Jones, Mitchell and Collier, 2021, "Youth Internet Safety Education: Aligning Programs With the Evidence Base," Trauma, Violence, & Abuse 22(5): 1233-1247 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: Crossref record]; the review found mismatches between program messages and the research and recommended building on established evidence-based prevention programs; the bullets below are the kit's design choices, not each a finding of that paper):

  • Practice (role-play, rehearsal, discussion) beats lecture.
  • A structured sequence, repeated, beats a one-off assembly.
  • Scare-based approaches and "information only" lessons are weak or unproven. Direct evidence on fear appeals in online safety is thin, so this guide steers clear of them without claiming they are proven to backfire [VERIFY].
  • Help-seeking is the outcome that matters most. International survey work found that many young people who were harmed told no one, and very few contacted a helpline or police (UNICEF Disrupting Harm, first 13 countries: about one-third told no one and about 3 percent reported to police or a helpline [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: unicef.org/innocenti]).

Honest limit: nobody has run a rigorous trial of a curriculum on modern sextortion or deepfake abuse. The Assessment Pack in this kit lets your school collect its own pre/post evidence.

Part 2. Hackles up? Pause · Check · Tell (grades 6-8 version)#

HACKLES UP? Noticing comes before the three steps. Your hackles are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off: too good, pushy, secret, or scary. Teach students to trust that gut feeling. A false alarm is fine and welcome, so nobody should feel silly for stopping over nothing, and if their hackles went up and something happened anyway, or they didn't notice in time, or they noticed and still got fooled, it is never their fault. Optional hand signal: raise a hand and brush it up the back of the neck, then go on with the Pause, Check, and Tell moves. In this band the mascot Hackle appears only as a small corner icon on posters and cards, never in dialogue.

This routine (notice, then three steps) opens and closes every lesson. By grade 8 students should be able to say it, explain it, and use it. That's the test.

PAUSE. Stop before you reply, click, pay, forward, or share. Take one slow breath. Most online tricks and threats run on pressure, so slowing down is a real defense. Pausing is also the first step of the Response Drill (STOP) in Lesson 7. Same move.

CHECK. Ask four quick questions:

  • Who is this, really, and how do I know?
  • What do they want me to do, and how fast?
  • Is anyone asking me to keep it secret, send something private, or pay?
  • How do I feel? (Rushed, flattered, scared, embarrassed? Feelings are information.)

TELL. Tell a person you trust. Telling is not tattling and it is not failing. It is the strongest move in every case file. Use the Help Ladder:

The three teacher promises (say these early and often)#

  1. "It is never your fault." Not when someone tricks you. Not when someone pressures you. Not when you made a choice you regret.
  2. "You are not in trouble for telling." Telling is how problems get fixed.
  3. "I can't promise to keep everything secret, but I promise I will help, and I will not blame you." Never promise secrecy. Students need to know that school adults sometimes have to bring in other helpers.

Part 3. How to Run a "Case File" Scenario#

The Case File method (the same every time)#

READ. You read the case file aloud (about one minute). Students follow along or listen.

MARK. Students mark clues on their worksheet. Where did the pattern show up? Which feelings did the character have? Which clue was the earliest warning?

DECIDE. Groups choose what the character should do next, using Pause · Check · Tell.

LADDER. Groups name who the character tells and say the first sentence out loud. This is the skill that gets practiced every single lesson.

The Analyst Rules (post these)#

Read these aloud before your first case file, and again before Lessons 6, 7, and 8.

  1. We analyze characters, not each other. Every case file is fictional. We talk about "the character," never about a real student, a real school, or a real post.
  2. We judge actions, never blame. If a character makes a mistake, we ask "what is the next good move?" and never "what is wrong with them?"
  3. No personal stories in the room. If you want to talk about something that really happened, talk to me, the counselor, or another adult you trust, privately. I will make time for that. This rule exists to protect you, not to silence you.
  4. Pass is always okay. You can put your green Pass card on your desk at any time. You do not need to say why. I will never ask.
  5. No names, no apps-as-gossip, no screenshots. We do not name real people. We do not share or pass around real messages or images, ever.
  6. No jokes at a character's expense. Humor is fine. Mocking a character who made a mistake is not.

Keep it real, not corny (tips for 11 to 13 year olds)#

  • Use your own words. The scripts are a safety net, and nobody is performing. If a line sounds stiff in your mouth, say it your way and keep the meaning, especially "it is never your fault" and "you are not in trouble for telling."
  • Vary how you say "it is never your fault" from lesson to lesson (a quick aside, a poster, a question to the class) so it lands as true and doesn't turn into a catchphrase.
  • The hand signal for Hackles up is optional. If your class finds it cringe, let them pick their own gesture or drop it. The words matter. The move doesn't. Nobody gets graded on a gesture.
  • Short beats long. When a hook runs long, cut to the question and let the students talk, because a room of twelve-year-olds will forgive almost anything except being lectured at.
  • Real beats staged. Ask for reasons ("Why red?"), not answers meant to please you. Let a good disagreement run for 30 seconds.
  • Humor is fine when it's warm and aimed at the situation, never at a character or a classmate. Skip jokes entirely in Lessons 6, 7, and 8.
  • Each lesson has an optional "Make It Stick" box: a hook variation, a game or debate, a peer-teaching or choice option, and a Level-Up Challenge. Pick one or two. In Lessons 6, 7, and 8 the boxes are deliberately calm, with no scoring, timers or competition.

Part 4. Before You Teach#

4.1 Disclosures are expected#

Teaching help-seeking works. So some students will tell you things. Treat every disclosure as a success of the program and as a serious responsibility, and decide on your own next step now, while nobody is sitting across from you waiting for an answer.

  • Review your school's mandated-reporter protocol and your state's reporting rules before Lesson 1. All 50 states and DC have child abuse reporting laws; many name school personnel explicitly, and in many states the duty is personal, not only to the principal [All 50 states and DC have reporting laws (childwelfare.gov, current through May 2023); procedures differ, so confirm yours with district counsel].
  • Learn the name and extension of your school counselor, your administrator, and your designated child-protection or Title IX contact.
  • Know your district's process for suicide or self-harm concerns. Look it up now, not that day.
  • Keep the Appendix C one-pager within reach during every lesson.

4.2 Lessons that need extra care: 6, 7, and 8#

Lesson 6 (Grooming Patterns), Lesson 7 (Pressure Online: Response Drill), and Lesson 8 (Fakes That Hurt) ask students to think about abuse and exploitation. They contain no explicit content, no real images, no graphic descriptions and no step-by-step offender tactics. Even so, they need extra care. Here is what that means:

  • Family pre-notice at least 5 school days before Lessons 7 and 8 (letters in Appendix A), with an easy opt-out.
  • A counselor heads-up before the lesson, and the counselor available on the day (checklists in Appendix B).
  • An opt-out and alternate-activity plan (inside each lesson).
  • Scheduling care: teach these mid-week and early or mid-day. Never right before a long break, a test, or a major school event, and never Lessons 6, 7, and 8 on consecutive days.
  • Teacher read-through of the full lesson, disclosure box included, the day before.

Plus lessons P1 and P2. Plus Lesson P1 (trafficking and exploitation) is treated like Lessons 7 and 8: specialist and counselor review, family pre-notice at least 5 school days ahead with an opt-out, a counselor heads-up, an Alternate Activity, and never on the same day as Lessons 6, 7 or 8. Plus Lesson P2 (mental health and compulsive use) needs a counselor or clinician review, a counselor heads-up and a family note; a formal opt-out is not required but is honored. P3 is not a sensitive lesson. See Part 7B and Appendices A and B.

4.3 What we never ask students to do#

  • Never ask for personal stories, examples from their own phones, or "raise your hand if this happened to you."
  • Never ask a student to show an image, message, or account.
  • Never display, pass around, or project any real or realistic image of a minor.
  • Never ask students to "try" a scam, a phishing attempt, or an extortion script on a classmate.

4.4 If a parent asks to opt a student out#

Honor the request. No questions. Use the Alternate Activity in the lesson, and don't single the student out. Offer the family a short, neutral summary of what was taught and the Family Card, so they can talk about it at home.

Hook (2 min)#

SAY (noticing step): "Hackles up? That is the feeling that something is off. Trust it. Telling someone about a feeling is always fine, even if it turns out to be nothing."

SAY: "Welcome to Case Files. For the next ten weeks you will be analysts. You will read short files about made-up people, find the pattern, and work out the best next move. Every character in these files is fictional. Nobody here has to share anything personal, ever."

SAY: "Before we start, here is the most important idea in the whole unit. Think silently, and do not raise your hand. Think of a time you wanted to tell someone about something, a problem, a worry, a mistake, and you held back. You do not have to share it. Most people have done this. And here is what is interesting: people hold back, not because the problem is small, but because the telling feels hard."

SAY: "So today we do not just talk about telling. We practice it, the way you would practice a free throw or a song. Because a skill you have practiced is a skill you can use when you are nervous."

Post or project the Help Ladder poster and read the four rungs aloud: Yourself, a friend who can come with you, a trusted adult, a reporting channel.

Activity (8 min)#

Grouping: whole class, then groups of 4-5, then triads.

Step 1 (1 min). Read the case file. Read CF-1 aloud:

CF-1: Jordan's Weird Message
Jordan is twelve. While playing an online game, someone Jordan has never met sends a private message: "You're really good. Want to chat somewhere more private? Also, don't tell anyone, okay?" Jordan feels uneasy. Jordan thinks about telling a parent but worries: "They'll take my tablet away. Or they'll think I was careless." Jordan closes the chat and stares at the screen.

Step 2 (2 min). Barrier Busters (groups of 4-5). Give each group the Barrier Cards. Each card states a reason someone might not tell (for example, "I will lose my device," "I will look dumb," "It is probably nothing," "I do not want to get someone in trouble," "Adults will freak out," "I already did something I am embarrassed about"). Each group picks the two cards that fit Jordan best and writes one true sentence that makes telling easier. Example: "Adults who care about you mostly want to fix the problem, not punish you."

SAY: "Notice that every barrier is a feeling, not a fact. Feelings are real, but they are not always right about how things will go."

Step 3 (4 min). Rehearse the Tell-3 (triads). Form triads. Roles: Teller (plays Jordan), Listener (plays the adult), Observer (holds the Tell-3 card and watches). Give each triad the Tell-3 Script Card:

  • Part 1, Start: "I need your help with something online."
  • Part 2, Say it in one sentence: "Someone I do not know asked me to chat privately and said not to tell anyone."
  • Part 3, Ask: "Can you help me figure out what to do?"

Activity (8 min)#

Grouping: whole class, then groups of 4-5.

Step 1 (1 min). Read the case file.

CF-2: Remy's Locked-Out Game
Remy is thirteen. One morning, Remy cannot log in to a favorite game. The password does not work. A friend texts: "Why did your account just ask me for a gift card?" Remy realizes someone else is using the account. Remy uses the same password, "Tiger123," for the game, for email, and for an old forum Remy joined two years ago and forgot about. Later Remy learns the old forum had a data leak, and lists of leaked passwords were being tried on other sites.

Step 2 (4 min). The Key Ring Game (groups of 4-5). Each group lays out the six Door Cards: Game, Email, School login, Music, Shop, Old Forum.

  • Round 1: One key for everything. Place the Master Key on all six doors. Draw the Leak Card ("The Old Forum has a data leak. Its keys are stolen."). Remove the Master Key from the table. Count how many doors are now open. (Answer: six.) Record on the Tally Sheet.
  • Round 2: A different key for every door. Place a different Unique Key on each door. Draw the Leak Card again. Remove only the Old Forum's key. Count open doors. (Answer: one, and it is the one nobody cared about.) Record.
  • Round 3: Add a second lock. Return to Round 1 (one key for all), but place a Second Lock Card on Email and on Game. Draw the Leak Card. How many doors can the thief open without the second lock? (Answer: four, and the two most important doors stay shut.) Record.

SAY: "The Second Lock is 2-step verification: after the password, the account asks for something you have, like a code from an app or a message sent to your own device, or something you are. A stolen password alone is no longer enough."

Step 3 (2 min). Email is the master key. Groups discuss: which door matters most? Draw out that email is the account that can reset many others, so it needs the strongest lock and the second lock first.

SAY: "There is one rule about codes. A code is a key. Nobody who is really helping you will ever ask you to read them a code. Not support, not a friend, not a game moderator. If someone asks for your code, that is a sign something is wrong."

Step 4 (1 min). The 'Oops' plan. Read the five steps aloud while students record them on the Workbook page. "If you think an account was taken:"

  • Tell a trusted adult. You are not in trouble.
  • Use the app's official recovery steps (from the official app or website, not a link in a message).
  • Change the password. Use a long passphrase that is new to that account.
  • Turn on 2-step verification.
  • Warn friends: "My account was hacked; ignore messages from it."

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Why did Round 2 leave only one door open?
  2. If you could add a second lock to only two accounts, which two, and why?
  3. Why is a long passphrase of several unrelated words better than a short clever password?
  4. Why do you think someone would pretend to be Remy's account and ask friends for gift cards? What should Remy's friend do? (Pause, do not pay, check with Remy another way, tell an adult.)

Takeaway (2 min)#

SAY: "Pause before you reuse. Check: is this the only account with this key? Tell: if an account is taken, tell an adult and fix it with the official steps. One key for everything means one leak opens everything. And it is never your fault if an account gets taken."

Hook (2 min)#

SAY (noticing step): "Hackles up? Prizes, deadlines, and requests for passwords are classic hackle-raisers. Trusting your gut and checking is smart, and a false alarm is fine."

SAY: "Here is a message. I will read it aloud. 'Your account will be closed in 30 minutes unless you verify now. Tap the link.' [Pause.] Silently ask yourself: what does this message want me to do, and how fast?"

Activity (8 min)#

Grouping: whole class for FLAGS; groups of 4-5 for card analysis.

Step 1 (1 min). Introduce FLAGS. Hand out bookmarks.

  • F: Fast. Does it rush you, with a countdown or a threat?
  • L: Login ask. Does it ask for a password, code, or account info?
  • A: Amazing offer. Is the prize or deal too good to be true?
  • G: Gotta keep it secret. Does it say not to tell anyone?
  • S: Strange sender or link. Does the sender or the link look off or does not match who it claims to be?

SAY: "One flag is a warning. Two or more is a good reason to stop. But a message can be a trick with no flags that you can see, so we also use our feelings: if it makes you rush, worry, or get excited, that is a cue to pause."

Step 2 (5 min). Lab time (groups of 4-5). Each group works through the ten DM cards in its packet. For each card they:

  • Circle the flags with the colored pencil (F, L, A, G, S).
  • Sort the card into one pile: Fake, Real, or Not sure, ask an adult.
  • Write the first move for each fake: do not reply, do not click, report, delete, tell.

Remind groups: some messages are real (for example, a friend's actual message, a school notice with no ask for a password). The goal is not to distrust everything, it is to check.

Step 3 (2 min). Debrief. Reveal answers using the Teacher Answer Key. Ask each group to share one fake that surprised them and one real message that looked suspicious.

SAY: "If you are not sure, that is a correct answer. 'Not sure' means ask an adult, and go to the real site or app yourself by typing the address or opening the app. Never use the link in the message."

Optional (if time): a 30-second "Oops path." SAY: "If you already clicked or typed something, here is what to do: tell an adult, change the password for that account, turn on 2-step verification, and watch for odd messages. You are not in trouble. Quick action is the goal."

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Which flag showed up most often? Which was the hardest to notice?
  2. Why do fake messages often pretend to be from a friend, a game, or a school?
  3. What is the safest way to check whether an account message is real? (Open the app or site yourself.)
  4. A friend forwards you a suspicious link that came from their account. What do you do? (Do not click, tell your friend their account might be taken, tell an adult.)
  5. How did it feel to say "Not sure"? Why is that a strong choice?

Takeaway (2 min)#

SAY: "Pause before you click. Check with FLAGS: Fast, Login ask, Amazing offer, Gotta keep it secret, Strange sender. Tell an adult when you are not sure. And if you did click, it is never your fault. Tell someone and fix it."

Activity (8 min)#

Grouping: whole class, then triads.

Step 1 (1 min). Read the case file.

CF-7: Anika and the Too-Good Friend
Anika is thirteen. In a game chat, she meets someone who says they are her age and who likes the same things. They chat for a few days. The person suggests moving to a private messaging app. Then the person asks Anika to send a private picture. Anika sends something she regrets (we will not describe it, and we do not need to). Immediately the person's tone changes: "Send gift cards or I will show everyone you know. You have one hour." Anika feels sick, ashamed, and alone. She thinks: "I cannot tell anyone. This is my fault."

SAY: "Stop right there. Anika's last thought is the one the person is counting on. The shame is part of the pressure. The person threatening her is the one breaking the law. Anika did not cause this. Now let's learn what Anika can do."

Step 2 (3 min). Learn the Drill (whole class). Point to each card as you teach. Students repeat each word aloud and use a quick hand cue: STOP (palm out), SAVE (pinch fingers), TELL (point to chest), REPORT (tap the desk).

  • STOP. Stop replying. Do not pay. Do not send anything else. The one-hour deadline is part of the pressure. Take one slow breath. Do not delete the chat or the account yet.
  • SAVE. Save the evidence of the threat: the username, the profile name, and the messages that threaten or ask for money, as words. Do not forward any picture to anyone. If saving feels like too much, skip it. Telling comes first, and an adult can help you save what is needed.
  • TELL. Tell a trusted adult right away. Use the Help Ladder. If the first adult does not help, use the Next-Rung Rule.
  • REPORT. With an adult's help: report the account in the app, report to the NCMEC CyberTipline, and use NCMEC's Take It Down tool if a picture needs to be removed. Adults can also contact law enforcement [VERIFY all contacts].

SAY: "Why do we say do not pay? Because paying usually does not make it stop. People who do this often come back for more. NCMEC says cooperating or paying rarely stops the blackmail [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: missingkids.org/theissues/sextortion]. The deadline and the threat are pressure tools. A calm 'stop and tell' beats any deadline."

Step 3 (3 min). Rehearse the Telling (triads). Roles: Teller (plays Anika), Listener (plays the adult), Observer. The Teller reads from the Tell Card:

"I need help. Someone online is threatening me, and I am scared. I have not paid and I will not. I have saved the messages. Can you help me report it?"

The Listener responds from the Listener Card:

"Thank you for telling me. You are not in trouble. This is not your fault. I am going to help you. Let's stop talking to them, save what we need, and report it together."

The Observer uses the Observer checklist: Did the Teller say "I need help"? Did the Listener say "not your fault"? Was "do not pay" mentioned? Was a next step named? Rotate roles after 60 seconds; do two rotations. Any student may choose the Observer role the entire time.

Step 4 (1 min). The two things the person is counting on.

SAY: "The person who does this is counting on two things: that you will feel too ashamed to tell, and that you will panic and pay. You have just practiced the two moves that beat both: tell, and do not pay. And here is something important about shame. Choosing not to send private pictures is a fine choice. But if someone did send one, or if a fake was made, they are still not at fault. The person who threatens is the one doing wrong. Always."

Discuss (3 min)#

All questions are about the character or about general ideas. Do not invite personal stories.

  1. Why do you think the person gave Anika only one hour?
  2. What does shame do to a person's choices? How can a friend or adult make it easier to tell?
  3. Whose fault is this situation? How do you know?
  4. Which step in the Drill do you think is hardest? What could make it easier?
  5. If a friend told you something like this was happening, what would you say first? (Draw out: "Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. I will go with you to tell someone.")

Takeaway (2 min)#

SAY: "Pause. Check. Tell. Here, Pause is STOP. Do not reply and do not pay. Tell is the most powerful step. STOP, SAVE, TELL, REPORT. It is never your fault, and telling is never too late."

Nia is a seventh grader. Someone used an app that alters photos to make a fake picture of Nia, using her face from a school photo. It is made to look private and humiliating. It lands in a class group chat. Nia did nothing; the picture is fake. Three classmates see it. Cam laughs and forwards it. Ari feels uncomfortable but freezes and says nothing. Idris feels sick and wants to help but does not know how.

Step 2 (1 min). Real or fake, the harm is the same.

SAY: "Notice that it does not matter whether the picture is real or fake. The hurt, the shame, and the whispering are the same. That is why people who make or share these pictures can face serious consequences at school, and in many places under the law. A federal law called the TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed in May 2025, treats sharing intimate images without consent, including AI-made fakes, as a crime, and it requires covered platforms (websites, apps and online services) to remove reported images within 48 hours [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: congress.gov Public Law 119-12; ftc.gov]. Many states have laws too (they vary; check yours with your counselor or administrator). We do not need legal details. The point is: this is not a prank."

Step 3 (4 min). The Upstander Four (groups of 4-5). Introduce the four moves:

  • STOP the spread. Do not open, forward, comment on, like, or screenshot the picture. If you receive it, do not pass it on.
  • SUPPORT the target. Reach out privately: "I am sorry this happened. It is not your fault. I am here."
  • TELL an adult. Use the Help Ladder. Say: "Something harmful is going around and I think someone needs help."
  • REPORT it. In the app or platform, and through an adult; adults can report to the school, law enforcement, or NCMEC as needed.

Each group takes a Bystander Card (Cam, Ari, or Idris; assign two groups to each if needed) and uses the Move Cards to write:

  • One line the character could send to Nia privately.
  • One line to the group chat (for example: "Not okay. I am not sharing this and I am reporting it.").
  • One adult to tell and the first sentence (a Tell-3 line).
  • What to report where.

Step 4 (2 min). If it is you, and if you made or shared one.

SAY: "If a fake picture of you ever appears, use the same steps: STOP, SAVE the words and links, not the picture, TELL, REPORT. NCMEC's free tool Take It Down can help remove images of someone under 18 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: takeitdown.ncmec.org]. If you have forwarded a fake picture, or if you were thinking of making or sharing one as a joke, it is not too late to stop. Stop sharing, tell a trusted adult, and take responsibility. A joke can become a crime, and it can hurt someone deeply."

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Why does a fake picture hurt even though it is not real?
  2. What is the difference between "Cam," "Ari," and "Idris" in the case file? Which move is easiest? Which is bravest?
  3. What could someone say to Nia that would help? What would not help? (Do not say "just ignore it" or "who cares.")
  4. Why is "it was just a joke" not an excuse?
  5. Why might it feel hard to report? How could a friend make it easier? (Offer to go with them.)

Takeaway (2 min)#

SAY: "Pause. Do not spread. Check: is this hurting someone? Tell an adult. The Upstander Four: STOP the spread, SUPPORT, TELL, REPORT. And it is never your fault if a fake picture of you exists. The person who made or shared it is responsible."

Exit ticket (about 1 min)#

"Write one sentence you could send privately to a classmate who has been targeted by a fake image." Optional line: "[ ] I would like to talk to the teacher or counselor privately."

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (Lesson 8: Fakes That Hurt)

A student may disclose being a target, knowing a target, having received or forwarded a fake picture, or having made one. Treat each differently. Do not wait until the end of the day.

If the student is a target:

  • SAY: "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble. I am going to help you. I cannot promise to keep this secret because I need to bring in people who can help, and I will tell you who."
  • DON'T SAY: "Why did you take that photo?" "It is just a fake, so it is not a big deal." "Just ignore it." "Show me."

If the student has received or forwarded a fake picture, or made one:

  • SAY: "Thank you for telling me. I am glad you are telling me now. Please do not send it to anyone else. I will help you do the right next steps."
  • DON'T SAY: Do not lecture, shame, or promise secrecy. Do not ask to see the image.

ESCALATE:

  • Treat a fake sexual image of a minor as suspected child sexual abuse material, even if it is AI-generated or fake [VERIFY with counsel for your state]. Do not view, copy, forward, or screenshot it. Do not ask any student to show it. Secure devices per policy and hand to administration or law enforcement.
  • Report personally and promptly to child protective services or law enforcement per school policy and state law, and to the NCMEC CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org, 1-800-843-5678 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: report.cybertip.org]).
  • Follow your school's harassment and Title IX or equivalent procedures [VERIFY].
  • Support the target: counselor, safe adult, protection from retaliation. Contact family per policy.
  • If a student who made or shared a fake needs support, involve the counselor; follow school discipline and legal reporting rules without shaming.
  • If there is any sign of self-harm, follow your district protocol and 988 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org].
  • Document the student's own words, date and time, and who you told. Telling the principal may not satisfy your own legal duty in your state [Confirm for your state at childwelfare.gov and with district counsel].

Header#

Field Detail
Grade band 6-8 (ages 11-13)
Strand E, Kind & Well
Big idea Group chats can turn from fun to harmful fast. You can save evidence without spreading it, support a target, and know when something is too big for the group and needs an adult now.
Time Hook 2 min, Activity 8 min, Discuss 3 min, Takeaway and exit ticket 2 min. Total 15 min.
Case file CF-9 "The Chat That Got Loud" (Workbook)
Reproducibles R9: twelve Escalation Meter cards, wall signs, three short chat excerpts (optional extension), upstander phrase cards

Standards (primary only; approximate; all [VERIFY])#

  • ISTE: 2b Digital Citizen and 7b Global Collaborator (use digital tools to engage with others respectfully).
  • CSTA: no direct match; closest is 2-IC-20 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017, rev.) text read; mapping approximate].
  • CASEL: Social Awareness and Relationship Skills.

Materials#

Unplugged default: Workbook CF-9 page; R9 Escalation Meter cards per group (chat excerpts optional) of 4-5; three signs on the wall (ANNOYING, HURTING, TELL NOW); upstander phrase cards.

Optional tech version: none.

Hook (2 min)#

SAY (noticing step): "Hackles up? When a chat starts to feel off or too heated, notice it. You do not have to be sure before you pause or tell someone."

SAY: "Group chats are where friendships live. They can also be where things go sideways. Think about how a joke can start small, then the group piles on, and soon someone is feeling left out or hurt. Today's case file is about a chat that got loud. When this happens through phones, games or chats, it is called cyberbullying. Our job: figure out when it crosses from annoying to harmful, what to save, and what to do."

SAY: "A reminder: every chat in today's files is made up. Please do not describe or name real chats or real people. And everyone in a chat is a person with feelings. It is never the target's fault when a group piles on."

Activity (8 min)#

Grouping: whole class, then groups of 4-5.

Step 1 (1 min). Read the case file.

CF-9: The Chat That Got Loud
Amir is in a class group chat. Someone posts a goofy photo of a classmate named Tess with a mean caption. A few people reply with laughing faces. Then someone says, "Let's all stop talking to Tess." Another person screenshots the chat and sends it to a different group. Tess leaves the chat. Amir saw all of it and did nothing. He feels uneasy and wonders what to do.

Step 2 (5 min). The Escalation Meter (groups of 4-5). Post three signs on the wall: ANNOYING (rude or irritating, but not harming), HURTING (repeated mean behavior, exclusion, humiliation), TELL NOW (threats, hate, sexual pictures or pressure, someone talking about self-harm, or spreading private information). Each group gets 12 Escalation Meter cards (short fictional messages and actions). The group places each card on the meter and writes the first move: ignore, support privately, say something kind in the chat, save evidence, report, tell an adult now.

Step 3 (1 min). Save, do not spread.

SAY: "If you need evidence, save the words and the usernames and the times, not the hurtful image itself. Do not forward a screenshot to other groups, because that spreads the harm. Evidence is for adults who can help, like a teacher or counselor. If a message includes a private or sexual picture of anyone under 18, do not save or forward it at all. Tell an adult immediately [VERIFY policy on screenshots]."

Step 4 (1 min). Upstander phrases. Read the phrase cards aloud and have students pick one they could use: "Not cool. Let's stop." "I am leaving this chat." "Hey, are you okay? (private message)" "I will go with you to tell someone." Remind students that a private message counts as upstanding.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. What was the first moment in the file when someone could have changed the direction? Who could have done it?
  2. What is the difference between "annoying" and "hurting"? Who decides?
  3. Why does staying silent feel safer but sometimes make things worse?
  4. How can a screenshot be used in a helpful way, and in a harmful way?
  5. What makes a message a "tell now" message? Who is the best adult to tell?

Takeaway (2 min)#

SAY: "Pause before you add to a pile-on. Check where the message sits on the meter. Tell an adult when it is hurting or worse. You do not have to fix it alone. It is never the target's fault, and a small kind move from you can change a chat."

Exit ticket (about 1 min)#

"Write one sentence you could say or send if you saw a group chat start to pile on someone." Optional: "[ ] I would like to talk to the teacher or counselor privately."

If a student discloses

If a student discloses (Lesson 9: Group Chat Dynamics)

Students may reveal that they are being targeted, that they have taken part in harm, or that something more serious is in a chat (threats, sexual pictures, self-harm statements).

SAY:

  • "Thank you for telling me. You are not in trouble for telling."
  • "It is not your fault, and it is not okay that this happened."
  • "I cannot promise to keep this secret if someone might be hurt, but I will tell you who I need to speak with."

  • 2-minute hook variation: First Spark (2 min): read Excerpt B (Remy, Pat, Tess; R9) aloud with no commentary and ask, "Find the first moment someone could have changed the direction." Lesson 10: Built to Keep You Scrolling#

Header#

Header, table 10
Field Detail
Grade band 6-8 (ages 11-13)
Strand E, Kind & Well
Big idea Apps are designed by people to keep your attention. When you can spot the design tricks, you can choose your own limits on purpose, without shame.
Time Hook 2 min, Activity 8 min, Discuss 3 min, Takeaway and exit ticket 2 min. Total 15 min.
Case file CF-10 "The Streak" (Workbook)
Reproducibles R10: three mock feed screens for the fictional app "ScrollSpot," Design Feature Cards, My Limits plan

Standards (primary only; approximate; all [VERIFY])#

  • ISTE: 2b Digital Citizen and 1a Empowered Learner (set goals and manage learning).
  • CSTA: 2-IC-20 (compare tradeoffs of computing technologies that affect people's everyday activities) [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: CSTA K-12 CS Standards (2017, rev.) text read; mapping approximate].
  • CASEL: Self-Management.

Materials#

Unplugged default: R10 mock feed screens (ScrollSpot is fictional) per pair, Design Feature Cards per group, Workbook CF-10 page and the My Limits plan, My Help Ladder card (final version).

Optional tech version: none. A family may later look at their device's built-in screen-time or notification settings together.

Hook (2 min)#

SAY (noticing step): "Hackles up? Notice how an app makes you feel when you want to stop and it will not let you. Trusting that feeling is a skill, and it is never your fault."

SAY: "Quick check, no talking. Have you ever opened an app for a minute and looked up half an hour later? That is not a personal failure. Many apps and games are built by teams of people whose job is to keep your attention. That is called persuasive design. Today you will be a design detective. We are not saying screens are bad. We are saying you should be the one choosing how you spend your time."

SAY: "Also: if scrolling is part of how you feel better when you are down, that is human. And if you ever feel hopeless or stuck, talk to a trusted adult. This lesson is about choices, not about shame."

Activity (8 min)#

Grouping: whole class, then pairs, then individual.

Step 1 (1 min). Read the case file.

CF-10: The Streak
Eli is twelve. He has a "streak" with a friend that counts how many days in a row they exchange a message. Eli's phone buzzes with reminders: "Do not lose your streak!" Eli feels pressure to open the app before bed. His feed never ends and the next video plays automatically. One night he realizes it is very late and he is tired, and he feels he cannot stop. He wonders if something is wrong with him.

SAY: "Notice Eli's last thought. Nothing is wrong with Eli. Features in the app were designed to make stopping hard."

Step 2 (4 min). Design Detective (pairs). Each pair gets the ScrollSpot mock screens (R10) and the Design Feature Cards. Pairs circle each feature on the screens and match it to a card:

  • Endless scroll (nothing marks the end).
  • Autoplay (the next video starts for you).
  • Streaks (loss avoidance, social pressure).
  • Notifications and badges (pulls you back).
  • Like counts and comparison (social comparison).
  • Variable rewards (you never know what comes next).

Two words to know (edit P-7): a push notification is an alert an app sends to tell you about something on your account, like a like, a comment or a message; social comparison is looking at other people's posts and measuring yourself against them, which can make some people feel worse about themselves [definitions from FLDOE's Student's Guide, pp.5-6: VERIFIED 2026-10-05, FLDOE Student's Guide to Internet Safety, May 2025 PDF].

For each, they write: "What it wants me to do" and "One way I could take back control."

Step 3 (2 min). My Limits plan (individual). Students complete the My Limits page: pick two changes (for example, turn off one kind of notification, use a bedtime for devices, set a time limit, move the app to the second page) and one replacement habit (a walk, a game, a call with a friend). Remind students they can choose different limits and change their minds.

Step 4 (1 min). Finalize My Help Ladder. Students take out their My Help Ladder card from Lesson 1 and update it with names and roles they would use, including the helpline numbers (988, text HOME to 741741 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org; crisistextline.org]). They keep it in a safe place, such as a notebook or backpack pocket.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Which design feature is hardest for you to resist? Why do you think it was designed that way?
  2. What is the difference between "choosing to use an app" and "being pulled by an app"?
  3. How can a friend help you keep a limit without making you feel judged?
  4. What could a company change to make its app easier to put down?
  5. How do you know when screen time is hurting your sleep, mood, or friendships? Who could you talk to?

Takeaway (2 min)#

SAY: "Pause when you notice you are scrolling on autopilot. Check which design feature is pulling you. Tell a trusted adult if screen time or an app is making you feel worse. It is never your fault if apps are hard to put down. They were built that way. You can still choose, and you do not have to do it alone."

Exit ticket (about 1 min)#

"Write one limit you chose and one person you could ask for help sticking to it." Optional: "[ ] I would like to talk to the teacher or counselor privately."

Activity (8 min)#

Grouping: whole class, then groups of 4-5, then triads.

Step 1 (1 min). Read the case file. Read CF-P1 aloud:

CF-P1: The Offer
Dani is thirteen. After posting a short video, Dani gets a message from an account that calls itself a "talent scout": "You could make real money. No experience needed. Don't tell anyone yet, in case someone steals your spot. I'll cover everything. We just need to chat on a different app." Dani feels flattered and a little uneasy. SAY: "Notice Dani's two feelings: flattered and uneasy. Both are normal. Our job is to listen to the uneasy one."

Step 2 (3 min). Offer Check (groups of 4-5). Give each group the six Offer Cards (RP1) and the Flag Strip. Each card is a short, made-up offer. Groups sort each card GREEN (looks fine; still check with an adult), YELLOW (check first) or RED (stop and tell) and circle the flags they see:

  • SECRET: "Don't tell anyone."
  • TOO GOOD: big rewards for very little effort.
  • MOVE: a push to another app, or to meet in person, alone.
  • OWE: gifts or favors that turn into a debt.
  • CUT OFF: someone pulls you away from family or friends.
  • TAKES: someone wants to hold your money, phone, documents or accounts.

SAY: "Notice that two of these cards are fine. Not every offer is a trick, and most jobs are real. The skill is asking three things: who is this, what do they want, and who else knows?"

Step 3 (2 min). Control Signs. Hand each group the Control Signs card and read it aloud. SAY: "These are things you might notice about a person who is being controlled. They are not things the person is doing wrong. Someone else holds their money, phone or ID. They are told not to talk to family or friends. They owe a debt that keeps growing. Teacher note: these reflect techniques the Legislature describes, such as isolating victims from the public and family members, confiscating identification documents, and controlling the victims' funds. Hook (2 min)#

Activity (8 min)#

Grouping: whole class, then groups of 4-5.

Step 1 (1 min). Read the case file and show the Check Strip.

CF-P3: The Closing Rumor
Tavi is twelve. A post says, "SCHOOL CLOSED TOMORROW because of a danger. Tell everyone!" It has a scary photo. Friends are forwarding it. Tavi's heart is pounding and Tavi's thumb hovers over "share."

Write the Check Strip on the board. Four questions always: WHO made it? PROOF: what shows it is true? WHO ELSE says so (a school, a trusted news source, an official)? FEELING: is it trying to scare, anger or rush me? Two more when they fit: LOOK CLOSER (a picture or video: when was it taken, who took it, has it been edited?) and DATA (a number or chart: who is it about, when, who collected it, one source or several?).

Step 2 (4 min). Claim Check (groups). Each group gets three of the six Claim Cards (RP3), so the class covers all six. Cards (all made up): (1) a school-closing rumor with a scary photo; (2) a "miracle cure" from an influencer; (3) a screenshot of a "private message" about a classmate; (4) a chatbot answer stated very confidently; (5) a chart saying "Our school's reading scores doubled!" whose bars start at 90, so a small gap looks huge; (6) a photo "from today" of a flooded street that is actually from years ago. For each card the group names the first Check Strip question to ask, what good evidence would look like, and a decision: SHARE, CHECK FIRST, DO NOT SHARE or TELL AN ADULT.

Step 3 (2 min). Debrief by card. One group per card gives its decision and reason. Use the Answer Key. For card 6, if you have time, show on the board how a reverse image search works in words only ("you upload the picture and the search shows where else it appeared and how old it is"), or demonstrate with a harmless sample picture. For card 5, redraw the chart on the board with the bars starting at zero.

Step 4 (1 min). Share Pause rule and when to tell. Students write their own one-line Share Pause rule: "If I cannot say who made it and who else says so, I do not pass it on." SAY: "If a post is spreading rumors about a person, scaring people, or asking for money or personal information, it is more than a wrong fact. Use the report button in the app, and tell an adult. If a rumor targets a classmate, treat it like the pile-on in Lesson 9. For AI-made fakes, see Lesson 8."

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. Why do false posts spread faster when they make us angry or scared?
  2. What is the difference between a mistake and an attempt to trick people? Does it matter if you share either one?
  3. Why might someone with good intentions share something false?
  4. Why do feeds show you more of what you already reacted to? What does that do to what you see?
  5. What can you do if a friend shares something false? (Draw out: a private message, not a pile-on.) Who could you check with, and who could you tell?

Takeaway (2 min)#

SAY: "Pause before you share. Check who made it, the proof and who else says so. Tell an adult if it is hurting someone or scaring people. It is never your fault if you shared something false by mistake. You fix it by telling people and not passing it on."

Exit ticket (about 1 min)#

"Write one question from the Check Strip you will ask before sharing and one person you could check with."

  • Withdrawal, sudden change in friend group, or avoiding the lesson.
  • Tearfulness, anxiety, or anger after the lesson.
  • An exit ticket with a check-in request or a message that sounds personal.

Appendix C. Disclosure Handling One-Pager#

Keep this at your desk. Remove before copying the guide for students.

1. Stay calm and listen#

  • Move to a private place within sight of other adults, per school rules.
  • Keep a neutral, caring expression. Listen. Let the student tell it their way.

2. Say#

  • "Thank you for telling me."
  • "It is not your fault."
  • "You are not in trouble."
  • "I am glad you told me. I want to help."
  • "I cannot promise to keep this secret, because I need to bring in people who can help. I will tell you who and why."

3. Do not say or do#

  • Do not promise secrecy.
  • Do not ask "why" questions that sound like blame.
  • Do not investigate or interrogate. Use open prompts: "tell me more," "what happened next."
  • Do not ask the student to show messages, pictures, or accounts. Do not view, copy, forward, or screenshot suspected child sexual abuse imagery.
  • Do not contact the person who harmed or threatened the student.
  • Do not delete content or tell the student to delete content; let the response team decide.
  • Do not promise outcomes.

4. Escalate (the same day; immediately if safety is at risk)#

  • Report personally and promptly to child protective services or law enforcement per school policy and your state's law. In many states the duty is personal and telling the principal may not satisfy it [Confirm for your state at childwelfare.gov and with district counsel].
  • For online exploitation or sextortion: also report to the NCMEC CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org, 1-800-843-5678 [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: report.cybertip.org]).
  • Notify the counselor and administrator per protocol.
  • If there is immediate danger: 911.
  • If there is self-harm risk: follow your district protocol; 988 call or text [VERIFIED 2026-10-05: 988lifeline.org]; do not leave the student alone.
  • Secure devices per school policy; hand them to administration or law enforcement.
  • Parents or caregivers: follow school policy and CPS or law enforcement advice. Do not notify a parent first if the parent may be involved or if it could endanger the child.

5. Document#

  • Record the student's own words, the date and time, who you told, and what you did. Do not add opinions.
  • Keep notes in a secure place per school policy.

6. Follow up#

  • Check in with the student kindly. Protect them from gossip or retaliation.
  • Refer to the counselor and support services.
  • Remember: a disclosure is a success of the program, and you are not alone. Seek support for yourself too.
