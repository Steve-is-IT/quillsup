Unit subtitle: "Case Files"
Trust your hackles. Pause · Check · Tell

Name: ___ Class: __ Start date: ___

Status: DRAFT for counselor, child-safety, and legal review. Items marked [VERIFY] must be checked before printing. Answer key: see separate Teacher Answer Key file.

Welcome, Analyst#

In this workbook you will read short case files about made-up people. You will find the pattern, decide the best next move, and practice telling someone you trust. You do not need a phone or an account. You do not have to share anything personal.

The Analyst Rules#

  1. We analyze characters, not each other. Every case file is fictional.
  2. We judge actions, never blame. If a character makes a mistake, we ask "what is the next good move?"
  3. No personal stories in the room. If you want to talk about something real, talk to your teacher, the counselor, or another adult you trust, privately.
  4. Pass is always okay. Put your green Pass card on your desk. You do not have to say why.
  5. No names, no gossip, no screenshots. We never share or pass around real messages or images.
  6. No jokes at a character's expense.

Hackles up? Then Pause · Check · Tell#

Your "hackles" are what rise on the back of your neck when something feels off: too good, pushy, secret, or scary. Trust that gut feeling. A false alarm is fine. If your hackles went up and something happened anyway, it is never your fault.

  • HACKLES UP? Notice it. Something feels off. That feeling is information, so take it seriously.
  • PAUSE: Stop before you reply, click, pay, forward, or share. Take one slow breath.
  • CHECK: Who is this, really? What do they want, and how fast? Is anyone asking me to keep it secret, send something private, or pay? How do I feel?
  • TELL: Tell a person you trust. Telling is not tattling and not failing.

The one sentence to remember#

It is never your fault. The person who pressures, tricks, threatens, or harms is the one responsible. You are not in trouble for telling. And it is never too late to tell.

The Help Ladder#

The Help Ladder
Rung Who Example
1 Yourself Pause. Stop replying. Take a breath.
2 A friend who can come with you "Will you come with me while I tell someone?"
3 A trusted adult Parent or caregiver, relative, teacher, counselor, coach, school nurse
4 A reporting channel The platform's report tool, school staff, a helpline, the NCMEC CyberTipline

The Next-Rung Rule: If the first person you tell does not help, you are not done. Tell the next person. Keep going until someone helps.

Lesson 1: Help Ladder: Real Practice#

CF-1: Jordan's Weird Message#

Jordan is twelve. While playing an online game, someone Jordan has never met sends a private message: "You're really good. Want to chat somewhere more private? Also, don't tell anyone, okay?" Jordan feels uneasy. Jordan thinks about telling a parent but worries: "They'll take my tablet away. Or they'll think I was careless." Jordan closes the chat and stares at the screen.

Worksheet 1A: Barrier Busters#

With your group, choose the two barriers that fit Jordan best. Write one true sentence that makes telling easier.

Worksheet 1A: Barrier Busters
Barrier (circle two) A true sentence that makes telling easier
I will lose my device.
I will look dumb or careless.
It is probably nothing.
I do not want to get someone in trouble.
Adults will freak out.
I already did something I am embarrassed about.

Worksheet 1B: Tell-3 Rehearsal Log#

The Tell-3 script: (1) Start: "I need your help with something online." (2) Say it in one sentence. (3) Ask: "Can you help me figure out what to do?"

Round My role (Teller, Listener, Observer) What made it easier to say or hear?
1
2
3 (Bumpy Response round)

My Next-Rung sentence: "That was not the help I needed. I am going to tell ____."

Lesson 2: Account Defense#

CF-2: Remy's Locked-Out Game#

Lesson P2 (Plus): Scroll Science#

Remember: you can use your Pass card at any time. We talk about characters and "some people." You never have to share your own screen time or feelings. It is never your fault, and you do not have to do it alone.

CF-P2: Pia's Night#

Pia is twelve. Most nights Pia scrolls in bed "for a few minutes." Last night it was two hours. Pia is tired in the morning and snapped at a friend. Looking at other kids' posts makes Pia feel like everyone else has a better life. A group chat went quiet after Pia posted something, and Pia keeps checking the phone, even in class, to see if anyone answered. Pia wants to stop checking and cannot seem to. Pia does not know what to do.

Pia is not lazy or weak. Many apps are built to be hard to put down.

Words to know#

  • Social comparison: looking at other people's posts and measuring yourself against them. Spending a lot of time on it can make some people feel worse about themselves.
  • Push notification: an alert an app sends to tell you about something on your account, like a like, a comment or a message. Notifications are one design feature that can make it harder to stop checking.
  • Addiction: a word some people use when something feels impossible to stop even though it is causing problems with sleep, school or friends. A doctor or counselor decides whether someone has a health problem. A quiz or a friend cannot.
  • Reward and the brain: your brain uses chemical messengers. One of them, dopamine, helps you notice and want things that feel rewarding, like a laugh, a win or a message from a friend. Apps are built so that likes, comments and alerts can feel rewarding at unpredictable times, which can make it easier to keep checking. Scientists are still learning how strong this is for different people, and you still get to choose.

Lesson P3 (Plus): Fact, Fiction or Fake?#

CF-P3: The Closing Rumor#

Tavi is twelve. A post says, "SCHOOL CLOSED TOMORROW because of a danger. Tell everyone!" It has a scary photo. Friends are forwarding it. Tavi's heart is pounding and Tavi's thumb hovers over "share."

The Check Strip#

  • WHO made it? Is the account real?
  • PROOF: what shows it is true? Fact or opinion? Was a source named?
  • WHO ELSE says so? A school, a trusted news source, an official? Where did the numbers come from, and when?
  • FEELING: is it trying to scare, anger or rush me?
  • When it fits: LOOK CLOSER at a picture or video (when, who, edited?) and check the DATA in a chart (who is it about, when, who collected it, one source or several?).
