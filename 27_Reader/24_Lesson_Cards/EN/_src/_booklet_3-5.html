Trust your hackles. Pause · Check · Tell.

Hackles · a free kit · quillsup.org. © 2026 Competence Collective. Lesson text CC BY-NC 4.0. Hackles name, logos and art: all rights reserved.

One page for every lesson. Read a card in two minutes, then teach it in fifteen: Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway and exit ticket 2.

Pacing planner#

Ten lessons fit a semester of weekly 15-minute sessions. Start with Lesson 1 and Lesson 10 in any case. Weeks 11 and 12 are optional review (replay the role-plays, play Scam Spotters again, give the post-survey, hold a showcase).

Sensitive lesson timeline#

  • 3 weeks before. Confirm which lessons are sensitive, check your policy on notice and opt-out, confirm counselor availability.
  • 2 weeks before. Send the family pre-notice with the lesson overview and a review option.
  • 1 week before. Offer a preview. Collect opt-out requests. Arrange alternates.
  • 2 days before. Send the counselor heads-up message. Confirm coverage.
  • Day of. Calm opening and closing scripts. Counselor on call. Offer the ask-the-counselor option.
  • 24 hours after. Debrief. Check in with anyone who was upset. Document any disclosure.
  • 1 week after. Send the follow-up note with the take-home card.

Notes for this band#

  • Only 5 lessons? Teach 1, 3, 6, 9, 10.
  • Lessons 1, 6, 7 and 10 are the most likely to prompt disclosures: tell your counselor which weeks you are teaching them.
  • A 30-minute slot can run the core plus the Lab Extension, or add a second role-play round.

Lesson 1: The Help Ladder#

If a student discloses

Counselor heads-up recommended. Invites disclosure. Tell your counselor which week.

Big idea: When something online feels wrong, telling someone is the strongest move. Climb the Ladder until someone helps.

Materials#

Ladder poster (R-0), Trusted Hand worksheet (p. 1), Ladder Scenario Cards (R-1.1), pencils.

Prep in 5 minutes#

  • Checkbox: Draw a ladder with three blank rungs on the board.
  • Checkbox: Copy the Trusted Hand worksheet; one card set per group.
  • Checkbox: Know your counselor and your reporting route.

Hook (2 min)#

Raise your hand if you have ever gotten stuck on a hard video-game level. Keep it up if you asked for help. "Almost everyone! Pros ask for help all the time. Kenji was playing a game when a message popped up: 'Hi! I'll give you 500 free coins if you click here.' He felt a tingle that said 'hmm.' He did not click. He paused, he checked, and he told Aunt Lin. She said, 'Thank you for telling me. You did exactly the right thing.' Telling is not tattling, and it is never getting in trouble. Today we learn the Help Ladder."

Activity (8 min): "Climb the Ladder"#

  1. (1 min) The rungs. Rung 1: tell a friend you trust. Rung 2: tell a trusted adult. Rung 3: report it (counselor, principal, helpline, report button). "If a rung does not work, keep climbing."
  2. (1 min) Trusted Hand. Five trusted adults, one per finger.
  3. (1 min) Sentence starters, said aloud: "I need help with something online." "Something happened and I'm not sure what to do." "I'm not in trouble. I just need to tell you something."
  4. (4 min) Triads. Teller reads a card, picks the rung, says the first sentence. Listener: "Thank you for telling me. It is not your fault. Let's figure it out together." Observer: one star, one wish. Rotate.
  5. (1 min) Two volunteers show it. Applaud the Teller, not the story.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. "Why do some kids feel nervous about telling?" (Accept all answers.)
  2. "What if the first grown-up says, 'I'm busy right now'? What is your next move?"
  3. "Does Tell mean you did something wrong?" (No. You are being smart.)

Takeaway (2 min)#

"When something online feels off, your hackles go up. Climb the Ladder even if it is a false alarm. Telling is never your fault. Pause, check, tell, and keep climbing until someone helps."

Hand signal: Hackles Up (hand brushed up the neck), Pause (palm), Check (magnifier fingers), Tell (hand on chest, then out).

Exit ticket#

"Write one grown-up you can tell and the first sentence you would say." A student who writes "nobody" gets a private follow-up with the counselor.

Fun Boost#

Ladder Climb (30 seconds). Mime climbing three rungs with big knee lifts, chanting "Friend! Grown-up! Report!" On the top rung shout "Keep climbing!"

Level-Up#

Ladder for a Friend. Write what you would say if a friend told YOU something scary, then teach the Ladder to a younger reading buddy.

Differentiate#

Give the Teller a sentence-starter strip; allow drawing on the Trusted Hand.

Family card#

Ask: "Who are your five trusted people?" Reply: "Thank you for telling me. You are not in trouble."

If a student tells you something#

Say:

  • "Thank you for telling me. You did the right thing."
  • "This is not your fault, and you are not in trouble."
  • "I can't keep this a secret, because I need to get you help. I will tell you who I tell."

Do not say:

  • "I promise I won't tell anyone."
  • "Are you sure?" or "Why didn't you tell me sooner?"
  • Anything beyond "Can you tell me more?"

First 10 Minutes: see the Disclosure Guide. Then: tell your counselor or designated person right away. Follow your school policy and your state's reporting law.

Lesson 2: Passphrase Lab#

Big idea: Length beats cleverness. A long, random passphrase is much harder to guess than a short, tricky password.

Materials#

Passphrase Word List (R-2.1, 36 words), two dice per group, Crack Timer Worksheet (p. 4). No real accounts or passwords.

Prep in 5 minutes#

  • Checkbox: One Word List and two dice per group.
  • Checkbox: Copy the Crack-Timer table (R-2.2) on the board; cover the last column.
  • Checkbox: Draw a robot: the Guess-Machine.

Hook (2 min)#

"Mateo from our squad has a secret clubhouse. He locked it with a number lock with just ONE number, zero through nine. Priya said, 'I bet I can open it in ten tries.' And she did! So Mateo made a lock with TWO numbers: up to a hundred tries. Then THREE numbers: up to a thousand tries. Every extra number makes the lock harder. A computer can try thousands of guesses a second, so the squad needs a lock that is long as well as clever. Today we run a Passphrase Lab. Lab coats on, hackers. Well, imaginary ones."

Activity (8 min): "The Crack-Timer Lab"#

  1. (1 min) Meet the Guess-Machine. It never tires and makes 1,000 guesses a second in our lab (a gentle model).
  2. (2 min) Reveal the Crack-Timer table row by row while the class says the multiplication aloud. Each new word multiplies the lock by 36. "Length wins."
  3. (3 min) Roll a practice passphrase. First die is the row, second die the column; roll three times. "Random is stronger than clever." Practice only, never for a real account.
  4. (2 min) Do the math. 3 words from 36 is 46,656 possibilities, about 47 seconds. Real lists have 7,776 words, so four words give over 3 quadrillion.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. "Why did we use dice instead of picking our favorite words?"
  2. "Who should know your real passphrase?" (Only you and your parent or guardian.)
  3. "What if a website or a person asks for your password?" (Pause. Check. Tell.)

Takeaway (2 min)#

"If someone asks for your password, your hackles should go up. A false alarm is fine. Long and random beats short and clever. Share your passphrase only with a parent or guardian."

Hand signal: Hackles Up (hand brushed up the neck), Pause (palm), Check (magnifier fingers), Tell (hand on chest, then out).

Exit ticket#

"Which is harder to guess: a 3-word dice passphrase or 'Dragon1'? Circle one and tell why in one sentence."

Fun Boost#

Lesson 3: Scam Spotters#

Big idea: Scams try to rush you or tempt you. SPOT the signs, then Pause, Check, Tell.

Materials#

Prep in 5 minutes#

  • Checkbox: Cut the cards: Half A is 1 to 18, Half B is 19 to 36.
  • Checkbox: Lay out the three mats for each group.
  • Checkbox: Hang the SPOT poster: Secrets asked, Pressure, Offer too good, Trick to move you.

Hook (2 min)#

"Detectives, quick quiz. Priya got a message in a game: 'WINNER! You get 10,000 free coins! Click now before it ends in 10 minutes!' What should she do? (Take 2 or 3 answers.) Activity (8 min): "The Great Scam Sort"#

  1. (1 min) Teach SPOT with motions. S: finger to lips. P: tap watch. O: hands wide. T: finger traces an arrow.
  2. (1 min) Model one card: "'Free coins, click here fast.' An Offer too good, Pressure and a Trick. Three SPOT signs. SCAM pile."

  4. (1 min) Reveal and tally. Celebrate PAUSE-AND-ASK as a smart choice.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. "Why do scams use hurry words?" (They want you to act before you think.)
  2. "How can you check if something is real?" (Ask a grown-up; open the official place yourself.)
  3. "Is it okay to feel tempted by a free offer?" (Yes. SPOT helps.)

Takeaway (2 min)#

"A message that feels too good, pushy or secret is a hackles-up moment. If you clicked anyway, it is never your fault. One SPOT sign means Pause. Check at the real place or with a grown-up. Tell, even if you already clicked. Say it twice, whisper then strong: 'No thanks. I'm checking with my grown-up.'"

Hand signal: The SPOT motions (lips, watch, hands wide, arrow), then Hackles Up and Pause, Check, Tell.

Exit ticket#

"Draw or write one SPOT sign you will watch for."

Lesson 4: Secret Code Lab#

Big idea: Encryption scrambles a message so only someone with the secret key can read it. A longer, more secret key is a stronger lock.

Materials#

Prep in 5 minutes#

  • Checkbox: Pre-cut and pin one wheel (or strip) per pair.

  • Checkbox: Know the answers: PAUSE CHECK TELL (key 3); TELL A TRUSTED GROWNUP (key 5).

Hook (2 min)#

"Mateo wants to pass a note to Amara across the room. But the note might get picked up on the way. So he writes it as a secret code: 'SDXVH FKHFN WHOO.' That looks like nonsense, right? But to Amara, who knows the secret key, it says something very important. (Pause.) When you visit a website with the little lock symbol, the message is scrambled so people on the way cannot read it. That scrambling is called encryption. Today you build a cipher wheel and become code makers and code breakers. This is a puzzle, and it is the very beginning of how real internet lockboxes work."

Activity (8 min): "Build and Use a Cipher Wheel"#

  1. (2 min) Get your wheel. The outer ring is the real message; the inner ring is the scramble.
  2. (1 min) Teach the key. For Key 3, turn the inner ring until D sits under outer A. Find each message letter on the OUTER ring and write the INNER letter beside it: PAUSE becomes SDXVH.
  3. (3 min) Decode. "SDXVH FKHFN WHOO" (key 3), then "YJQQ F YWZXYJI LWTBSZU" (key 5).
  4. (2 min) Make your own. Encode a safety message with a key of your choice. Give your partner the message but NOT the key.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. "What is a key, and why must it be secret?"
  2. "If a message is encrypted on its way, is the person you are talking to safe?" (No. It does not tell you who is on the other end.)
  3. "Where else do we use secrets or keys?" (House keys, passcodes, passphrases.)

Takeaway (2 min)#

"When someone asks for your key or secret, your hackles go up. A false alarm is fine. A code is only as strong as its key, and a key is only safe if it stays secret. Pause before you share. Check who is asking. Tell a grown-up."

Hand signal: Hackles Up (hand brushed up the neck), Pause (palm), Check (magnifier fingers), Tell (hand on chest, then out).

Exit ticket#

"Decode 'ULCLY ZOHYL FVBY WHZZDVYK' (key 7), then write what a key is in your own words." (NEVER SHARE YOUR PASSWORD.)

Fun Boost#

Key Handshake (30 seconds). Partners invent a secret three-move handshake. That is their key. Can neighbors copy it without being taught?

Big idea: Everyone makes mistakes online. What matters is what you do next. There is always a step-by-step way to fix it, and you are never in trouble for telling.

Materials#

Hook (2 min)#

"Mateo was playing a game when a pop-up said 'Your tablet has a VIRUS! Call this number NOW!' He panicked and tapped it. A page asked for his password.   2. (1 min) Model one card step by step on the board.
  3. (4 min) Triad rehearsal. Teller reads a card and says: "I made an oops and I need help." Grown-up: "Thank you for telling me. It's not your fault. Let's fix it together." Observer points to the OOPS step heard. Rotate; three cards.
  4. (2 min) If the grown-up looks upset: "I know you're upset. I told you because I want to fix it." If the first grown-up cannot help, tell the next one.

Discuss (3 min)#

  1. "Why does telling quickly make the fix easier?"
  2. "What if you are afraid the grown-up will take your device?" (Say the sentence starter. Your family has a card to help them respond kindly.)
  3. "What would you tell a friend who made an oops?"

Takeaway (2 min)#

"Even if you ignored your hackles this time, or noticed them late, it is never your fault. Notice now, then tell someone. That is how oops becomes okay. Mistakes happen, and fixes are possible."

Hand signal: The four OOPS motions. Everyone says: "I made an oops and I need help."

Exit ticket#

"Write the four OOPS steps in your own words."

Fun Boost#

OOPS Moves (30 seconds). Do the four OOPS motions as a speed round, three times, then one big calm breath.

Level-Up#

Teach the Unit. Pick your favorite lab and teach its one key move to a younger buddy in 60 seconds.

Differentiate#

Offer the picture version of the OOPS card, a buddy reader and 6 cards.

Family card#

Respond calmly when your child tells you about a mistake: "Thank you for telling me. Let's fix it together." Please avoid taking away devices as a response to telling.

If a student tells you something#

Say:

  • "Thank you for telling me. You did the right thing."
  • "This is not your fault, and you are not in trouble."
  • "I can't keep this a secret, because I need to get you help. I will tell you who I tell."

Do not say:

  • "I promise I won't tell anyone."
  • "Are you sure?" or "Why didn't you tell me sooner?"
  • Anything beyond "Can you tell me more?"

First 10 Minutes: see the Disclosure Guide. Then: tell your counselor or designated person right away. Follow your school policy and your state's reporting law.

If a student tells you something: quick reference#

First 10 Minutes, from the Disclosure and Incident Guide, Section 2. Your school's child-protection policy and your state's reporting law come first.

If a student tells you something: quick reference
Do Do not
Stay calm. Soften your voice. Keep your face neutral. Show shock, disgust, anger or panic.
Move to a private place that is still visible to other adults (door open, or a glass window). Be alone behind a closed door with a child.
Say: "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble." Say: "Why did you...?" "How could you...?" "You should have..."
Listen. Use open prompts: "Tell me more." "What happened next?" Ask leading questions or press for detail.
Be honest: "I can't keep this a secret, because I need to get you help. I will tell you who I tell." Promise secrecy. Promise outcomes you cannot control.
Write down the child's exact words afterward. Investigate, confront the other person, or contact the alleged offender.
Report personally to CPS or law enforcement per law and policy. Assume telling the principal satisfies your duty.
If a device holds images: do not look, copy, forward or screenshot. Secure it per policy. Ask the child to show you the images, or look yourself "to check."
If the child may harm themselves: stay with them, follow your school's crisis protocol, call the counselor or an administrator at once, and use 988. Leave a child at risk alone.

  • Who I report to, and how fast: ____
  • Counselor on call and phone: ____
