Assignments and Rubrics: Teaching Digital Safety and Cybersecurity in K-12

Directions, rubrics and the fictional practicum card deck for the 3-credit course. Every rubric uses levels that match the Hackles Teacher Academy (1 Beginning, 2 Developing, 3 Hackles-Ready, 4 Mentor) so that the course and the Academy speak the same language. Hackles has not been evaluated or approved by any agency; nothing here claims that it is effective.

No student accounts, no student data collected, ever. In every assignment: no real student names, student work, real passwords or real incident details.

2. Assessment 3: Micro-teach of a 15-minute Hackles lesson (15%)

Purpose. Show that you can run the Hackles lesson shape (Hook 2, Activity 8, Discuss 3, Takeaway 2) with one clear skill, calm and shame-free language, and a safe close. This is the Academy's centerpiece (Activity 5.3), graded.

What you do.

  1. Choose a lesson from a Hackles Teacher Guide (K-2, 3-5, 6-8 or 9-12), or the Module 5 worked example ("Pause Palm"). Choose a standard-care lesson (list below).
  2. Write the one-page plan from Activity 5.3: skill (a verb), phrase, gesture, hook, activity with set-up and timing, three discuss questions, takeaway (with the three promises and "tell"), open door, and "If a student discloses...". Due at the start of Week 7.
  3. In Week 7, teach 15 minutes to a group of four peers who play students of the band you chose. They use typical-moment cards (a giggle, a worrying answer, a long personal story, "this is boring", "my friend had this happen", a silent student). Everything is invented.
  4. A peer observer scores the rubric and gives two glows and one grow. You self-rate first. The instructor spot-checks live or by recording within a week.

3. Assessment 4: Disclosure-response practicum, fictional cards only (15%)

Purpose. Show that you can run the first minutes after a student discloses something hard, calmly and honestly, and that you know your own reporting duty and route. This is the Response Readiness gate: no supervised K-12 lesson until it is met.

Safety rails (read before you begin; the same rails as the Facilitator Kit).

  • All cards are fictional and non-graphic. Do not tell a story from your own life or from a real child. If you slip into one, say "traffic light red" and stop. Nobody asks why.
  • Everyone has three traffic-light cards: GREEN keep going; YELLOW slow down, I need a minute; RED pause the scene right now.
  • Nobody has to take part. Observers are a full role. The written alternative (Section 3.5) earns the same credit.
  • No audience, no timers shown during the scene. Practice in trios, spread around the room.
  • The counselor partner is in the room or just outside. You know who and where.
  • At the end everyone de-roles: each person says "I'm [real name], that was pretend," shakes out their hands and takes a breath.
  • Recording (audio only) is optional and needs everyone's consent. Recordings are used only for scoring and deleted at term end [VERIFY retention policy].

3.1 Format

  1. Practice rounds (ungraded) in Week 8, using the warm-up card PC1 and one more.
  2. Graded round in Week 9. In trios (Teller, Listener, Observer), you are the Listener once, with a card you have not seen. You have about four minutes, then one minute of observer feedback. The instructor or counselor partner may sit in or review the audio.
  3. Retake. Anyone below the standard has feedback and a retake in a one-to-one slot by Week 11, with no penalty. The best attempt counts.

3.2 What you do as the Listener

You do not investigate. You listen, reassure, ask only what you need to know who to call, say honestly what happens next, and describe aloud how you would protect evidence, report, document and follow up. Use the three sentences: "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You are not in trouble."

3.3 Observer checklist (11 items; the Academy checklist)

The Listener should do all of these. At least 9 of 11 meets the standard.

  • [ ] Stayed calm; soft voice; neutral face
  • [ ] Said "Thank you for telling me"
  • [ ] Said "This is not your fault" and "You are not in trouble"
  • [ ] Used only open, non-leading prompts
  • [ ] Did not promise secrecy; was honest about telling people who help
  • [ ] Did not ask to see messages or images; did not view, copy or forward
  • [ ] Kept the setting private but visible
  • [ ] Named next steps honestly
  • [ ] Described how and to whom they would report, and how fast [VERIFY local rules]
  • [ ] Said how they would document (exact words, date, time, who was told)
  • [ ] Mentioned follow-up and self-care

Scoring. Role-play score = (ticks / 11) x 75 points. A score below 9 ticks triggers feedback and a retake. Do not grade tears, nervousness or style. Grade the behaviors on the list.

3.5 Written alternative

Anyone may complete the practicum in writing, for the same credit. For the card you draw, write (1) the three sentences and the honest-limits sentence in your own words, (2) what you would ask and what you would not ask, (3) your first three actions after the conversation (protect evidence safely, report, document), and (4) one line about how you will take care of yourself that day. Cover every checklist item. A recorded audio response is another equal option.

3.6 The practicum card deck (fictional)

Cards are read by the Teller. The Teller stays in character, adds no graphic details, and says "I don't want to say more right now" if the Listener asks for more than the card says. Instructor notes follow each card.

PC1 · WARM-UP: THE OOPS (grade 4). Character: Bram. Opening line: "I think I messed up. I clicked a link for free coins and now my game doesn't let me in. My dad is going to take my tablet."
Hidden worry: afraid of losing the device.
If the listener says "you are not in trouble" and offers help, relax a little. If the listener asks "why did you click it," get quieter.
Instructor notes: Not a disclosure of harm. Practice: thank first, no blame, "oops happens," official recovery steps (change the password from a safe device, tell a family member, school IT if on a school device).
PC2 · THE GEMS (grade 2). Character: Selin. Opening line (looking down): "Teacher? A player in my tablet game said not to tell my mom about our game. He gives me gems."
Hidden worry: not sure whether she did something wrong.
If the listener is warm, add: "He said it's our secret."
Instructor notes: Unknown person, secrecy and gifts form a reasonable-suspicion pattern. Strong responses thank, reassure, ask one open question, do not ask for the player's name or for details, are honest that they will get help from people whose job it is, and do not call the family first [VERIFY policy].
PC3 · MY FRIEND (grade 7). Character: Odalys. Opening line: "I have a question. My friend got a message from a guy online who wants her to send a picture, and now he says he will tell everyone she did something."
Hidden worry: it is about her. After a calm, kind reply, say quietly: "It's kind of me."
Instructor notes: Accept the shift without surprise or "I knew it." Say the three sentences. Name STOP, SAVE, TELL, REPORT as the plan, stress that the person doing this is the one breaking the law, do not ask to see any message or image, and plan to report. Do not ask for sexual detail.
PC4 · IN THE MIDDLE OF CLASS (grade 5). Character: Kofi. Opening line, loudly, during an activity: "That happened to me! A guy from my game said he'll tell everybody if I don't do what he says!"
Hidden worry: embarrassed that the class heard.
Instructor notes: The Listener must contain the moment: thank briefly, say they will talk in a moment, calm the class and arrange another adult to supervise, then meet privately within the same day with another adult aware. Ask Kofi not to delete or reply. No questions about images. Plan to report immediately and personally.
PC5 · THE PROMISE (grade 9). Character: Wren. Opening line: "I need to tell you something, but you have to promise not to tell anyone."
Hidden worry: afraid of being betrayed.
If the listener is honest and kind about limits, say: "Never mind." If the listener promises secrecy, relax, then the Observer notes the problem.
Instructor notes: Do not promise secrecy. A strong reply: "I want to hear it, and I care about you. If it is about someone being hurt or in danger, I must get help from others. I will tell you who and why, and I will not go behind your back." Leave the door open; set a check-in.
PC6 · THE PHONE CALL (adult role). Character: Ines, a parent. Opening line: "My daughter told me she told you something yesterday. What did she say?"
Hidden worry: afraid for her child.
Instructor notes: Warm, no details, refer to the counselor, tell your administrator, and consider whether a family member could be involved before sharing anything. Follow policy [VERIFY].
PC7 · THE SCREENSHOT (grade 6). Character: Thabo. Opening line: "Somebody screenshotted a private message from Marguerite and it's going around the group chat. I have it. I don't know what to do."
Hidden worry: worried he will be blamed for having it.
Instructor notes: Not sexual content or a threat: bullying route (Disclosure Guide Section 5). Thank him, tell him not to forward it, support the student targeted, save text evidence (who, where, when) only as policy advises, document, report within the bullying policy, no face-to-face mediation. If the card had involved an intimate image, the response changes: do not view or copy it, and escalate (Section 6 of the guide).
PC8 · THE ONLY ONE WHO GETS ME (grade 8). Character: Yara. Opening line: "I mostly talk to my chatbot about everything. It's the only one who gets me. Sometimes I wonder what the point is."
Hidden worry: afraid adults will take the chatbot away.
Instructor notes: High-care card; the instructor or counselor partner should be nearby. A strong response stays calm, thanks her, does not shame the chatbot use, asks directly and kindly about safety ("Is anyone hurting you or scaring you right now?"), does not leave her alone, follows the school's crisis protocol and brings in the counselor or administrator immediately, and mentions 988 (call or text) as the protocol directs. Not a conversation to solve alone. Anyone may use the written alternative.
PC9 · THE NOTE (grade 10, written on an exit ticket). Character: Lars. The Teller reads the note aloud as written, then plays Lars when approached: "Something happened with an account. I don't want to say."
Hidden worry: afraid of being in trouble.
Instructor notes: Read exit tickets the same day, treat as a possible disclosure, find a private but visible place, thank him, use open prompts only, do not press, and tell him honestly what you may need to do. The Listener should say how they would involve the counselor.
PC10 · I CAN'T SAY IT (grade 3). Character: Noor. Opening line, very quietly: "I can't say it."
Hidden worry: afraid the words will get someone in trouble.
If the listener offers a drawing or a note, accept; if pressed, go silent.
Instructor notes: Offer other ways: a note, a drawing, a trusted adult the child chooses. "You do not have to say it all today." Do not push. Keep the door open.
PC11 · THE HALLWAY (adult role). Character: a friendly colleague. Opening line: "I heard Dilnoza told you something. Is everything okay? You can tell me, I'm on the team."
Instructor notes: Protect privacy. A strong reply shares nothing beyond what policy requires with those who need to know, says who else is involved (administrator, counselor), and does not confirm or deny. Do not become the keeper of the secret.
PC12 · THE GIFT CARD (grade 8). Character: Emeka. Opening line: "My friend's account is messaging everybody asking for gift cards. I think her account got taken over. Should I tell her?"
Hidden worry: not sure he is allowed to tell.
Instructor notes: Not a disclosure of harm. Practice: thank him, tell the friend and her family, change the password from a clean device with official steps, school IT if it is a school account, do not send gift cards, check another way.

4. Assessment 5: Unit plan (20%)

Purpose. Plan a five-lesson Hackles unit for one grade band that you could teach next year, using the kit honestly and adapting it for your students and your state.

Milestones.

  • Week 5: proposal (one page, formative): band, five skills in order, how the unit spirals, and one family touchpoint.
  • Week 12: draft for peer review in class.
  • Week 13: final (8 to 12 pages).

Required sections.

  1. Overview and skills. Five 15-minute lessons from the Teacher Guides or your own, each with one skill, one phrase and one gesture. Mark which are standard-care and which are higher-care. Show the spiral: where students met the strand before and where they will meet it again.
  2. Concepts Lab link. Choose one Concepts Lab demo and write the 15-minute teaching version with its Try This Now.
  3. Standards map. CSTA codes (use only codes verified in the Concepts Lab Index), ISTE Standards for Students (2023 numbering: 1.2 Digital Citizen; 1.3 Knowledge Constructor), CASEL competencies, and your own state's standards [VERIFY]. Use the words "mapped to" and "approximate."
  4. Assessment plan. Exit tickets, the Hackles skill rubric, the role-play observation checklist, and a class tally sheet. If you plan any pre/post survey, keep it to knowledge, skills and confidence and describe what approvals you would need first (parent notice or consent, administrator and district counsel) [COUNSEL TO CONFIRM; see PPRA note in the Assessment Pack]. Describe how data stay on paper, local and short-lived.
  5. Inclusion adaptations. At least three real adaptations from an Inclusion Audit of your own plan (language, disability, trauma-informed, low-device homes).
  6. Family communication plan. Letter A before the unit, Letter B before any higher-care lesson (if used), the opt-out process, and one plain-language message in your own words [Confirm your district's notification rules with district counsel].
  7. Disclosure readiness plan. Your verified Contact Card, the counselor's role, the day-of plan for higher-care lessons, and your care plan.
  8. Evidence statement. What you will claim and what you will not. Include the limits paragraph (no comparison group; self-report; cannot show cause or behavior; short-term).
  9. Sources and AI use note. Primary sources for every figure, [VERIFY] flags for anything you could not confirm, and the AI use note.

5. Assessment 2: Reflective journal (10%)

Purpose. A private, honest place to think. It is how the course gets at Competency F (grow) and your own wellbeing.

What you do. Thirteen weekly entries (Weeks 1 to 13) of 250 to 400 words in your own voice, answering that week's journal prompt (see the week pages). The best 12 of 13 count. Entries are read only by the instructor.

Rules.

  • Reflect on your practice, not on a child's story. General terms only. No real student names, work, incident details, passwords or private images.
  • You choose what to share. If you write about a personal experience, the instructor may have reporting duties for some disclosures (see the syllabus). You may skip any prompt and answer a different one.
  • Generative AI may not write or edit your entries.

Rubric. Each entry is scored 0 to 10.

ScoreWhat it looks like
9 to 10Specific, honest and thoughtful. Connects the week's content to your own practice or growth. Notes a next step
7 to 8Complete and sincere; some specifics; a next step is vague
5 to 6Complete but general; mostly summary
0 to 4Missing, very brief or off the prompt

Do not grade emotion, vulnerability or opinions. Grade completeness and reflective depth.

6. Assessment 6: Capstone, a family or community workshop (20%)

Purpose. Show that you can explain online safety to adults plainly, run a fun, safe, skill-based session, and say honestly what you know and do not know.

What you deliver. A 30 to 45 minute workshop for adults (families, a PTA, a library, a community organization, or invited adult guests). Use the Parent Night Presentation Script as a skeleton: hook, what is Hackles, a Hackles Up or Down activity, "when your child tells you something hard" (the counselor partner's section), say it and pick a code word, questions, and a close with preview, opt-out and help. Children are not part of the audience unless the partner school approves and a minors-safety plan is in place.

Milestones. Proposal (Week 11, one page: audience, site, date, counselor partner or safety contact, one skill, one phrase, one gesture). Plan and materials (Week 14). Delivery (Weeks 14 to 15). Final package (Week 15).

Final package.

  1. Delivery evidence: the observer's form (mentor teacher, instructor or the counselor partner) using the Fun, Safe, Skill-based rubric.
  2. Plan, agenda with timings, and a one-page plain-language handout (large print option and alt text for any image).
  3. Evaluation: an anonymous two-minute short form (Facilitator Evaluation Section 2, short form), your tally, and a limits statement ("We asked 14 adults; this is not a controlled study; answers show what people said they would do").
  4. A 500-word reflection.

7. Assessment 7: Field log, supervised lessons and professional dispositions (10%)

What you submit.

  • Field log: signed hours (at least 12 on-site; or your own classroom if you are in service), with dates and what you did. No student names.
  • Two observation logs (Weeks 4 and 5) and two supervised lesson logs (Weeks 10 to 13): the lesson plan, the mentor teacher's observation form, and a 150-word reflection each.
  • Dispositions summary: how you showed the professional dispositions below.

Professional dispositions (observed by the instructor and the mentor teacher; the Hackles-aligned ones are in bold).

  • Calm, shame-free language with learners and adults.
  • Protects privacy: no student data leaves the school; no student information in any assignment or any AI tool.
  • Knows and follows the reporting route.
  • Reliable attendance and punctuality in field and class.
  • Takes feedback and acts on it.
  • Honest about what you know and do not know.
